# Chapter 2

## When the Measure Becomes the Target

### The Observer Enters the System

In the last chapter, we treated measurement as a way of looking at reality.

We have something we care about. We find something we can measure that tells us about it. The measurement is incomplete, but useful. It gives us a map.

There is just one complication.

Sometimes the territory notices that we're holding a map.

This is one of the fundamental differences between measuring physical objects and measuring human systems. If I measure a board, the board does not care. It doesn't notice that it's forty-seven inches long and begin wondering whether management would prefer forty-eight.

People are different.

We notice what is being measured.

And once we notice, the measurement itself becomes part of the environment.

There is a loose analogy here to the observer effect in science. The basic idea is not mysterious: sometimes obtaining information about a system requires interacting with the system, and that interaction can change what we're trying to observe.

Human systems have their own version of this problem.

Suppose a company becomes concerned about how much time engineers spend in meetings.

Reasonable concern.

Engineers complain about meetings constantly. Managers complain about meetings while scheduling meetings to discuss how many meetings everyone is attending. Eventually someone decides we need data.

So we start tracking meeting hours.

Immediately, the measurement has a cost. Someone has to collect the data. Maybe calendars are analyzed automatically. Maybe employees categorize meetings. Maybe managers review the reports.

If the process is elaborate enough, we can eventually consume several hours of engineering work producing a number that tells us how many hours of engineering work we're consuming.

This is mildly ridiculous, but measurement cost is usually manageable. The more interesting effect begins when people learn that the number exists.

"Oh. They're watching meeting hours."

Now behavior changes.

Maybe engineers decline meetings they would otherwise have attended. Maybe managers shorten recurring meetings. Maybe teams move conversations into chat. Maybe people become more thoughtful about who actually needs to be in the room.

Excellent.

That may be exactly what we wanted.

The measurement has directed attention toward a problem, and the system is responding.

But notice what happened. We are no longer merely observing meeting behavior. The act of measurement has become one of the causes of meeting behavior.

This is not necessarily bad. In many cases it's the point.

If a hospital begins carefully tracking infections, we hope people become more attentive to infection prevention. If a factory starts tracking injuries, we hope safety receives more attention. If a software organization measures customer-reported defects, we may very much want teams to care about reducing them.

Measurement directs attention.

Attention changes behavior.

That means every time we introduce a metric into a human system, there are really two questions worth asking.

**What will this measurement tell us?**

And:

**What will measuring this cause?**

Those questions sound similar. They aren't.

Consider AI adoption.

A software organization wants its engineers to make effective use of AI tools. Management believes these tools may improve productivity, accelerate learning, reduce repetitive work, or help engineers solve problems more effectively.

All reasonable goals.

So the organization starts measuring AI usage.

Again, reasonable.

The first question is whether the metric tells us something useful. Perhaps we measure how often engineers use approved AI tools. That gives us information about adoption.

But then the second question appears.

What will measuring AI usage cause?

Before the metric exists, an engineer facing a problem might ask:

"Would AI help me solve this?"

After the organization starts publishing AI-usage numbers, another question can quietly enter the room:

"Where can I use AI?"

Those are not quite the same question.

The first begins with the work and considers the tool.

The second begins with the tool and looks for work.

Maybe that's still useful. Perhaps the organization is deliberately trying to overcome resistance and wants engineers to experiment. A temporary push toward usage might expose people to capabilities they would otherwise ignore.

But we should understand that we're no longer simply measuring adoption.

We're influencing it.

And once people know which number we're watching, that number begins acquiring a new kind of power.

### And Then We Attach Consequences

Attention alone can change behavior.

Consequences change it much more.

Imagine a customer-service organization trying to improve efficiency. Management notices that shorter calls tend to correlate with faster resolution and lower operating costs.

So the organization begins tracking average call duration.

This is not an absurd metric.

If one representative routinely takes forty minutes to resolve problems that everyone else handles in ten, that's worth knowing. If average call duration suddenly doubles across the entire organization, that's worth investigating.

The metric contains information.

Then we set a target.

Eight minutes.

Now the number is doing something different.

A customer calls with a straightforward problem. The representative solves it in six minutes. Wonderful.

Another customer calls with a complicated problem. Maybe they're confused. Maybe several things have gone wrong. Maybe they're elderly and need a little more help walking through the process.

The call reaches eight minutes.

The problem isn't solved.

What happens inside the representative's head?

If the eight-minute target is merely informational, perhaps nothing.

But what if average call duration appears on their performance dashboard?

What if their manager asks about it every week?

What if bonuses depend partly on it?

What if employees with long calls tend to receive lower performance ratings?

Now the representative is no longer choosing only how best to help the customer.

They are also managing risk.

Keep helping and the metric gets worse.

End the call and the customer experience gets worse.

This is where the perspective of the measurer and the measuree begins to diverge.

From the manager's perspective, call duration may genuinely be only one data point. The manager may care deeply about customer satisfaction, resolution quality, employee judgment, and all the context surrounding an unusual call.

"We're not saying every call has to be under eight minutes," the manager might explain. "We just want to understand the trend."

And they may mean it.

The employee, however, sees something else.

They see their name next to a red number.

They know their manager sees it. They know the manager's manager may see it. They know that performance decisions will eventually be made, and they may not know exactly how much weight this particular number will carry when that happens.

**For the measurer, perhaps it really is one data point. For the measuree, it may be the data point with their name next to it.**

That difference matters.

Organizations are full of messages whose meaning changes as they travel.

An executive sees a dashboard and asks a vice president why a metric has moved.

The vice president asks a director.

The director asks a manager.

The manager asks the employee closest to the work.

Nobody in this chain needs to be unreasonable.

The executive may genuinely be curious. The vice president may simply want context. The director may be trying to understand the trend. The manager may honestly believe the employee should use judgment.

But by the time the question arrives at the person whose performance is represented by the number, "We're curious about this trend" can feel remarkably similar to "Make the number go up."

Or down, depending on the dashboard.

This is one reason power matters in measurement.

The person designing or reviewing the metric usually has more freedom to think of it as information. The person being measured has to think about consequences.

That doesn't mean every employee is secretly terrified of every dashboard. It doesn't mean managers should stop measuring performance. Organizations need information, and performance really does matter.

It means that a metric can carry more weight at the bottom of an organization than the people at the top realize they put on it.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences expectation.

Then we attach a target, a bonus, a rating, a promotion, a public ranking, or simply the knowledge that someone important is watching.

Expectation becomes incentive.

And incentives have a way of making people creative.

### Goodhart's Law

Economist Charles Goodhart is associated with an observation that has become almost unavoidable in discussions about metrics. The popular version goes like this:

**When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**

The exact claim needs some nuance. A metric does not become useless the instant someone sets a goal for it. If a doctor tells me my blood pressure is high and I change my habits to lower it, the measurement has not somehow been corrupted by my success.

The problem appears when we can improve the proxy without improving the thing the proxy was meant to represent.

Return to our customer-service representative.

Average call duration began as evidence about efficiency. If calls became shorter because representatives received better training, software improved, or common problems became easier to solve, then the falling number would reflect real improvement.

But there are other ways to make calls shorter.

Rush customers.

Transfer difficult calls.

Discourage questions.

End calls before every problem is resolved.

Suddenly the same number can improve for very different reasons.

The organization wanted efficient customer service.

It asked for shorter calls.

Those are related.

They are not identical.

This suggests a useful question whenever we create a metric:

**How could someone make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing I care about?**

The question isn't cynical.

We're not assuming employees are dishonest or waiting for an opportunity to manipulate the system. We're trying to understand the distance between the proxy and the purpose.

If there is no way to improve the number without improving the underlying reality, wonderful.

Usually there is.

Software engineering provides endless opportunities to discover this.

As I mentioned earlier, I once worked in an environment where speed mattered a great deal. We needed engineers to move quickly, solve problems, and keep projects advancing.

Again, entirely reasonable.

One engineer was particularly good at producing visible work. Code appeared quickly. Bugs were fixed quickly. Things moved.

Unfortunately, some of the code produced quickly also created bugs.

Which created more work.

Which the engineer then fixed quickly.

From the perspective of the measurement system, this could look fantastic.

Code written.

Bug discovered.

Bug assigned.

Bug fixed.

Another bug discovered.

Another bug fixed.

The machine had achieved perpetual motion.

The organization was vibrating with productivity.

Meanwhile, another engineer might spend more time thinking through a change before committing it. They might review the architecture, test an assumption, or notice that the proposed solution would create a problem three months later.

Then they write the code.

It works.

Nothing happens.

And that's the problem.

Nothing happened.

There is no dramatic bug report. No heroic debugging session. No urgent patch. No string of visible artifacts showing how quickly the engineer responded.

**Prevention has terrible marketing.**

This does not mean the faster engineer was bad or careless. Software is difficult. Bugs happen to excellent engineers. Speed matters too, and excessive caution can become its own form of dysfunction.

Nor does it mean management deliberately created a system for rewarding bugs. Nobody sat in a meeting and said, "What we really need around here is more defective software."

The organization wanted urgency.

It wanted responsiveness.

It wanted visible progress.

The measurements were attempts to see those things.

But the proxy made one kind of contribution easier to observe than another.

Activity leaves evidence.

Prevention often leaves absence.

And absence is difficult to put on a dashboard.

The careful engineer's contribution may be a bug that was never filed, a customer escalation that never happened, or a redesign that never became necessary.

How many disasters did you prevent this quarter?

Please enter a whole number.

This is one of the recurring challenges of measurement. We tend to reward what leaves artifacts because artifacts are countable.

That doesn't make the artifacts meaningless.

It means we should be careful about confusing visibility with value.

### Rational Adaptation

When people respond to incentives, we sometimes accuse them of gaming the system.

Sometimes that's exactly what they're doing.

If someone falsifies data, hides failures, invents sales, or deliberately manipulates a measurement they know is supposed to represent something else, that's dishonesty.

But much of what gets called gaming is more ordinary than that.

People adapt.

Suppose I'm an engineer with five bugs assigned to me.

One is easy and low impact. I can probably close it today.

Another is difficult and potentially important. It may take several days just to understand what is happening, and I have no idea how long the eventual fix will take.

Now suppose my organization closely tracks average time to bug closure.

Which bug should I work on?

There may be good reasons to choose the difficult one. It could affect an important customer. It could expose a deeper architectural problem. Solving it might prevent future failures.

But the easy bug has one interesting property.

I know how it will look on the dashboard.

If bug-closure time is one of the ways my performance is evaluated, that matters.

This doesn't require me to be deceptive.

I may still care deeply about the product. I may still want to do excellent work. I may simply understand the environment I'm operating in.

We created the game and published the rules. We should not be shocked when people learn how to play it.

This is why employee behavior can sometimes be diagnostic.

When a team begins choosing easier bugs, avoiding risky projects, or breaking work into smaller units because smaller units score better, the immediate temptation may be to correct the employees.

Sometimes that's appropriate.

But another question is worth asking first:

What behavior have we made rational?

Consider an engineer with a free Friday afternoon.

She could ship a small feature that will appear immediately in the tracking system. She could refactor a fragile part of the codebase that has been creating subtle problems for months. Or she could spend the afternoon helping a newer engineer understand a difficult part of the system.

All three may create value.

Only one may produce a satisfying green box by Monday morning.

**The measurement system has changed the economics of the decision.**

The feature produces visible evidence quickly.

The refactoring may prevent future problems that nobody will know were prevented.

The mentoring may produce its value months later in someone else's work.

When people are busy—and people in organizations are almost always busy—these differences matter. Work competes for attention. If some kinds of work reliably produce recognized evidence while others depend on someone noticing their long-term effect, the visible work gains an advantage.

Over time, invisible work can begin disappearing.

Not because anyone decided it was unimportant.

Because the system made something else easier to justify.

This is particularly important when employees have unequal power.

A senior engineer with years of credibility may feel comfortable saying, "I know this doesn't help my metric, but it's the right thing to do."

A newer employee may not.

Someone who has recently received a poor rating may not.

Someone worried about layoffs may not.

Someone whose manager repeatedly asks about a particular number may reasonably conclude that improving that number is safer than exercising judgment that will require explanation later.

People do not optimize only for rewards.

They also optimize for safety.

That is why "Use your judgment" can coexist with a measurement system that quietly punishes judgment.

The manager may sincerely want both.

The employee has to decide which message carries more consequence.

This is not an argument against accountability. "Context" can become a wonderfully flexible explanation for poor performance if we allow it to explain everything. Sometimes the bug really did take too long. Sometimes the project really is behind because decisions were poor. Sometimes a person is underperforming.

Numbers help us see those things.

The point is not that every bad metric should be excused.

The point is that the behavior around a metric is part of the evidence too.

If capable people repeatedly make choices that look irrational from the standpoint of the larger goal but perfectly rational from the standpoint of the measurement system, the system may be telling us something about itself.

### The Metric Eats Its Own Signal

There is one final complication.

Once people begin optimizing a metric, the metric itself becomes harder to interpret.

Return to average call duration.

Suppose it falls from eleven minutes to eight.

Good news?

Maybe.

Perhaps the organization improved its training. Maybe representatives now have better tools. Maybe a software change removed several unnecessary steps.

Or perhaps employees learned that long calls hurt their numbers and began ending them faster.

The average really did fall.

The data is accurate.

What's less clear is what the improvement means.

This is the strange thing about optimized metrics: the more attention we direct toward them, the more carefully we may need to interpret them.

Before the target existed, average call duration may have been a relatively natural observation of how long calls tended to take.

After employees know that eight minutes is the goal, the number contains something new.

It contains the work.

And it contains the response to being measured.

**Once people begin optimizing a metric, you must interpret that metric in light of the optimization.**

This is easy to forget because the opposite instinct feels natural.

A metric becomes important, so we track it more closely.

We track it more closely, so people pay more attention to it.

People pay more attention to it, so they optimize it.

Then, because everyone is paying attention to the number, we give it even more authority.

But optimization may have made the signal noisier at exactly the moment we became more confident in it.

Suppose AI usage doubles.

That could mean engineers discovered valuable new workflows.

It could mean training improved.

It could mean previously hesitant employees became comfortable with the tools.

Or it could mean everyone learned that AI usage is being watched and started using AI in places where they previously wouldn't have bothered.

Probably it's some mixture.

The usage number still tells us something.

It just no longer tells us exactly what it told us before the organization made increasing it a goal.

This is why a healthy measurement system needs room for two sentences that can initially sound contradictory:

"The number looks bad, but..."

And:

"The number looks great, but..."

If every red number must indicate failure, people will learn to avoid red numbers.

If every green number must indicate success, people will learn to produce green numbers.

Reality is rarely that cooperative.

Sometimes a metric gets worse because a team chose to tackle a difficult problem.

Sometimes a metric improves because people discovered a shortcut that damages the thing we actually care about.

Sometimes the number is bad and the situation is bad.

Sometimes the number is good and the situation is good.

The point is not to distrust the number.

It's to remain curious about what produced it.

This brings us back to the two questions we started with.

**What will this measurement tell us?**

**What will measuring this cause?**

And once a target enters the picture, add a third:

**How could someone make this number look fantastic without improving the thing I care about?**

Those questions do not eliminate the problems of measurement.

They make the problems visible.

They also make conversations possible.

A manager can look at an engineer's unusually long bug-closure time and ask what happened rather than beginning with an accusation. The engineer can explain that the bug exposed an architectural problem without pretending that every delay is automatically justified.

The number provides a reason to look.

The conversation provides context.

Judgment still has work to do.

That's the important change from measuring a board.

The board doesn't care that we're measuring it. It doesn't know the target. It doesn't optimize its length. It doesn't worry about its performance review.

People do.

**When you introduce a metric into a human system, you are not merely installing a thermometer. You are changing the temperature.**

That doesn't mean we stop measuring.

It means we measure with our eyes open.

Because once we start measuring people, the dashboard is no longer merely a picture of the system.

It has become part of the system.

And that raises another problem.

The dashboard is useful because it compresses all that complexity into something a manager can see.

But what happens when the compressed view becomes more real to us than the work itself?
