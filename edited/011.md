# Chapter 11

## Useful Servants

### So What Do We Put on the Dashboard?

At this point, a reasonably impatient manager may have a question. This has all been very interesting. The maps and territories. Goodhart's Law. Incentives. Culture. Identity. Transcendence. The epistemological limitations of human representation. Wonderful. I still have to run the company on Monday.

Fair enough.

We've spent ten chapters examining everything that can go wrong when numbers acquire too much authority, but budgets still need to balance. Projects need schedules. Doctors need test results. Investors need financial statements. Churches need to know whether they can pay the electric bill. And tomorrow morning I will probably step on a scale.

We're not getting rid of the numbers. The goal isn't to fire them. It's to clarify the reporting structure.

Suppose a dashboard shows that a software bug took three weeks to close. That's information. Then comes the dangerous word: *therefore*. Therefore the engineer is struggling. Therefore the team is inefficient. Therefore our process is broken. Maybe one of those things is true, but the number didn't say any of them. The number said the bug took three weeks. It's the *therefore* that deserves our attention.

Imagine I'm the manager responsible for that team and I ask the engineer, "Why did this take you three weeks?" There may be perfectly good reasons to ask that question, but depending on the relationship, the history, and the power difference between us, the engineer may hear something slightly different: *Defend yourself.*

Now imagine I ask, "What made this one difficult?" Same number, different conversation. Maybe the engineer underestimated the problem and waited too long to ask for help. That's useful information, and perhaps there's something they need to change. Or maybe the bug crossed four subsystems, reproduced once every six hours, and uncovered an architectural problem nobody knew existed. That's useful information too.

Maybe there's an employee problem. Maybe there's a system problem. Maybe there's no problem. Some bugs are just hard.

The dashboard cannot know which one occurred, but it can tell us where something unusual happened. That's valuable. It changes the dashboard from a remote-control panel for the organization into a map of places worth visiting. Something is happening over here. We should probably go look.

This matters because the people living underneath a metric experience our curiosity differently from the people looking at it. A manager may intend a red cell as a question while an employee experiences it as an accusation. If every outlier turns into a trial, people will eventually learn to avoid outliers. They'll choose safer work, manage appearances, and become reluctant to take the complicated bug or uncertain project because the measurement system has taught them that complexity is personally dangerous.

If an outlier can lead instead to genuine curiosity, the same metric becomes useful in a very different way. Sometimes what we find is that someone needs to improve. Sometimes the conversation will be uncomfortable. Curiosity does not require pretending everything is fine; it simply means we allow reality to enter before reaching the verdict.

A good metric should deepen the conversation, not end it. Use numbers to know where to look, then use relationships to understand what you find.

### Start With the Point

Before deciding what belongs on the dashboard, there is a question worth asking: What's the point?

This sounds embarrassingly simple, which may be why it's so easy to skip. Suppose a company wants engineers to use more AI. That's a perfectly plausible goal right now. AI tools can help write code, understand unfamiliar systems, automate repetitive work, generate tests, summarize information, and accelerate all kinds of engineering tasks.

So management begins tracking AI usage, and usage rises 40 percent. Success?

Maybe. Why did we want people using AI? Presumably not because the universe becomes a better place as token consumption rises. Perhaps we wanted software developed faster. Maybe we wanted higher quality, less repetitive work, faster learning, or more engineering capacity.

Now suppose AI usage rises 40 percent and none of those things improves. AI usage improved. The point didn't.

Reverse it. Suppose usage falls, but teams become better at identifying the places where AI actually provides leverage. They use it less frequently but more effectively. Development gets faster, quality improves, and engineers spend less time on repetitive work. Did the AI initiative fail? Only if we forgot why we started it.

One useful exercise is almost embarrassingly small: *We are measuring ______ because we care about ______.* We are measuring bug-closure time because we care about resolving customer problems quickly. We are measuring weight because we care about health. We are measuring attendance because we care about whether people are participating in the community.

The second blank matters more than the first. The measurement may be good. It may be the best practical proxy available. But completing the sentence reminds us that the two blanks are not the same thing.

This brings us back to the hierarchy we've been building throughout the book:

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment**

Meaning tells us what matters. Measurement helps us see something about it. Judgment decides what to do with what we've learned.

The order matters because when measurement moves into the first position, the proxy begins defining the purpose. We stop asking whether AI is helping engineers and start asking how to increase AI usage. We stop asking whether customers are getting their problems solved and start asking how to reduce handling time. The easiest number quietly becomes the mission.

Judgment matters just as much because the measurement cannot interpret itself. Suppose the metric moved in the wrong direction. Is that noise? A warning? A tradeoff? A sign that someone's behavior needs to change? Evidence that the metric itself is misleading? The number cannot answer those questions for us.

That's why the dream of eliminating judgment through measurement is so appealing. Judgment is messy. It can be biased. It requires context, experience, humility, and sometimes uncomfortable conversations. A formula feels cleaner. But the purpose of measurement is not to eliminate judgment; it is to give judgment better information.

Even the purpose remains open to revision. Reality may teach us that what we originally thought mattered wasn't quite right, or that the problem changed while we were busy solving it. Meaning guides measurement, but meaning can learn too. The whole system remains in conversation with reality.

### Keep the Conversation Attached

Metrics are particularly useful at scale because they compress. Suppose fifty bugs were closed this month. What does that tell us? At minimum, it tells us that fifty bugs were closed.

We can immediately begin expanding the number's résumé. The team was productive. Quality improved. Engineers performed well. The new management strategy is working. That's an impressive career trajectory for a bug count.

Maybe some of those conclusions are true. The metric may provide evidence for them. But evidence is not equivalence. A metric tells us *something* about quality, productivity, health, performance, community, or whatever larger reality we're trying to understand. The word *something* is doing important work.

Once we remember that, we can ask another question: What behavior have we made rational?

Suppose we measure how quickly bugs are closed. A reasonable engineer might respond by working more efficiently or becoming more disciplined about moving bugs through the process. Excellent. They might also start preferring simple bugs over complicated ones because the simple bugs make the number look better. Less excellent.

The interesting question isn't whether someone can cheat the metric. That framing turns employees into adversaries before anything has happened. The more useful question is how a reasonable person will respond to the incentives we've created. People adapt to systems. That's not a character flaw; it's one of the most predictable things about us.

This is why conversation has to remain attached to measurement. The number can reveal a pattern across hundreds or thousands of events. The person closest to the work can explain what the pattern looks like on the ground. A manager may see that bug-closure time has increased across six teams, a pattern no individual engineer could see from their own work. An engineer may know that a new dependency has made a certain class of bugs dramatically harder to diagnose, something the dashboard has no field for.

Managers need the map. Employees live in the territory. The point isn't to decide which perspective wins. The point is to put them together.

This is especially important around outliers. A red cell on a dashboard should mean *look here*, not *guilty*. There will be times when we look and discover poor performance, and then we should address it. There will be other times when the outlier teaches us something about the system.

And occasionally we'll discover that the outlier is exactly what we wanted.

### Let the Metric Lose

Can it lose?

Suppose a customer-service organization has a target of eight minutes per call. Most of the time, that's useful. Customers don't want to spend forty-five minutes resolving something that should take five, and the organization doesn't want simple problems consuming enormous amounts of staff time.

Then an employee gets a complicated call. Thirty-seven minutes later, the problem is solved.

The metric looks bad. Did the employee fail? Not necessarily. Perhaps thirty-seven minutes was exactly what good service required.

Or take our engineer with the difficult bug. Three weeks may represent terrible performance if the engineer was stuck for eighteen days and never asked for help. It may also represent excellent engineering on an unusually difficult problem. The closure-time metric gives us information, but it cannot settle the question by itself.

A healthy measurement system needs room for the metric to lose. Otherwise it isn't really information anymore. It's the boss.

That doesn't mean every ugly number gets explained away with context. That would simply replace metric tyranny with excuse-making. If customer calls consistently take five times longer than expected, something deserves attention. If a project repeatedly misses commitments, eventually the pattern matters. The point is that an individual number is allowed to encounter reality before judgment is rendered.

Sometimes healthy systems produce ugly numbers because reality itself is irregular. Good judgment has to tolerate that.

The same principle applies to culture. Every measurement system teaches people something about what happens around here. If we say experimentation matters but every failed experiment leads to an unpleasant review, people learn something. If we say mentoring matters but promotions consistently reward only visible individual output, people learn something there too.

The metric has a curriculum whether we intended one or not. So every now and then, it's worth asking what we're teaching people through the things we measure, discuss, reward, and punish.

While we're asking uncomfortable questions, here's another: Do we still need this metric?

Organizations accumulate measurements the way garages accumulate extension cords. Someone creates one for a perfectly good reason. It goes onto a dashboard. Time passes. The original problem changes. The organization changes. The metric remains.

Eventually someone asks why we're still tracking it, and the answer is, "Because we've always tracked it."

Congratulations. We have discovered a metric with tenure.

Metrics should occasionally have to justify their continued employment. What decision does this number help us make? What behavior does it encourage? What would happen if we stopped measuring it? Does anyone remember why we started?

Sometimes the answer will be obvious and we'll keep it. Sometimes we'll realize the metric is still useful but needs to change. And sometimes we'll discover that an entire organization has been carefully maintaining a number nobody actually uses.

The purpose has authority over the proxy. The purpose stays; the proxy is negotiable. And as we've already seen, even the purpose remains open to what reality teaches us. Measurement is supposed to help us learn. We should probably allow ourselves to learn.

### Useful Servants

After all of this, I wish I could offer a perfect measurement system. Perhaps a framework. Frameworks are reassuring. They have boxes and arrows and, when necessary, overlapping circles. We could give it an acronym, roll it out across the organization, and spend the next three years measuring whether everyone is following the framework.

I think I'll pass.

The problem isn't that we lack a sufficiently clever system for measuring reality. The problem is that reality keeps being larger than the systems we create to represent it. That's not going to change, so perhaps the goal isn't perfect measurement. Perhaps it's a healthier relationship with measurement.

Numbers should be allowed to challenge us. If every metric that contradicts our preferred story gets dismissed as incomplete, we've learned nothing from this book. Measurement earned its place because human beings are capable of extraordinary self-deception. Sometimes the number sees something I don't want to see, and I need to listen.

But numbers should also be open to challenge. We should be able to ask what they actually know, what had to be left out, what behavior they encourage, what context is missing, whether they can lose, and whether we still need them. Those aren't hostile questions. They're how we keep a useful instrument within the boundaries of what it can actually tell us.

The same humility belongs on both sides of the metric. The measurer needs to remember that the person closest to the work may see something the dashboard cannot. The measuree needs to remember that the measurer may see patterns impossible to see from the ground. Managers need the map. Employees live in the territory. We need both.

Between those perspectives sits judgment. Not arbitrary judgment, and not whatever the most powerful person feels like doing. Judgment informed by data, context, experience, purpose, relationships, and the reality the metric was created to help us understand.

This is messier than letting the number decide. It is also the work.

The dream of the perfect metric is, in part, the dream of escaping that responsibility. If the formula could tell us what matters and what to do, we wouldn't have to wrestle with ambiguity. But no metric is going to save us from judgment. The best it can do is help us judge more wisely.

And that is enough to make metrics extraordinarily valuable. We don't need them to tell us what reality is. We need them to help us see parts of it that we might otherwise miss. We don't need dashboards to replace conversations; we need them to help us recognize where a conversation might be useful. We don't need metrics to decide what matters; we need them to give us information about the things that matter while remaining open to the possibility that reality will teach us something new.

That's a good job for a number. An important one.

The numbers can come with us.

We need them, and we would be foolish to leave them behind.

They just don't get to decide where we're going.
