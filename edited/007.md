# Chapter 7

## Measure Where You Have Agency

### You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I would like to lose a pound this week.

Excellent. Let's put it on the calendar.

Thursday, 2:00–2:30 p.m.: Lose one pound.

There is only one problem. When two o'clock arrives, I have no idea what activity I'm supposed to perform. I know how to go for a walk. I know how to exercise, prepare a meal, or choose what I'm going to eat. But I don't know how to perform the activity *lose one pound*.

Weight loss is an outcome. It's something I want to happen, and something my choices can influence enormously, but it isn't something I can directly do.

This distinction seems painfully obvious when stated that way, yet we confuse it all the time. We set goals around outcomes and then hold ourselves accountable as though the outcomes themselves were actions. We want to lose weight, get promoted, grow our investments, sell books, or build a successful business. These are all perfectly reasonable things to want. None of them are completely ours to command.

Investing makes the distinction particularly clear. Suppose I decide my portfolio should grow by 8 percent this year. That's a reasonable hope and may even be historically informed, but it's a terrible personal performance target. I can decide how much I save, choose an investment strategy, keep fees low, and resist panic-selling because someone on television used the word *crash* in a sufficiently dramatic tone. I cannot control the market.

If my portfolio rises 20 percent during a booming market, I haven't necessarily become Warren Buffett. If it falls during a broad market decline, I haven't necessarily failed.

**The outcome contains my choices. It also contains the world.**

That's what makes outcomes complicated. My effort and judgment are in there, but so are circumstances, timing, other people, biology, randomness, and things I couldn't possibly have known. The farther we move from an action toward an outcome, the more the rest of reality gets a vote.

My scale does this every morning. It gives me one number containing some information about my recent behavior along with water, food, digestion, exercise, timing, and whatever mysterious biological process causes the scale to wait until the morning after I've done everything right to increase by 1.7 pounds.

The scale doesn't separate those variables. It reports the outcome, and then I have to decide what the outcome means.

That's why outcomes can be poor daily performance reviews. Suppose I want to lose two pounds this week. Friday arrives and I've lost half a pound. Did I fail?

Maybe. Perhaps I ignored the plan, ate considerably more than I intended, and barely moved. The disappointing result may contain useful information about my behavior.

Or perhaps I followed the plan remarkably well. I ate thoughtfully, exercised, walked, and generally did exactly what I intended to do. My weight simply didn't move as quickly as I hoped.

Same outcome. Completely different performance.

The mistake is believing that because I can measure something precisely, I must also control it precisely. I don't. There is an important difference between influence and command.

I influence my health. I influence my career. I influence my finances and relationships. I can influence whether a book succeeds. In some of those areas, my influence is enormous.

But influence isn't command, and accountability works best when it attaches to something I can actually do.

This does not mean outcomes are unimportant. In fact, we need them precisely because our actions don't guarantee what happens next.

Suppose I follow a weight-loss plan faithfully for six months. I eat the way I intended, exercise regularly, prepare meals, and generally do the things I decided should lead toward the goal.

Six months later, I weigh exactly the same.

That matters.

It doesn't mean the previous six months were a moral failure or that I lack discipline. It means reality is giving me information. Maybe my assumptions were wrong. Maybe my measurements were inaccurate. Maybe something else is happening that I haven't considered.

Whatever the explanation, the process isn't producing the outcome I expected.

This is one reason measurement became so powerful in the first place: it allows our theories to fail. Without feedback, I can continue believing indefinitely that something *should* be working. Eventually reality gets to respond.

That's useful.

Process without outcomes can become its own form of self-deception. I followed the plan. Great. Did the plan work?

Those are different questions, and we need both.

A person can be extraordinarily disciplined in the wrong direction. There is probably no shortage of people who have followed ineffective plans with admirable consistency. Discipline is a multiplier; it does not automatically choose what gets multiplied.

So outcomes deserve a place in the system. They just need the right job.

**Actions help me evaluate my behavior. Outcomes help me evaluate my assumptions.**

If the scale jumps tomorrow morning after I followed my plan, I probably don't need to rewrite the plan. If it trends upward for six months while I'm following that same plan, I probably need to learn something.

Time matters. Patterns matter. A single result may be noise, while a persistent result deserves curiosity.

This gives us a way around two equally tempting mistakes. One is treating every outcome as a verdict: number down, success; number up, failure. We already know where that leads. The other is declaring that outcomes don't matter as long as we faithfully follow the process.

If the process never produces the thing it exists to produce, perhaps the process deserves a performance review of its own.

The relationship should be a loop. I act, reality responds, and I use what happens to learn and adjust. The outcome doesn't get to declare my worth, but it does get to challenge my strategy.

I still care about the destination.

I just can't steer by staring at it.

### Measure Where You Have Agency

If outcomes matter but make poor daily performance reviews, where should I look?

Closer.

Take weight loss. I cannot directly control what the scale says tomorrow morning, but I have considerably more influence over what I eat today. I can choose my meals. I can choose whether to exercise or go for a walk. I can choose whether to keep eating after I'm full because three fries remain on the plate and apparently leaving them there feels like an unresolved moral issue.

Those choices aren't perfectly under my control either. Human behavior is influenced by hunger, stress, sleep, environment, habits, and the unfortunate continued existence of pizza. Still, my choices are much closer to me than tomorrow morning's weight.

That's the principle:

**Measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency.**

If I want to hold myself accountable, I can move the measurement toward something I can do. Instead of asking whether I lost two pounds this week, I can ask whether I generally ate the way I intended, moved my body, and made choices consistent with the goal.

The scale remains. It simply has a different job. Over time, it tells me whether those behaviors appear to be producing the outcome I want.

This distinction travels well beyond weight.

I would like this book to sell a lot of copies. Ten thousand sounds nice. Let's do that.

Unfortunately, *sell ten thousand copies* is another activity I can't put on Tuesday's calendar. I can write, revise, publish, tell people the book exists, listen to readers, and try to make the next version better. I can do things that make sales more likely.

Then thousands of other human beings get a vote.

The sales number matters. It tells me something about whether the book is reaching people. But if I use sales as my primary measure of daily success, I've handed my sense of accomplishment to a large group of strangers who don't know they're participating.

Better to move closer.

Did I write? Did I make the book better? Did I do the work that was mine to do?

Then, periodically, I can look up and ask whether that work seems to be producing the outcome I hoped for.

This is one of the deeper reasons agency matters. When I measure myself primarily by outcomes, I can accidentally make myself responsible for the entire system. But I am almost never the entire system.

My body is a system. A market is a system. A workplace is a system. A relationship contains at least one other person, which immediately introduces a troublesome independent variable.

I participate in these systems. My choices matter. Sometimes my choices matter enormously. But there is a difference between having agency and having sovereignty.

**I have agency. I do not have sovereignty.**

This isn't a new insight. The Serenity Prayer asks for the wisdom to distinguish what can be changed from what must be accepted. The Stoics spent a great deal of time thinking about what belongs to us and what doesn't. Human beings have apparently been struggling with this problem for a while.

I don't think the distinction is perfectly binary. Even my own behavior isn't under the kind of absolute control the word *control* sometimes suggests. Fatigue changes me. Stress changes me. Habits and environment affect what I do. I can influence those things too, which means we could keep drawing smaller circles of agency until eventually someone creates a spreadsheet containing my Agency Percentage.

Please don't.

The point isn't to classify every variable. It's to ask a more practical question:

**What part of this is mine?**

Sometimes the answer is uncomfortable.

Quite a lot.

Maybe I didn't prepare. Maybe I avoided the difficult conversation. Maybe I kept making the same choice while telling myself the outcome was mysterious. Agency isn't supposed to make us feel better; it's supposed to show us where action is possible.

Other times the answer may be: not very much.

That's useful too. I may still have something to learn, but learning doesn't always produce a lever. Sometimes I can understand why something happened without acquiring the ability to make it unhappen.

There is a point where action ends and acceptance begins.

That's hard for people who like optimization. If a number is bad, we want a lever. If the lever doesn't work, we want another lever. The possibility that there may be nothing useful to do feels suspiciously like giving up.

But acceptance and passivity aren't the same thing. I can do what is mine to do and still recognize that the outcome isn't mine to command.

Before an important job interview, I have work to do. I can prepare, learn about the role, think carefully about my answers, and show up ready. After the interview, someone else makes the decision.

Beforehand, agency.

Afterward, reality.

Wisdom requires knowing when I've crossed that line.

### Process Metrics Can Become Tyrants Too

Once we realize outcomes are noisy, the obvious solution is to move our measurements closer to behavior.

This is a good idea.

Naturally, we can ruin it.

Instead of simply weighing myself, I can track calories. Since I'm tracking calories, I might as well track protein. And carbohydrates. And fiber. Then steps, workouts, sleep, water, heart rate, and whatever mysterious number my watch has recently decided is the key to human flourishing.

Wait.

I think I liked it better when I just had the scale.

There is a peculiar magic to a sufficiently comprehensive dashboard: it can find disappointment anywhere.

This is the danger of replacing one overpowered outcome metric with twenty-seven process metrics. We haven't escaped the measurement system. We've expanded it.

The principle of agency doesn't mean measuring everything near an action. It means choosing measurements that help us make better decisions.

That's a different standard.

**What decision will this number help me make?**

Suppose I track my steps because seeing a low number in the afternoon reminds me to go for a walk. Great. The measurement has a job.

I track my weight because I want to know whether the long-term trend is moving in the direction I intend. That has a job too.

But my watch can generate considerably more information than I know what to do with. Just because a sensor can measure something does not mean the number deserves management attention.

Sometimes the appropriate response to a number is, "Huh. Interesting."

Then continue making breakfast.

We understand this intuitively in other systems. A modern car can measure an astonishing number of things about itself, but most of that information is hidden from the driver because the driver is trying to get to the grocery store, not operate a mobile telemetry center.

The dashboard shows what is likely to matter for the decisions I need to make. If something goes wrong, more detailed information can become useful.

Our personal dashboards could benefit from similar restraint.

Information consumes attention, and attention is limited. Every metric I monitor creates another thing to interpret, another target to hit, and another opportunity to conclude that I'm somehow behind.

The goal isn't maximum information.

It's useful information at the point where a decision needs to be made.

This is where measuring closer to agency needs a companion principle: measure less.

Not because ignorance is virtuous, but because attention is part of the system too.

### Rigor and Intuition

There are two wonderfully convenient ways to avoid reality.

The first is to ignore the numbers.

The second is to hide behind them.

Suppose I decide that I don't need to track food because I know my body. I eat intuitively. I can tell when I've eaten enough.

Maybe that's true.

There is only the minor complication that this is the same body whose current condition I'm trying to change.

Perhaps intuition deserves a seat at the table. I'm just not sure it should chair the meeting.

This is where measurement can be incredibly helpful. Track the food for a while. Look at the portions. Discover that my intuitive tablespoon of peanut butter has roughly the dimensions of a tennis ball.

The number can correct the story.

Without that correction, intuition can become a sophisticated word for *whatever I felt like doing*. I can convince myself that I'm eating less, exercising more, spending carefully, or working effectively while reality quietly accumulates evidence to the contrary.

Rigor asks me to look.

What actually happened?

That's valuable.

But the opposite mistake is just as easy. Once I have numbers, I can start treating them as more trustworthy than every other form of information.

The spreadsheet says 1,800 calories, so 1,800 calories must be correct even if I'm miserable and constantly hungry. The training plan says workout today, so I work out despite feeling exhausted. My sleep tracker says I slept poorly, so apparently the fact that I woke up refreshed is an unreliable anecdote.

The measurement knows something.

So do I.

A human being is not merely an unreliable sensor that needs to be replaced by a better device. Hunger is information. Fatigue is information. Mood, pain, enjoyment, and the feeling that a plan is becoming impossible to sustain are information too.

Some of that information is noisy. Some of it is biased. That's why rigor matters.

The measured data is incomplete too. That's why intuition matters.

**Rigor without intuition becomes tyranny. Intuition without rigor can become self-deception.**

The relationship I want is a conversation.

Rigor asks, *What actually happened?*

Intuition asks, *What did it mean?*

If my weight isn't moving, the scale gets to say so. I don't get to dismiss six months of evidence because I feel like the plan ought to be working. But the scale also doesn't get to tell me, by itself, why the plan failed or what I should do next.

If I'm exhausted, that matters. If the calorie target is making me miserable, that matters. If a plan looks perfect in a spreadsheet but cannot survive contact with my actual life, that matters.

The number can challenge my experience.

My experience can provide context for the number.

Judgment has to listen to both.

This is not as clean as letting the dashboard decide. Judgment rarely is. It requires attention and the possibility of being wrong. Sometimes I'll use context to excuse behavior I should change. Other times I'll let a number bully me into changing something that was working fine.

There is no metric that removes that responsibility.

That's the recurring disappointment of this entire book.

We keep creating numbers partly because we want reality to become easier to judge. Often it does. But eventually the number reaches the edge of what it knows, and judgment has to take over.

### Did I Do the Things That Matter?

So where does this leave me tomorrow morning?

I can step on the scale.

I can look at the number.

Then, instead of asking it to tell me whether yesterday was a success, I can ask a different question:

**Did I do the things that matter?**

Did I generally eat the way I intended? Did I move? Did I make choices that were consistent with the life I'm trying to build?

Those questions put accountability closer to my contribution.

But they aren't enough by themselves.

Every so often, I also need to ask:

**Are the things I'm doing actually working?**

That question turns me back toward the outcome.

Together, the two questions create a healthier feedback loop. My actions tell me something about my behavior. Outcomes tell me something about whether my assumptions are holding up. Neither has to do the other's job.

This also changes the emotional relationship with a disappointing result.

If the number moves the wrong way, I don't have to choose between condemning myself and ignoring it. I can become curious.

What happened here?

What part was mine?

Is this noise or a pattern?

Does something need to change?

Curiosity gives me somewhere to stand between praise and blame. It keeps me accountable without requiring every result to become a verdict.

And sometimes curiosity leads to the deeply unsatisfying conclusion that I should do nothing.

Bodies take time. Investments take time. Careers take time. Relationships take time. Writing a book takes considerably more time than the person writing it usually thinks it should.

If I demand constant outcome feedback, I will spend a great deal of time reacting to noise. A favorable number will tempt me to repeat whatever I happened to do yesterday. An unfavorable number will tempt me to change a plan that may be working perfectly well.

Sometimes the wise response to new data is simply to keep going long enough for a pattern to emerge.

That can feel passive, particularly in a culture built around optimization. We like action. We like adjustments. We like the satisfying feeling of finding a lever and pulling it.

Patience doesn't look very impressive on a dashboard.

But the purpose of measurement is not to maximize the amount of intervention in my life. It's to help me understand what is happening well enough to act wisely.

Sometimes wisdom means changing course.

Sometimes it means admitting that the plan isn't working.

Sometimes it means accepting an outcome I cannot control.

And sometimes it means leaving the plan alone.

This is why I don't want a perfect personal measurement system. I don't want to spend my life discovering increasingly sophisticated ways to evaluate whether I'm living correctly.

I want health, not excellent health metrics.

The distinction sounds obvious, but it's easy to lose. The scale is visible every morning. Health is larger and slower. Steps are easy to count. A life in which I can move freely, feel good, and participate in the things I care about is harder to fit on a screen.

The numbers can help me get there. That's why I keep them.

But the point of measuring my life is not to turn my life into a measurement system.

It's to help me live it.
