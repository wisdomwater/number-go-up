# Chapter 9

## Nickels and Noses

### We Were Just Playing Music

I play music at church.

Mostly drums and piano. There are few things I enjoy more than sitting behind a drum kit with a group of musicians and feeling a song come together. Everyone is listening to everyone else, leaving space, adding something where it belongs. On a good morning, you stop thinking quite so much about what you're playing and become part of something happening in the room.

For a long time, the task felt fairly simple.

What songs should we play?

What speaks to us?

What might help people pray, worship, reflect, or encounter something meaningful?

Then a megachurch moved down the street.

I don't say that ominously. The megachurch wasn't the Death Star. It was simply a very large church with more people, more programs, more resources, and a level of production that a smaller congregation could not easily match.

They had excellent musicians. Excellent facilities. Programs for every conceivable age and stage of life. They had the kind of children's ministry that makes you wonder whether the children will eventually refuse to go home.

And people went there.

A lot of people.

Again, there is nothing wrong with that. If a church is serving people well and people want to participate, growth can be a wonderful thing. A full room may represent hundreds or thousands of people finding community, receiving help, exploring faith, or discovering something that genuinely changes their lives.

But the presence of a large church nearby changed the comparison set.

Suddenly, without anyone necessarily deciding it, a new question entered the room.

What will draw people?

That isn't an unreasonable question either. Churches generally want people to come. A worship team playing to an empty sanctuary has a fairly limited ministry, however transcendent the chord progression.

But notice what happened.

*What will help people worship?* is difficult to answer.

*What will draw people?* gives us data.

People came or they didn't. Attendance rose or fell. Visitors returned or disappeared. One service attracted more people than another. One style of music seemed to connect with a particular demographic.

Now we have something we can work with.

This is the same movement we've watched throughout the book. Something important is difficult to see directly, so we find a proxy. The proxy gives us visibility. Visibility gives us feedback. Feedback allows us to adjust.

None of that is foolish.

The problem comes when the visible thing gradually acquires authority over the invisible thing it was meant to serve.

A church can count how many people came on Sunday. It cannot stand at the door afterward and report that 14 percent experienced forgiveness, 8 percent became more compassionate, and three people achieved statistically significant increases in their capacity to love their enemies.

Attendance arrives in a spreadsheet.

Transformation arrives as a story, if it arrives at all.

That creates an imbalance. The measurable part of the mission is easier to discuss, easier to compare, and easier to manage. Eventually the measurable question can begin crowding out the harder one.

Did people come?

Number go up?

Good.

Maybe.

But if we believe anything spiritually meaningful is happening, the number cannot tell us very much about it.

This is where the problem of measurement begins to touch something deeper than management technique.

The people in the room are real. The chairs are real. The instruments are real. Sound moves through the air as physical waves. Money paid for the building and the electricity and the microphones and whatever quantity of coffee was required to get everyone through Sunday morning.

We live in a physical world.

If the room is empty, that means something.

But if we also believe there is a reality beneath the immediately visible one—God, spirit, grace, transformation, communion, whatever language we use—then the physical world participates in something it cannot completely explain.

Three hundred people in a room is real information.

It's just not the whole reality of what happened there.

### Nickels and Noses

There is an old expression for the two numbers churches are particularly tempted to watch:

Nickels and noses.

How much money came in?

How many people showed up?

The phrase is usually dismissive, as though any church that cares about attendance or giving has obviously abandoned spirituality for corporate management.

I don't think that's fair.

Churches need money.

Buildings cost money. Staff members need salaries. Programs require resources. Hungry people need actual food. Musicians need instruments, children's rooms need supplies, and Sunday morning coffee must apparently be provided in quantities sufficient to sustain the kingdom of God.

A church cannot pay its electric bill with transcendence.

So count the nickels.

Churches also need people. If nobody is coming, that is relevant information for an organization whose mission involves people. A church that has steadily declined from five hundred people to twelve should probably resist explaining the trend entirely as a purification of the remnant.

Count the noses too.

The physical world gets a vote.

But then ask a question we've been asking throughout this book:

What exactly did we count?

Attendance tells us how many people were present. It doesn't tell us what happened inside them.

Giving tells us how much money was given. It doesn't necessarily tell us how generous anyone became.

A person with enormous wealth might write a large check and barely notice the money is gone. Someone else might give twenty dollars after wrestling with the decision all week. The larger gift may be more useful to the church. That's real. It can pay for more food, more staff time, more ministry.

But if we're trying to understand generosity, the dollar amount alone isn't enough.

The physical expression can reveal something about a deeper reality without containing it.

That's an important distinction.

The mistake would be to swing too far in either direction. We can become so enchanted with the invisible that we dismiss everything concrete. Attendance doesn't matter. Money doesn't matter. Outcomes don't matter. Only the heart matters.

That can become a wonderfully spiritual way of avoiding reality.

If a church claims to love the poor but never actually feeds anyone, eventually the physical world is entitled to ask some questions. If a community claims to be radically welcoming while newcomers consistently feel ignored, the stated value doesn't get to overrule the lived experience.

The transcendent has to touch the immanent somewhere.

Love becomes a meal.

Compassion becomes time.

Generosity becomes money or attention or effort.

Forgiveness changes how one human being relates to another.

The invisible leaves fingerprints.

But fingerprints aren't the person.

That's the tension.

The measurable world can give us signs of realities that are larger than measurement. Problems begin when we mistake the sign for the whole thing.

Suppose a church grows by 20 percent over the course of a year.

Wonderful.

Now suppose that during the same year the community becomes angrier, more fearful, more tribal, and less capable of loving anyone outside its boundaries.

Number go up.

Success?

Or suppose attendance falls by 20 percent while the remaining community becomes more generous, more compassionate, more connected, and more willing to serve people around them.

Number go down.

Failure?

I don't know.

And I think *I don't know* is an important answer.

Maybe the growing church is flourishing. Maybe the shrinking church is dying. Maybe one dimension is improving while another is deteriorating. Maybe the numbers are showing us something important that our preferred spiritual interpretation would rather ignore.

We need more reality.

That's what Meaning → Measurement → Judgment was always supposed to protect.

Meaning asks what we're actually trying to cultivate.

Measurement gives us information about some part of it.

Judgment tries to understand what the information means in context.

Churches make this hierarchy unusually visible because the stated purpose is so difficult to compress. If the purpose is transformation, love, communion with God, wisdom, reconciliation, or becoming a certain kind of person, then our easiest numbers are several steps removed from the point.

And yet those numbers arrive at the meeting with charts.

Attendance has a trend line.

Giving has a percentage.

Program participation can be compared year over year.

Compassion has a conversation.

Forgiveness has a story.

Love has a human being saying, "Something in me is different."

**The invisible part has no dashboard to defend itself.**

That gives the visible part cultural gravity. Not because anyone has rejected the invisible, but because the measurable is easier to manage.

Eventually a church can become very good at growing the things it knows how to count.

Which raises the uncomfortable question:

What exactly are we trying to grow?

### How Do You Measure Forgiveness?

Imagine that we decide forgiveness is important enough that we should start tracking it.

We send out the quarterly spiritual health survey.

*On a scale of one to ten, how completely have you forgiven the person who hurt you?*

Seven.

Excellent. Up from six last quarter. At the current rate of improvement, full forgiveness should arrive sometime in Q3.

Something about this is obviously ridiculous.

But why?

Forgiveness isn't imaginary. It can be one of the most consequential things that happens inside a human life. Someone carries an injury for years. Anger becomes part of the way they understand the world. Then, slowly or suddenly, something changes.

The memory remains, but it loses some of its power.

The person stops rehearsing the injury.

Maybe a relationship changes. Maybe it doesn't. Forgiveness and reconciliation aren't always the same thing, and sometimes wisdom requires distance.

But something real has happened.

We may even be able to see evidence of it. A person can hear a name without becoming angry. They can tell the story differently. They stop imagining the argument they wish they'd won ten years ago.

Those are observable changes.

So the problem isn't that forgiveness has no relationship to the measurable world.

The problem is what happens when we turn it into 7.3.

Quantification requires us to choose a dimension and create boundaries around it. To count something, we have to decide what counts. To rank something, we need a scale. To compare something, we have to decide which qualities will survive the comparison.

Again, that's not a defect in measurement.

It's how measurement becomes possible.

But some realities are altered by the way we frame them.

Consider love.

I love my wife.

I could give you evidence. Time. Loyalty. Choices I've made. Things we've walked through together. Ordinary acts of care that would be boring to anyone outside a marriage and may be some of the most important things inside one.

The evidence matters.

If I claimed to love her but consistently treated her with contempt, the word *love* should not be allowed to float safely above my behavior. The physical expression matters because love that never enters the world is difficult to distinguish from an idea about love.

But now suppose I decide to measure it.

Current Love for Wife: 8.7.

Is that good?

What was last year's score?

How does it compare with other husbands in my age group?

Am I on track for 9.1 by December?

Something has gone wrong.

The problem isn't merely that the score is inaccurate. The act of scoring has changed the way I'm looking at the relationship.

Something particular has become abstract.

Something relational has become comparable.

Something lived has become ranked.

The measurement hasn't merely failed to capture love. It has diminished my way of seeing it.

This may be one of the limits we encounter when we approach transcendence with the tools of quantification.

Measurement works by drawing boundaries. It says: this is the thing we're measuring, this is the dimension we're measuring it along, and this is how one amount differs from another.

Transcendence points in the opposite direction.

If there is an ultimate reality that exceeds our ordinary categories—if God, Being, Spirit, or whatever language we use is more than an object sitting alongside other objects—then our categories cannot contain it.

We can still speak.

We can name.

We can tell stories and use metaphors. We can describe experiences. We can recognize effects in the world.

But our descriptions remain smaller than the reality toward which they point.

This is the map-and-territory problem at its deepest level.

Earlier, a dashboard couldn't contain a company.

Then a scale couldn't contain a life.

Now we're asking what happens when the territory is reality itself.

The answer is not silence. At least I don't think it is. Human beings have always reached for language, image, music, ritual, story, theology, poetry, and prayer to speak about what exceeds us.

The mistake is confusing the reaching with the thing reached for.

A category can point.

A number can reveal.

A story can disclose.

None of them have to contain the whole.

Perhaps some of the most meaningful things in life require precisely this kind of humility: enough confidence to recognize them and enough restraint not to imagine we've captured them.

### Productivity: Zero

One of my spiritual practices is contemplative prayer.

Sometimes that means sitting quietly for twenty minutes.

That's about it.

I don't bring a list of requests. I'm not trying to solve a problem or manufacture an insight. I'm not studying anything. I'm not attempting to become more efficient at prayer.

I sit.

From the perspective of modern productivity culture, this is a deeply concerning use of twenty minutes.

I could answer email.

I could exercise.

I could write.

I could clean something.

I could listen to a podcast at double speed so I can become wise in half the time.

Instead, I sit in silence.

Productivity: zero.

That may be part of why the practice matters to me.

For twenty minutes, I'm not trying to make anything go up.

I'm not improving a score. I'm not accumulating output. I'm not trying to turn myself into a better-performing version of myself.

I'm practicing presence.

I do think contemplative practices can change people. Perhaps I become more patient. Perhaps I become less reactive. Perhaps the silence creates space between impulse and action. Those effects matter, and if they show up in the way I treat people, all the better.

But I want to be careful.

If I justify twenty minutes of contemplation entirely because it increases my patience later, I've smuggled productivity back in through the side door.

Prayer becomes a technology for improving the Patience KPI.

Maybe presence itself matters.

Maybe relationship matters.

Maybe being with another person matters even when nothing is accomplished.

This isn't limited to spirituality.

Sit with someone who is grieving.

You may not solve anything.

You may say very little.

You may spend an hour in a room with a person whose pain is essentially unchanged when you leave.

Productivity: zero?

Only if productivity is the only category we have for value.

Listen to music without doing anything else.

Watch a sunset.

Walk slowly with someone you love.

Sit beside a hospital bed.

Hold a sleeping child.

None of these experiences need to produce a secondary benefit before they are allowed to matter.

**Not everything valuable has to produce something else in order to justify its existence.**

That's difficult for an optimization culture to accept because optimization assumes a function. Better according to what?

Sometimes the answer is obvious. Faster delivery. Lower cost. Better health. More reliable software.

But what is the optimization function for sitting beside someone you love?

What is the ROI of awe?

How many units of meaning should a piece of music produce before listening to it becomes a responsible use of time?

The questions sound absurd because we're applying a useful form of reasoning outside the domain where it remains useful.

There are parts of life in which value isn't downstream.

The experience itself is the point.

This is where transcendence and immanence meet for me.

Contemplative prayer is profoundly physical. I sit in a chair. I breathe. Time passes. My back occasionally hurts. The transcendent, if I encounter it at all, is encountered through an ordinary body in an ordinary room.

Spirituality doesn't require escaping the physical world.

It happens here.

Love happens here too. So does forgiveness. Compassion becomes action through hands and feet and money and time.

The transcendent touches the immanent.

But the immanent does not exhaust the transcendent.

A loving act can reveal love without containing all of love. A gift can reveal generosity without becoming a complete measure of generosity. A moment of silence can participate in prayer without giving us a quantity called Prayer Produced.

This gives us another way to think about evidence.

Maybe we don't always need a score.

Maybe sometimes we need a sign.

The Christian tradition often uses organic language for this. Seeds. Vines. Trees. Fruit. Jesus talks about knowing a tree by its fruit. Paul describes qualities such as love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.

Fruit is visible.

Something growing inside eventually appears in the world.

But fruit isn't a score.

It doesn't tell us Patience: 83.

It gives us a sign.

I can know someone for twenty years and reasonably say, "She's become more patient." I may have evidence. I've watched how she handles disappointment, conflict, difficult people, and ordinary frustration.

I don't need to know whether she's 17 percent more patient.

In fact, pretending I do may make my understanding worse.

This kind of knowing is slower. It requires relationship, memory, context, judgment, and humility. It is less precise than a dashboard.

That doesn't make it less real.

We can recognize without ranking.

We can notice without scoring.

We can see fruit without turning the tree into a quarterly business review.

And perhaps that is a more fitting way to approach realities whose meaning exceeds the categories we use to describe them.

### Not Everything That Grows Gets Bigger

We use the word *growth* constantly.

Businesses grow. Revenue grows. Markets grow. Churches grow.

Usually we mean more.

More customers. More money. More people. More activity.

Number go up.

But spiritual growth is strange because sometimes growth looks like less.

Less fear.

Less resentment.

Less need to win.

Less need to be noticed.

Less certainty that I understand everyone else's motives.

Less need to control what other people think of me.

Something is growing because something else is disappearing.

That makes spiritual maturity particularly inconvenient for a dashboard.

Sometimes the most important change in a person is subtraction.

An argument that once would have consumed three days no longer needs to be won. An old injury doesn't need to be rehearsed again. The need to prove something begins to loosen. A person becomes more comfortable leaving some questions unanswered.

What number would we like to assign that?

Maybe none.

Maybe we can simply notice.

This brings me back to the church.

A church can get bigger.

A church can also get deeper.

Those are not the same dimension.

And I want to be careful here too, because smallness isn't holiness. A church of thirty people can be profoundly unhealthy. A church of thirty thousand can do extraordinary good. Size does not become spiritually suspicious merely because it is measurable.

Size tells us about size.

Let it.

If attendance is growing, ask why. If it is declining, ask why. If giving increases, be grateful and use it wisely. If it falls, understand what is happening.

The numbers belong in the conversation because the church exists in a physical world.

But then keep asking what the point was.

I still care whether people come.

I care whether the music connects. If we play to an empty room every Sunday, I'm not going to announce that our worship has simply become too transcendent for popular appeal.

Reality gets a vote.

But I also don't want to help fill a room merely so that the room will be full.

I want what happens in that room to matter.

I want the music to create space for something that may not fit inside the categories we use to evaluate it. I want people to become more loving, more free, more compassionate, more awake to God and to one another.

I want those things to become physical.

I want compassion to feed someone.

I want forgiveness to change a relationship.

I want love to become action.

But I don't need to pretend that because the reality becomes visible in places, the visible parts contain the whole.

The numbers are part of the world.

So are we.

Count what can be counted.

Learn from it.

Let it challenge the stories we tell ourselves.

And when the measurement reaches the edge of what it can know, don't assume reality ended there.

Perhaps the limitation belongs to the instrument.

Perhaps the map has simply reached its edge.

And perhaps some of the most meaningful things in life begin exactly where the numbers start running out of language.
