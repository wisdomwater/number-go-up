# Chapter 5

## Every Metric Is a Message

### We're Just Tracking It

Most metrics start innocently enough.

Someone wants to know what's happening. A manager wants to understand whether a new process is working. Leadership wants visibility into a part of the organization that's difficult to see. A team has invested in a new tool and wants to know whether anyone is actually using it.

So they measure something.

That's reasonable. In fact, it's exactly what we've spent much of this book defending. Measurement gives us information. It allows reality to push back against intuition. Instead of sitting in a meeting debating whether people seem to be using the new tool more often, we can look at the data and discover that usage increased 23 percent.

Now we know something.

I think it's important to give managers the benefit of the doubt here, because it's easy to tell this story with a villain. Management creates dashboard. Management watches dashboard. Management demands number go up. Employees suffer.

Sometimes that happens, but most managers I've known are trying to do something considerably more difficult and considerably less sinister. They're trying to understand a complicated human system with limited time and incomplete information. They have teams to coordinate, commitments to manage, people to develop, information to communicate in several directions, and decisions to make with consequences they cannot fully predict.

Of course they want information.

The complication is that managers don't experience information in quite the same way employees do.

Imagine that I notice a number on a dashboard showing that AI usage declined this month. I mention it to a coworker over lunch.

"Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?"

Maybe we talk about it. Maybe we speculate about why. Maybe my coworker shrugs and takes another bite of sandwich.

Now imagine that person's manager asks exactly the same question during a one-on-one.

"Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?"

The words haven't changed, but the sentence has.

The manager may genuinely be curious. The employee, however, hears the question through the context of authority. Does my manager think this is a problem? Was I supposed to be using AI more? Is our team behind? Is this going to come up again? Should I have an explanation?

Nothing threatening had to be said. There was no mandate, no quota, and perhaps no target at all. There was simply a person with authority asking about a number.

**Authority gives words weight.**

This is one of the strange things about becoming a manager. You can continue feeling like the same person you were before the promotion while everyone else starts hearing you differently. A suggestion may sound like a request. Curiosity may sound like concern. A question you intended as "I wonder what's happening here" may arrive on the other side of the table as "You should probably fix this."

That doesn't mean managers should stop asking questions. A manager who notices something potentially important and then stares silently at it for six months to avoid sending the wrong message is probably not the management breakthrough we're looking for.

Managers need to ask questions precisely because they carry responsibility for things larger than their own individual work. The point is simply to recognize that measurement inside a hierarchy has more than one effect. The manager sees information; the employee sees the same information and also notices that the manager is paying attention to it.

That is already a message.

At first, leadership may genuinely say, "We're just tracking this," and mean exactly that. There is no quota or performance consequence. Then the number appears in a monthly meeting. Someone asks about it. The next month it appears again. A team with a high number gets mentioned, while a team with a low number gets asked what's happening.

Nobody has to be punished or rewarded for people to start learning. They notice which numbers receive attention and which questions keep coming back.

This is how a metric can gradually acquire cultural meaning without anyone deciding that it should. Culture rarely changes because someone sends an email announcing that, effective immediately, everyone should begin caring about a particular number. It changes through accumulated signals: the questions that get asked, the things that receive follow-up, the problems leaders notice, and the successes they praise.

Each signal may be perfectly reasonable on its own. Together, they tell a story.

That's why every metric is also a message.

### Cultural Gravity

Organizations measure what they care about. A company cares about revenue, so it measures revenue. It cares about quality, so it measures defects. It cares about customers, so it measures satisfaction. It cares about delivery, so it measures schedules and commitments.

There is something real behind all of those numbers. Corporate metrics are not generally arbitrary inventions created by people who enjoy spreadsheets a little too much. Revenue matters. If a company consistently spends more money than it brings in, eventually the philosophical discussion about metrics will be interrupted by bankruptcy.

Quality matters. Schedules matter. Customers matter. Productivity matters. The things organizations measure are often things they genuinely should care about.

Because they care about them, they pay attention to them.

Attention, however, is limited. A manager cannot pay equal attention to everything happening across a team, and an executive responsible for thousands of employees certainly cannot. There are too many projects, customers, technologies, relationships, decisions, and risks competing for awareness.

Metrics help direct that scarce attention. A sudden increase in defects says, "Look here." Falling customer satisfaction says the same thing. A project turning yellow on a dashboard gives leadership a reason to investigate.

That's incredibly useful. Imagine running a large organization without any mechanism for deciding where to look. Every problem would arrive as an anecdote, and every success would be a story. Leadership would have no reliable way to distinguish a systemic problem from the unusually persuasive person who happened to get invited to the meeting.

A metric works a little like a flashlight. It illuminates part of a much larger landscape and helps us focus.

A flashlight also leaves most of the landscape in the dark.

That doesn't mean nothing is there. It means we're not looking at it.

An experienced engineer might spend two hours helping a younger engineer understand a difficult part of the system. Knowledge moves. The younger engineer becomes more capable. Future mistakes may be prevented, and the team may be stronger because of those two hours.

Where does that appear on the dashboard?

Possibly nowhere. Meanwhile, the experienced engineer shipped two fewer units of whatever the organization happens to count.

Both realities exist. One is simply more legible to the measurement system.

This is where a natural process can begin to reverse itself. We start by measuring what we care about. Over time, we can also begin caring more about what we measure.

Not because everything else suddenly stops mattering, but because measured things keep returning. They appear in weekly meetings and monthly reports. They have trend lines and targets. They can turn red and demand explanations. The unmeasured things depend on somebody remembering to bring them up.

Imagine a leadership review with an hour available. Revenue has a slide. Schedule has a slide. Quality has a slide. Customer satisfaction has a slide. AI adoption has a slide. Staffing has a slide. Each comes with numbers, trends, colors, and comparisons.

Then somewhere near the end, someone says, "We also need to make sure we're encouraging thoughtful risk-taking."

Everyone agrees.

Of course we do.

Then the meeting moves on.

Nothing dishonest happened. Nobody secretly decided risk-taking was unimportant.

The measurable things simply arrived at the meeting better prepared.

They had charts.

I saw a version of this tension in my own company. At one point, we had two values that I found particularly interesting. One was risk-taking. We wanted people to challenge assumptions, explore new technologies, pursue ideas that might not work, and avoid becoming so afraid of failure that we only attempted things whose outcomes we already knew.

That makes sense, particularly in a technology company. Innovation requires uncertainty.

Another value was flawless execution. That makes sense too. Customers generally prefer products that work. Projects have commitments, products have schedules, and bugs have consequences. A company cannot spend all of its time courageously exploring fascinating possibilities while forgetting to ship anything.

We needed both.

The problem was that the two values entered the management system very differently.

Flawless execution practically walks into a quarterly review carrying its own PowerPoint. Did we hit the date? How many defects escaped? Did we meet the commitment? How accurate was the forecast? Was the milestone green?

Risk-taking is harder.

Suppose an engineer proposes an unconventional technical approach. The reasoning is sound, the potential payoff is significant, and the team understands the uncertainty. They decide the experiment is worth attempting.

Then it fails.

Was that good risk-taking?

It might have been. The decision can be good even when the outcome is bad. That's part of what makes a risk a risk.

But look at what happened to the measurable side of the equation. The experiment consumed time. The schedule slipped twelve days. Some work had to be discarded. Maybe another team was delayed.

The organization may also have gained valuable knowledge, but "valuable knowledge gained from a thoughtful failed experiment" is a difficult field to add to a dashboard.

Schedule variance is much easier.

**Flawless execution has dates. Risk-taking has stories.**

That difference creates what I think of as cultural gravity. The things that are easiest to measure naturally pull organizational attention toward themselves. They aren't necessarily more important, but they are easier to see, compare, target, and review.

This doesn't mean leadership is secretly lying when it says risk-taking matters. Leaders can genuinely value both risk-taking and execution. The problem is that one value is much easier to operationalize.

To understand whether a failed experiment represented good risk-taking, someone has to ask judgment questions. Was the reasoning sound? Was the potential benefit worth the uncertainty? Did the team understand the risk? Did we learn something useful? Would a reasonable person have made the same decision with the information available at the time?

Those questions require context, conversation, and sometimes disagreement.

The schedule metric is considerably more cooperative.

Missed by twelve days.

There. We know something.

We just don't yet know what it means.

### The Values on the Wall

Companies like values. Integrity. Innovation. Collaboration. Customer focus. Courage. Respect.

Nobody puts **Protect the Quarterly Metrics at All Costs** on the wall.

I also don't think most companies are lying about the values they choose. Organizations are full of people who sincerely want the place to be innovative, collaborative, respectful, and courageous. The problem is not necessarily a gap between what leaders believe and what they secretly believe.

It's often a gap between what can be stated as a value and what can be operationalized as one.

Courage is important, but it doesn't aggregate very well.

"Courage: 73 percent. Up four points quarter over quarter. Strong work, everyone."

The absurdity is obvious, so we don't usually do that. Instead, we measure things that can be measured reliably and hope the harder-to-measure values survive alongside them.

Sometimes they do. Sometimes the measurable values slowly become louder.

There is a progression here. Merely tracking something says that we think it is worth understanding. Giving it a target says we expect it to move. Comparing teams says performance relative to the number matters. Attaching rewards or consequences turns the volume up again.

At no point does someone need to declare that the metric is the most important thing. People can infer its importance from the machinery surrounding it.

This is why employees sometimes react to a metric more strongly than managers expect. From the manager's perspective, it may be one useful piece of information among many. From the employee's perspective, it may be the number that appears beside their team's name every month and turns red when they miss it.

The number may also be completely accurate.

Suppose a team misses a schedule by twelve days. The employee explains that a thoughtful technical risk failed, requiring the team to redesign part of the product.

Nothing about that explanation makes the schedule variance untrue. The team still missed the date.

**The number is accurate, and the number is incomplete.**

Those two statements should be able to coexist without one being treated as an attack on the other. Context does not erase the number. The number does not erase the context.

Trouble begins when the number becomes the evidence and the conversation becomes an excuse.

Once that happens, judgment has been quietly demoted. Instead of using judgment to interpret the metric, we begin using judgment to defend deviations from what the metric has already declared.

The number becomes the default truth, and the human being gets to file an appeal.

That is a strange amount of authority to give a representation we created ourselves.

The same thing can happen through language. Organizations gradually develop a vocabulary built around the abstractions they use to manage themselves. We talk about utilization, adoption, throughput, efficiency, engagement, performance, and execution.

"We need to increase adoption to improve utilization while maintaining execution against our productivity targets."

Everyone nods.

Somewhere, presumably, a human being is doing something.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this language. Large organizations need abstraction, as we've already seen. The problem comes when we lose the ability to translate the abstraction back into ordinary reality.

If AI adoption increased, what are people actually doing differently? Are they solving problems faster? Are they producing better work? Are they learning something useful? Are they using AI because it helps, or because we've made using AI the visible sign of progress?

We need to remain bilingual. We should be able to speak dashboard when the organization needs compression and speak reality when we need to understand what the compression means.

Otherwise, the abstractions can begin sounding more concrete than the work itself.

### What Happens Around Here?

Nobody needs to hand you the real employee handbook.

You figure it out.

You learn which deadlines are truly immovable and which ones aren't. You learn whether it's safe to disagree with your manager. You learn which processes are treated seriously and which exist mainly because nobody has gotten around to deleting them.

You learn what happens when someone makes a mistake. You learn what gets praised, what gets ignored, and what gets remembered at performance-review time.

Most importantly, you learn what happens when two values collide.

The company may say it values both quality and schedule. Fine. What happens when meeting the schedule requires taking a quality risk?

It may value both collaboration and individual performance. What happens when helping a coworker means getting less of your own visible work done?

It may value both risk-taking and flawless execution. What happens when a thoughtful risk fails and the project turns red?

The answers to those questions teach more than the values statement does.

Culture is not simply what an organization says it believes. Nor is it merely the behavior of its leaders. It accumulates through repeated experience.

**Culture is the accumulated answer to: What happens around here?**

People are remarkably attentive students.

If a manager says, "Bring me bad news early," and then becomes angry every time someone does, the team learns. If leadership says, "Take risks," and every failed experiment produces a painful review, people learn. If collaboration appears on the values page but promotions consistently go to the people with the strongest individual numbers, people learn.

They may never discuss the contradiction explicitly. They don't have to.

The system teaches through repetition.

This is where the three perspectives we've been following through the corporate part of the book finally come together.

**The measurer sees information. The measuree experiences an incentive. The culture absorbs a value.**

Imagine a leader decides to measure bug-closure time. From the leader's perspective, the metric provides useful information about how quickly problems are being resolved.

The engineer experiences something additional. This is a number the organization is watching, so closing bugs quickly may become safer than choosing difficult bugs whose resolution time is uncertain.

Then the behavior repeats across teams and over time. People begin to understand that fast closure is what good performance looks like around here.

Nobody had to write that sentence down.

The culture absorbed it.

This is why the question "What behavior have we made rational?" from the previous chapter isn't quite enough. It's a very good question when designing or interpreting a metric, but culture adds a longer time horizon.

We also need to ask:

**What are we teaching people?**

That question includes behavior, but it reaches beyond behavior. It asks what people are learning about safety, status, success, priorities, and judgment.

Are we teaching people that a missed target invites investigation or prosecution? Are we teaching them that context matters until it conflicts with the dashboard? Are we teaching them that difficult work is valued even when it produces ugly numbers?

These lessons accumulate slowly. Eventually they become assumptions.

That's when culture becomes powerful. People no longer need to stop and calculate what the metric wants them to do. They already know what happens around here.

### Yesterday Is Still in the Room

There is one more complication.

Organizations can change a metric much faster than they can change what people learned from it.

Suppose a company spends three years emphasizing schedule predictability. Missed dates produce difficult conversations. Teams that hit their commitments are praised. Managers learn to protect schedules, and employees learn to avoid uncertainty.

Over time, estimates become conservative and teams become cautious about experimentation.

Leadership eventually notices.

This isn't what we wanted.

The company wants more innovation. People should take thoughtful risks again.

So the CEO announces that failure is okay.

Everyone goes back to work.

And surprisingly little changes.

This can be frustrating from the leadership side. The message seems clear. We said risk-taking matters. We explicitly told people that thoughtful failure is acceptable. Why aren't they behaving differently?

Because an announcement happened once.

Experience happened every Tuesday for three years.

The people in the organization aren't necessarily being cynical or resistant. They're remembering. They learned through repeated experience that schedule misses were painful and uncertainty was dangerous. A new slide in the all-hands meeting does not instantly erase that education.

The organization remembers through its people.

This is why cultural change takes longer than changing the dashboard. If leadership wants people to believe that thoughtful risk-taking is genuinely safe, employees eventually need to see someone take a thoughtful risk, fail, and remain safe.

Then they need to see it again.

If leadership wants bad news brought forward early, people need to watch someone bring bad news without being punished for bringing it. If management wants judgment to matter more than mechanical compliance, employees need to see a manager look at a bad number, understand the context, and conclude that the person made the right decision.

This is not because employees require elaborate demonstrations before believing anything leadership says. It's because actions provide information too.

In fact, actions often provide better information.

A policy tells me what the organization intends to value. An experience tells me what happened when the value became expensive.

That's where values become real.

It's easy to value collaboration when collaboration costs nothing. It's easy to value quality when quality and schedule agree. It's easy to encourage risk when the risk succeeds.

The revealing moment comes when the values compete.

What happens when the thoughtful risk costs twelve days?

What happens when helping a coworker reduces someone's visible output?

What happens when doing right by the customer takes thirty-seven minutes instead of eight?

Those moments are cultural instruction.

A measurement system, then, is also a teaching system. The things we choose to track receive attention. The targets we attach to them create expectations. The way we respond when people miss those targets teaches them how much context and judgment really matter.

This doesn't mean we should become afraid to measure anything because employees might get the wrong idea. Organizations still need numbers. Managers still need visibility. Targets still have value.

It means we should treat the cultural effects of measurement as part of the design rather than as an unfortunate side effect.

When we introduce a metric, we should certainly ask what we hope to learn. We should ask what behavior it might make rational. We should think about what it leaves out and whether it can lose when reality requires something different.

But there is another question worth asking, particularly for anyone with authority:

**What are we teaching people?**

Because the dashboard will eventually change.

The quarter will end. Priorities will move. Targets will be revised. A new executive will arrive with a new set of slides. The metric everyone watched for three years may disappear almost overnight.

The people won't.

They will still know what happened when someone missed the date, took the risk, raised the problem, helped the coworker, or made the number look worse for a good reason.

The metric may be gone.

Yesterday is still in the room.
