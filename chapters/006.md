# 6 - The Limits of Control

> *We can influence the future without owning it.*

## What Exactly Do I Control?

A plan quietly invites a bargain with the future. If I do the right things consistently enough, I expect the result to follow. Eat less, move more, lose weight. Save steadily, become secure. Work hard, remain valuable.

There is enough truth in those equations to make them useful. Our actions matter, often a great deal, and pretending otherwise would make responsibility impossible. But usefulness can slide into something stronger, a feeling that the outcome is now owed to us because we behaved correctly.

Weight loss exposes the problem quickly. I can follow the plan for several days, step on the scale, and discover that the number has gone up. Water, sodium, digestion, sleep, stress, and a body with its own timing all get a vote, which is inconvenient when I would prefer a cleaner system of cause and effect.

The reverse can happen too. I can have an imperfect day and still see the number drop the next morning. Apparently the scale has not agreed to serve as an immediate moral accounting system, despite how often I try to employ it that way.

What I actually control is narrower than I sometimes want to admit. I can choose whether to take the walk, what to eat next, whether to track honestly, and whether to return to the plan after getting off course. I cannot directly command what the scale will say tomorrow.

That distinction sounds almost obvious when stated plainly. Living by it is harder. We like actions and outcomes to stay close together because it makes the world feel orderly, but much of life happens in the space between what we do and what eventually comes of it.

## The Goal Is Not an Action

“Lose weight” sounds like an instruction, but it is not actually something I can do. There is no *lose weight* button available to press after breakfast. Weight loss is an outcome that emerges from a collection of smaller behaviors carried out over time: what I eat, how much I move, how consistently I follow the plan, how I sleep, and a collection of biological processes that have shown very little interest in joining my project-management system.

This brings us back to something we encountered in the workplace: metrics are proxies. Body weight is an outcome metric. If my goal is to lose weight, the scale gives me useful evidence about whether that outcome is occurring, but it tells me very little about what to do at seven-thirty on a Tuesday evening.

So I move one level closer to action. I can track calories, steps, workouts, protein, or the number of days I stayed within the plan. These activity measures are useful because they point toward things I can actually choose to do.

The relationship between activity and outcome, however, is never perfect. Ten thousand steps do not mechanically produce a particular number of ounces lost, and hitting a calorie target today does not guarantee a lower weight tomorrow. The activity measures are proxies for behaviors that tend to improve my chances of reaching the outcome, not little levers connected directly to the scale.

We have seen the same problem at work. Commits can stand in for productivity until someone produces more commits without producing more value, and badge swipes can represent presence until employees become unusually creative about what counts as being present. Once the proxy separates from the purpose, the number can improve while the thing we actually care about stays put.

Self-measurement is vulnerable to the same drift. I can become very good at closing the rings, maintaining a streak, logging every calorie, or hitting an arbitrary step count while quietly forgetting why I started measuring any of it. The proxy gives me something immediate to accomplish, which feels satisfying, but satisfaction and progress are not always the same thing.

That does not make the activity measure broken. It means the measure has to remain connected to its purpose. Calories, steps, workouts, and habits matter because they influence an outcome I care about, but none of them *is* the outcome, and none can guarantee it.

## Reality Has More Variables

Human systems are wonderfully complicated, even when the system contains only one human being. A simple equation is comforting: do this, and that will follow. Eat less and lose weight. Work hard and advance. Save diligently and become secure.

Reality does not always honor the neatness of those equations. The body alone is a tangle of metabolism, sleep, stress, medication, appetite, hydration, digestion, muscle, age, and genetics, all interacting in ways that are only partly visible from the outside. Add emotion, habit, memory, and environment, and the little model we built on Sunday evening begins to look somewhat optimistic.

The same is true of larger human systems. At work, one person's effort is filtered through managers, coworkers, budgets, strategy, market conditions, organizational politics, and plain luck. In finance, disciplined saving meets inflation, market cycles, interest rates, taxes, and events nobody remembered to put into the retirement calculator.

That complexity frustrates us because we prefer causes that stay close to their effects. If I behave well today, I want evidence tomorrow morning. If I work hard this year, I want the review to reflect it cleanly. If I save responsibly for decades, I would very much like the universe to issue some sort of certificate declaring me safe.

Complex systems rarely offer that kind of contract. One action contributes to an outcome without owning it. Sometimes the connection is strong, sometimes weak, and sometimes the effect disappears beneath enough other variables that it takes a long time to see.

This does not make disciplined action pointless. Eating well still improves the odds of better health. Saving still improves the odds of greater financial resilience, and doing good work still improves the odds of career stability. The important word is *improves*.

## Agency Without Sovereignty

Life is less like issuing commands and more like participating in a conversation. We act, the world responds, and then we decide what to do with what came back. Sometimes the response is close to what we expected, and sometimes reality seems to have answered a different question.

That is true whether I am trying to lose weight, build software, invest money, or navigate a career. I can choose an action with intention, but I do not have my hands on every lever influencing the result. Other people, biology, timing, markets, institutions, and chance all enter the system with agendas of their own.

This is where humility becomes useful. I do not mean humility as making myself smaller than I am, but as seeing my actual scale within the system. I am a cause, sometimes an important one, but I am not the only cause.

That changes how feedback feels. If the result is worse than I hoped, I can ask what it teaches me without assuming my effort was worthless. If the result is better than expected, I can appreciate it without pretending that I personally engineered every condition that made it possible.

The pattern becomes iterative: act, observe, adjust, and act again. The task is not to discover the perfect set of inputs that will finally force reality into compliance. It is to remain responsive enough to learn from what actually happens.

There is freedom in that, though it is not the kind of freedom control promises. I do not need to command every outcome in order to participate meaningfully in my life. I need enough agency to choose the next wise action and enough humility to notice what comes back.

## What Money Can and Cannot Secure

I have been saving and investing since I started working. Over the years, I have contributed to retirement accounts, put money into brokerage accounts, and tried to let time do what time does best. The habits themselves are not particularly glamorous: live below my means, save regularly, diversify, and become suspicious whenever an opportunity sounds a little too enthusiastic about making me rich.

Those habits have given me something real. Money creates options. It can absorb a surprise, soften a job loss, pay for a repair, or buy time when life does not cooperate with the original plan. In that sense, saving does provide a form of security.

It does not provide certainty. At the time I am writing this, economists and investors are debating whether the enthusiasm surrounding artificial intelligence has inflated another market bubble. Maybe it has, maybe it has not, and I have no special window into the future that everybody else somehow missed.

I can read the arguments, look at valuations, listen to people who know more than I do, and make the best decisions I can. The market will still do whatever millions of people, companies, governments, technologies, interest rates, fears, enthusiasms, and unforeseen events collectively persuade it to do. My brokerage account gives me a seat in that system, not control of it.

So I return to principles rather than predictions. Live below your means. Diversify. Avoid putting everything on one story about the future. If something sounds too good to be true, there is a respectable chance that somebody is hoping I will stop asking questions.

Those principles do not guarantee a particular outcome. They are ways of behaving wisely inside a future I cannot see. The distinction matters because I once imagined financial security as a number I might eventually reach, as though there were some account balance at which uncertainty would sign the paperwork and leave.

A larger balance can make life more resilient, and I am grateful for that. But the feeling of being secure is not deposited automatically along with the money. Some part of security has to be cultivated in the ability to adapt, live within limits, and trust that I can respond even when I cannot predict.

## Good Work, Uncertain Outcomes

I have spent more than a quarter century at the same technology company, which is long enough to see several versions of the place come and go. Strategies change, organizations reorganize, products rise and fall, and every so often the company enters another round of layoffs. Some of those reductions have been severe, with entire groups shrinking by more than twenty percent.

I have seen poor performers lose their jobs, which fits the tidy story we like to tell about employment. Perform well, stay valuable, remain employed. But I have also watched highly capable people disappear because a project was canceled, a business shifted direction, or leadership decided that a particular kind of work no longer belonged in the future they were building.

That is harder to fit into the equation. Hard work still matters. Competence, reliability, judgment, relationships, and the ability to solve difficult problems all increase the value a person brings to an organization, and becoming better at what I do remains one of the most sensible actions available to me.

But increasing my value is not the same as guaranteeing my place. I can control how seriously I approach the work, how well I prepare, how much I learn, and how I treat the people around me. I cannot control the market, the budget, the next reorganization, or whether leadership decides that the product I have spent years supporting is no longer where the company intends to place its bets.

This is another place where activity and outcome refuse to form a perfect mechanical link. A strong performance record is a meaningful signal, but it is not a force field. Sometimes excellent work creates opportunity, and sometimes excellent work happens to be attached to a strategy that gets erased from the slide deck.

That uncertainty is difficult because employment rarely feels like a purely economic transaction. We spend enormous portions of our lives at work, build relationships there, develop expertise there, and gradually weave part of our identity into the contribution we make. A spreadsheet may describe a workforce reduction as a percentage, but every percentage point contains people who had plans for Monday morning.

## We Are Not Blank Slates

A machine can often be reassigned with relatively little ceremony. Install different software, change the configuration, point it at another workload, and the hardware begins doing something new. Human beings bring more history into a transition.

We arrive with loyalties, fears, habits, relationships, identities, and a finite amount of energy. A reorganization may look clean on an org chart, but the people inside it are not being moved from one empty box to another. They remember the last round of layoffs, the manager who believed in them, the project they spent years building, and the quiet fear that the next decision may land differently.

This is one reason uncertainty around work can feel larger than the event described on paper. Employment is about income, but it is also about belonging, competence, routine, status, and usefulness. A change in one part of that structure can reverberate through several others at once.

Our biology does not help us keep the categories separate. Human beings are deeply social creatures, and for most of our history exclusion from the group carried real consequences. Modern employment is not a prehistoric tribe, but the nervous system is not always especially interested in organizational nuance; it can hear “headcount reduction” and respond with something much older: *Am I still safe here? Do I still belong?*

That makes the modern pursuit of efficiency a peculiar fit for human systems. Companies need to reorganize, automate, cut costs, and move people toward changing priorities. Those decisions may be economically rational, but the people affected are not interchangeable components waiting patiently for reassignment.

They are already carrying a life into the decision. The same review score, layoff announcement, or role change can land very differently depending on what a person has lived through before it. Human systems absorb outcomes through memory and emotion as well as logic.

A model of performance or efficiency can be numerically clean while missing most of that texture. The number may still be useful. It just arrives in a human nervous system rather than on a blank slate.

## Living With Unfinished Control

Metrics help us act because they give shape to things that would otherwise remain vague. Outcome measures tell us where we hope to arrive, while activity measures give us behaviors we can actually perform. The trouble is that biology, markets, organizations, chance, and other people all live in the space between the two.

That does not make the measures useless. Good habits improve probabilities, disciplined work increases value, saving creates resilience, and thoughtful choices can move life in a better direction. What they cannot do is turn participation into command.

The tension becomes sharper because human beings do not receive outcomes as neutral data points. A market decline can feel like danger. A layoff can feel like rejection, and a number on a scale can feel like evidence that something about us is failing.

The system may be reporting one thing while the person hearing the report receives something much larger. That is where measurement begins crossing into more intimate territory, because the number is no longer only describing what happened.

Sooner or later, the scale, the account balance, the performance review, and the productivity score begin brushing against a question they were never designed to answer.

What am I worth?
