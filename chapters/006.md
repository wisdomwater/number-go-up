# Chapter 6 — The Quantified Self

## We Have Dashboards for Ourselves

We are a numbers-obsessed culture.

I realize this is a strange complaint from someone who has spent his career as an engineer.

Numbers are kind of our thing.

But somewhere along the way, measurement escaped the laboratory, the factory, and the quarterly business review and followed us home.

Then it climbed onto our wrists.

Modern technology has given us an extraordinary ability to measure ourselves.

We can track our weight.

Our steps.

Our calories.

Our heart rate.

Our resting heart rate.

Our heart-rate variability.

Our sleep.

Not merely how long we slept, either. That would apparently be far too simple.

We can know how much time we spent in light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep. We can receive a sleep score in the morning informing us how successfully we performed the unconscious activity of being asleep.

You can wake up feeling perfectly refreshed, look at your phone, and discover that you slept poorly.

Good morning.

We can measure how far we run, how fast we run, our pace per mile, elevation gain, cadence, heart-rate zones, estimated calories burned, and how today's run compares with one we did seventeen months ago.

We can track how much water we drink.

How long we meditate.

How many books we read.

How many consecutive days we've studied Spanish.

How much time we spend looking at our phones, which we learn by looking at our phones.

We can track our spending, savings, investment returns, net worth, credit score, productivity, focus time, screen time, work time, downtime, and probably the amount of time we spend tracking all the other things.

Technology has made enormous leaps in what we can measure.

Sensors became smaller.

Computing became cheaper.

Storage became nearly invisible.

Our phones became permanent companions.

Wearable devices can collect information continuously without requiring us to do much of anything.

Things that once would have required specialized equipment can now be measured while we're standing in line for coffee.

This is genuinely remarkable.

But something else happened at the same time.

The measurements became scores.

And the scores became games.

Ten thousand steps.

Close your rings.

Maintain your streak.

Beat yesterday.

Reach the next level.

Hit your goal.

Earn a badge.

Congratulations.

Confetti.

Your watch is proud of you.

Personal technology didn't simply give us more information about ourselves. It learned something that every video game designer already knew:

Human beings really like making numbers go up.

Give me a number and I might look at it.

Give me a target and I'll try to hit it.

Give me a progress bar and I will experience a strange psychological need to fill it.

Give me a streak and suddenly missing Tuesday feels like destroying something I've spent forty-seven days building.

Nothing physically exists.

There is no streak.

The universe has not been carefully recording my daily language lessons on a stone tablet.

There is a number on a screen.

And yet I don't want to break it.

That's the power of gamification.

It takes behaviors we believe are good for us and gives them immediate feedback.

Exercise today, earn the points today.

Read today, preserve the streak today.

Walk another eight hundred steps, close the ring today.

The actual benefits of many healthy behaviors may take months or years to appear.

The little digital celebration arrives immediately.

That's clever.

And often helpful.

If a watch gets someone off the couch because they're 900 steps short of their goal, fantastic.

If a streak gets someone to practice a language every day, the streak has done something useful.

If watching a savings number increase encourages someone to spend less than they earn, number go up is doing its job.

This is not a chapter about throwing our watches into the ocean.

The interesting question is why all of this feels so natural to us.

Because the technology arrived inside a culture that was already primed for it.

We love optimization.

Optimization is one of the great promises of modern life.

There is always a better morning routine.

A better workout.

A better diet.

A better budgeting system.

A better productivity method.

A better way to sleep.

A better way to breathe.

A better way to organize our notes.

A better way to organize the notes about how we're organizing our notes.

Somewhere out there is apparently an optimal version of me, and if I can just collect enough data, perhaps I can finally locate him.

Optimization has become something close to a cultural god.

It promises that life can be improved if we can understand the variables.

Measure.

Analyze.

Adjust.

Repeat.

This works spectacularly well in some domains.

Engineers optimize systems because systems really can be optimized.

Change an algorithm and software gets faster.

Modify a manufacturing process and defects decrease.

Redesign a component and it uses less power.

Measure the result, change something, measure again.

It's intoxicating because it works.

Then we look at ourselves.

Surely the same principle applies.

So we turn the instruments inward.

We measure our bodies.

Our habits.

Our work.

Our money.

Our attention.

Our sleep.

Our leisure.

Sometimes even our spirituality.

And we start looking for opportunities to optimize.

There is something deeply appealing about this.

Numbers create a sense of control.

Life is complicated.

Numbers are clean.

**I want to be healthier** is vague.

**10,000 steps** is something I can do.

**I want to learn more** is vague.

**Read 30 books this year** gives me a target.

**I want to be financially secure** is terrifyingly broad.

**Increase savings by 10 percent** fits nicely into a spreadsheet.

The number gives shape to the aspiration.

It turns something fuzzy into something actionable.

And just as importantly, it tells us whether we're winning.

That's where the dashboard comes in.

We no longer simply have goals.

We have personal performance indicators.

The corporation has revenue, schedule, quality, and customer satisfaction.

I have weight, steps, sleep, savings, and screen time.

The corporation has quarterly targets.

I have daily ones.

The corporation has dashboards.

So do I.

The corporation reviews its performance.

I check mine before breakfast.

We've spent the first half of this book examining what happens when organizations measure human beings.

Now something interesting is about to happen.

We're going to remove the organization.

There is no executive.

No manager.

No HR department.

Nobody is forcing us to track anything.

We buy the devices ourselves.

We install the apps.

We choose the targets.

We create the dashboards.

And then we live underneath them.

We have brought the measurement system home.

## This Is Actually Pretty Amazing

After all of that, I should probably say something important.

This stuff is amazing.

Seriously.

I can make fun of my watch for grading my sleep, but the fact that a device strapped to my wrist can collect information about my body throughout the night is extraordinary.

I can step on a scale in the morning and get a reasonably accurate measurement of my weight in seconds.

I can go for a walk carrying a device that knows where I went, how far I traveled, how quickly I moved, and roughly how hard my body was working.

I can open an app and see years of financial history.

I can look at patterns in my spending.

I can see how often I exercise.

I can track what I eat.

I can compare what I intended to do with what I actually did.

For most of human history, ordinary people had access to almost none of this information.

Now we carry an astonishing collection of measurement instruments in our pockets.

And the reason we keep using them isn't simply because we're obsessed with numbers.

It's because the numbers can tell us things we need to know.

Human beings are not particularly reliable measurement instruments.

We remember selectively.

We estimate badly.

We notice dramatic events and forget ordinary ones.

We construct stories about ourselves that may be directionally correct and numerically ridiculous.

Ask me how much I've been eating lately and I can give you an answer based on a complicated scientific instrument known as **how I feel like I've been doing**.

I've been pretty good.

What does *pretty good* mean?

No idea.

But it feels reassuring.

Then I track my food.

Oh.

Apparently *pretty good* included more calories than I remembered.

The numbers didn't create the calories.

They made them visible.

This is one of the great gifts of measurement.

It gives reality a way to disagree with us.

We've encountered this before.

At work, a manager can believe a team is performing poorly until the data reveals otherwise. An engineer can believe a change made software faster until the benchmark says it didn't. A company can believe customers love a product until customer data suggests a more complicated story.

Personal measurement can do exactly the same thing.

We can believe we're active.

Then we count our steps.

We can believe we're sleeping enough.

Then we look at when we actually go to bed.

We can believe we're not spending very much on restaurants.

Then we categorize three months of transactions and discover that apparently we have been personally supporting the local food-service economy.

The number pushes back.

That's useful because self-deception doesn't always look like deception.

Usually we're not consciously lying to ourselves.

We're approximating.

We remember the salad.

We forget the handful of chips.

We remember Saturday's long walk.

We forget that we barely moved Monday through Friday.

We remember the month when we saved a lot of money.

We don't quite integrate the six months when we didn't.

Our minds are telling stories.

The data is keeping receipts.

Neither one necessarily has the complete picture.

But the receipts matter.

This is especially valuable when change happens slowly.

If I gained twenty pounds overnight, I would notice.

Among other things, I would have several questions.

But bodies don't usually work that way.

A pound appears.

Then another.

Weight fluctuates.

Clothes stretch.

We adjust.

The new normal becomes normal remarkably quickly.

Months later, we can find ourselves somewhere we never consciously decided to go.

Measurement interrupts that process.

A scale can say:

**Something is changing.**

A bank balance can say:

**This trajectory isn't sustainable.**

A fitness tracker can say:

**You're moving considerably less than you think.**

A screen-time report can say:

**You may want to sit down before looking at this.**

These aren't moral judgments.

They're information.

And sometimes information is exactly what we need.

Measurement also makes progress visible.

This matters because meaningful change is often painfully slow.

Exercise for one day and you probably won't look dramatically different in the mirror.

Save money for one week and retirement remains inconveniently far away.

Practice a musical instrument for twenty minutes and you may sound approximately as bad as you did twenty minutes earlier.

But accumulate the data and something becomes visible.

Three months.

Six months.

A year.

The trend appears.

That's motivating.

The number can preserve evidence of progress during periods when progress is difficult to feel.

This is one reason personal dashboards can be so powerful.

They extend our perception.

They help us see across time.

Memory gives us moments.

Data can give us patterns.

And patterns can help us make better decisions.

That's the positive case for the quantified self.

It isn't trivial.

It isn't foolish.

And it isn't something I want to dismiss.

If I'm trying to improve my health, I want information.

If I'm trying to improve my finances, I want information.

If I'm trying to understand my habits, I want information.

Intuition matters, but intuition without feedback can become a wonderfully sophisticated way of telling ourselves what we'd like to hear.

Numbers can challenge that.

They can introduce rigor where memory is fuzzy.

They can expose trends before those trends become crises.

They can tell us whether the thing we thought was working appears to be working.

They can help us learn.

So keep the scale.

Keep the watch.

Keep the spreadsheet.

Keep the app.

Measurement is a wonderful tool.

But something happens after the number appears.

I don't merely learn that I weigh a certain amount.

I react to it.

The number goes down.

I feel good.

The number goes up.

I feel bad.

Suddenly the scale isn't merely measuring my body.

It's evaluating my morning.

And that's where things start getting interesting.

## The Scale Gives Me My Performance Review

I am trying to lose weight.

A lot of weight.

This makes the scale an obvious tool.

If the goal is to lose weight, it seems reasonable to occasionally determine how much I weigh.

So I step on the scale.

The scale thinks for a moment.

Then it delivers the verdict.

**Number go down.**

Excellent.

I am a disciplined and responsible human being whose wise choices are finally producing the results they deserve.

Perhaps I have finally figured this out.

Perhaps this is the beginning of a new chapter in my life.

Perhaps I should write a book about weight loss.

Twenty-four hours later, I step on the scale again.

**Number go up.**

I am a failure.

Apparently everything is broken.

The diet doesn't work.

My body doesn't work.

Science may not work.

Yesterday I was a model of disciplined self-improvement. Today I am considering whether pants with elastic waistbands represent a more realistic long-term strategy.

This is the most brutally efficient performance review system ever invented.

Step on.

Wait three seconds.

Receive feedback.

No manager required.

And the scale has one particularly compelling feature.

It gives me a number.

Not a vague assessment.

Not:

**Michael appears to be making reasonable progress but should continue focusing on several development opportunities.**

No.

247.6.

Very precise.

It even includes a decimal point.

We've already established what decimal points do to the human mind.

They make things look serious.

The problem is that my body has not agreed to the performance-management system I've constructed for it.

I would like the arrangement to be simple.

Eat well.

Exercise.

Lose weight.

Repeat.

Preferably in that order and preferably on a schedule that allows me to update the spreadsheet every Friday.

Instead, weight fluctuates.

Water moves around.

Food moves through the digestive system.

Salt matters.

Carbohydrates matter.

Exercise can affect water retention.

The time of day matters.

A dozen things can move the number in either direction without telling me much about whether I lost body fat yesterday.

The scale doesn't know any of this.

To be fair, the scale isn't claiming to.

The scale has one job.

It measures how much I weigh at this particular moment.

That's it.

It does not display:

**Congratulations. You made thoughtful food choices yesterday.**

It does not know whether I exercised.

It does not know whether I ate vegetables.

It does not know whether I stopped eating when I was full.

It does not know whether I spent the evening fighting the desire to order something involving melted cheese.

It knows my weight.

**The number knows what the number knows. Nothing more.**

I am the one who adds the rest.

This produces some wonderfully absurd situations.

I can have an excellent day.

I eat reasonably.

I exercise.

I make choices completely aligned with what I'm trying to accomplish.

Then I wake up the next morning and weigh more.

And emotionally, it feels like failure.

Or I can have a terrible day.

I eat far more than I intended.

I barely move.

I make choices that clearly move me away from the habits I'm trying to build.

The next morning I step on the scale.

Down a pound.

Success!

Apparently the pizza was part of the plan.

If I were managing an employee this way, I hope someone would intervene.

Imagine an employee does everything we asked.

They follow the process.

They exercise good judgment.

They do excellent work.

Then some noisy outcome metric moves in the wrong direction and I say:

**I'm disappointed in your performance.**

The employee would reasonably respond:

**But I did the right things.**

And I would point solemnly at the dashboard.

**The number went up.**

We spent several chapters examining why that is a bad way to manage people.

Then I walk into my bathroom and do it to myself before breakfast.

This is where personal measurement becomes psychologically interesting.

The number starts as information.

But information can become evaluation remarkably quickly.

I don't simply observe:

**My weight increased 1.4 pounds since yesterday.**

I experience:

**Yesterday didn't work.**

Those are not the same statement.

One is measurement.

The other is interpretation.

And the interpretation can influence what I do next.

Maybe I get discouraged.

Maybe I restrict food more aggressively.

Maybe I decide the plan isn't working.

Maybe I abandon it entirely.

Or perhaps the number goes down and creates the opposite problem.

Maybe I loosen up.

Maybe I decide yesterday's choices were apparently fine.

Maybe I learn the wrong lesson from a favorable fluctuation.

The measurement hasn't failed.

The scale is probably working perfectly.

The problem is the authority I've given it.

I've asked a measurement of today's weight to evaluate yesterday's behavior.

Those things are related.

They are not identical.

This is the same mistake organizations make when they collapse a proxy into the thing it represents.

Weight matters to me.

I'm not going to solve the problem by declaring the scale an oppressive instrument of numerical tyranny and throwing it away.

If I want to lose a significant amount of weight, the long-term trend in my weight is useful information.

I want to know it.

But there is no activity called **lose one pound** that I can add to my calendar for Thursday afternoon.

I can't directly perform weight loss.

I can make choices.

My body produces outcomes.

Those outcomes happen on their own timetable.

That's an important distinction, and we'll come back to it.

For now, I'm interested in something stranger.

Nobody imposed this measurement system on me.

Nobody from corporate wellness is standing outside the bathroom with a clipboard.

I bought the scale.

I chose the goal.

I decided when to measure.

I decided what the number means.

And then I stood there feeling judged by it.

At work, we've been able to distinguish between two people.

The measurer creates the system.

The measuree lives underneath it.

Here, that distinction disappears.

I am both of them.

## I Am the Manager and the Employee

At work, the roles were relatively clear.

Someone creates a metric.

Someone else lives underneath it.

Management wants visibility.

Employees experience expectations.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences an incentive.

This distinction helped us understand why the same metric could look so different depending on which side of the dashboard you were standing.

Then I stepped on the scale.

Now there is only one person in the room.

Me.

I chose the metric.

I chose the target.

I bought the equipment.

I collect the data.

I review the results.

And when performance is disappointing, I know exactly where to find the employee responsible.

This is remarkably efficient.

Most companies require an entire management structure to create this much anxiety.

I can do it by myself before breakfast.

I am the manager.

I am the employee.

And apparently neither of us is particularly pleasant to work with.

Think about how strange this is.

Manager Me establishes the objective:

**Lose weight.**

Employee Me agrees.

Seems reasonable.

Manager Me creates the measurement system.

We'll weigh regularly.

We'll track progress.

We'll establish targets.

We'll look for trends.

Excellent.

Then the numbers start coming in.

Employee Me has several good days.

Manager Me is pleased.

**Great work. Keep it up.**

Then the scale jumps two pounds.

Manager Me would like a word.

Employee Me attempts to provide context.

**Weight fluctuates. I ate well yesterday. I exercised. This is probably water.**

Manager Me has reviewed the dashboard.

**I hear what you're saying. But the number went up.**

We have seen this meeting before.

The only difference is that now both people are in my head.

This is one of the peculiar consequences of self-measurement.

We can internalize the entire organizational system.

Targets.

Performance reviews.

Rewards.

Disappointment.

Comparison.

Optimization.

Even surveillance.

Except now surveillance is remarkably convenient because we voluntarily strap the monitoring equipment to our own bodies.

My watch doesn't need permission to check whether I'm moving.

I paid for it to do that.

Then it reminds me that I've been sitting too long.

Thank you, tiny middle manager attached to my wrist.

Again, this can be useful.

Sometimes I really should stand up.

The interesting part is how quickly an external measurement can become an internal voice.

The device doesn't actually care.

The scale isn't disappointed.

My watch isn't concerned about my career trajectory.

The calorie-tracking app does not lie awake at night wondering whether I'm reaching my potential.

They're displaying information.

**I supply the judgment.**

That's important.

Because it's tempting to blame the technology.

We say the watch makes us anxious.

The scale makes us feel bad.

The productivity app makes us feel behind.

Sometimes technology is deliberately designed to create psychological pressure. Gamification works precisely because designers understand motivation.

But the deeper mechanism doesn't require an app.

Give me a notebook and a pen and I can become my own micromanager.

I can create a checklist.

Establish targets.

Track streaks.

Assign scores.

Compare this week with last week.

Circle disappointing results.

Maybe add a little red ink if the system isn't sufficiently hostile.

The technology makes measurement easier.

The human mind supplies the meaning.

And because I am both sides of the relationship, something else happens.

There may be nobody around to challenge the metric.

In an organization, an employee can at least theoretically say:

**I don't think this number captures what happened.**

A good manager might listen.

A coworker might provide context.

A team might push back on a bad target.

There is another perspective in the system.

When I'm measuring myself, I can become a closed loop.

I create the standard.

I fail the standard.

I judge the failure.

Then I use the judgment as evidence that the standard was correct.

Nobody gets invited to the meeting who might ask:

**Is this actually helping?**

This is especially easy because self-discipline is usually considered a virtue.

We admire people who hold themselves accountable.

And we should.

There is enormous value in taking responsibility for our choices rather than constantly explaining them away.

If I consistently eat more than I intended, declaring that calories are merely a social construct is unlikely to move me toward my goals.

Reality matters.

Accountability matters.

The numbers can help.

But accountability and punishment are not the same thing.

Neither are rigor and hostility.

A good manager doesn't improve performance by treating every disappointing result as evidence of a defective employee.

Why would I expect that strategy to work better when the employee is me?

This is where the lessons from the workplace become unexpectedly personal.

Earlier we said a healthy manager should be able to look at a bad metric and ask:

**What happened?**

Not:

**Who failed?**

The same principle applies here.

The scale goes up.

What happened?

I missed my exercise target.

What happened?

I spent more than I planned.

What happened?

I broke the streak.

What happened?

That question creates information.

**What's wrong with me?** creates something else.

There is a difference between investigating our behavior and prosecuting ourselves for it.

One produces learning.

The other mostly produces a very unpleasant workplace with only one employee.

And I can't quit.

This doesn't mean lowering every standard until success is guaranteed.

Manager Me does not need to become the kind of manager who responds to six months of missed targets with:

**You're doing amazing. No notes.**

Sometimes I need to change my behavior.

Sometimes the data is telling me exactly what I don't want to hear.

Sometimes I made a commitment to myself and didn't keep it.

That deserves honesty.

But if the purpose of measurement is improvement, then the response to the number should serve improvement.

That's the standard.

Does this way of measuring myself help me learn?

Does it help me make better choices?

Does it help me notice patterns?

Does it help me become the person I'm trying to become?

Or have I created a personal bureaucracy whose primary function is documenting my inadequacy?

That's a question worth asking.

Because once I become both measurer and measuree, the relationship between those two roles matters.

I can become my own coach.

I can become my own analyst.

I can become my own teacher.

Or I can become the worst manager I've ever had.

And whichever one I choose, I'm taking them home with me.

## Self-Measurement Changes Behavior

Of course, there is a reason we do all of this.

We want our behavior to change.

If I put a step counter on my wrist and continue living exactly as I did before, the device isn't accomplishing much.

The whole point is that I look down at four o'clock, see that I've taken 3,800 steps, and think:

**I should probably go for a walk.**

Then I go for a walk.

Success.

Measurement changed behavior.

This is not an unintended consequence.

It's the product.

The same thing happens when I track food.

Without tracking, I might pour some chips into a bowl and think:

**That seems reasonable.**

Then I measure them.

Apparently my definition of *reasonable* has been doing some fairly ambitious work.

The number changes what I do next.

Maybe I take fewer.

Maybe I choose something else.

Maybe I eat them anyway, but at least now the chips and I are operating with full disclosure.

Financial tracking works the same way.

Watch your spending closely enough and purchases begin to feel different.

A twenty-dollar expense is no longer merely twenty dollars.

It's twenty dollars that will appear later in a category.

The purchase has acquired an audience.

Even if the audience is just me.

This is why measurement can be such an effective tool for changing habits.

It interrupts autopilot.

It brings consequences closer to the behavior.

Many of the things we want for ourselves have delayed rewards.

Exercise today, benefit later.

Save money today, benefit much later.

Eat well today, maybe see a change on the scale eventually.

Practice an instrument today, become better gradually.

Our brains aren't always thrilled by this arrangement.

We tend to prefer rewards with somewhat faster shipping.

So measurement creates one.

Take the walk.

Watch the step count increase.

Save the money.

Watch the balance increase.

Finish the workout.

Close the ring.

Practice the language.

Preserve the streak.

The long-term benefit may still be months away, but the number moves now.

That's powerful.

Gamification makes the feedback even stronger.

The app doesn't merely record the behavior.

It celebrates it.

Lights.

Badges.

Animations.

Points.

Streaks.

Levels.

A small digital fire because I have apparently performed the heroic act of opening an app for nine consecutive days.

It can feel silly.

It also works.

But once a measurement begins changing behavior, something familiar happens.

We begin optimizing.

And as we've already established, human beings are extremely good at optimization.

Suppose my goal is to walk more.

I set a target of 10,000 steps.

Wonderful.

The step count is a proxy for physical activity.

Then it's 9:30 at night and I have 9,614 steps.

Now I have a decision to make.

I could go to bed.

Or I could walk around the kitchen for 386 steps.

At some point my wife may reasonably ask what I'm doing.

**Improving my health.**

Am I?

Maybe.

Walking is good.

Those 386 steps aren't fake.

But something interesting has happened.

A few weeks ago, the goal was to become more active.

Now the goal is to make the number say 10,000.

The proxy and the purpose are still related.

They're just no longer exactly the same thing.

This can become even more obvious with streaks.

Suppose I decide I want to read more.

Great goal.

I create a daily reading streak.

Also reasonable.

Then one night I realize I'm exhausted.

It's late.

I should sleep.

But I haven't read yet.

So I open a book and stare at three pages while absorbing approximately none of them.

Streak preserved.

Reading achieved.

Knowledge somewhat less certain.

The system says I succeeded.

The point might disagree.

This is Goodhart's Law in sweatpants.

When the measure becomes the target, our behavior begins orienting toward the measure.

And because we're measuring ourselves, we can become surprisingly creative about it.

We choose the easiest workout that closes the ring.

We log the task because completing the checklist feels better than working on the messy problem that matters.

We choose the short book because we're trying to hit a reading goal.

We avoid looking at an account because we don't want to see the number.

We reorganize our productivity system instead of doing the work.

None of this necessarily involves conscious cheating.

That's important.

Just as in organizations, optimization is normal.

The metric gives us a definition of success.

We naturally begin moving toward it.

Sometimes that's exactly what we wanted.

If my step count gets me walking every day, fantastic.

If tracking food changes my portions, excellent.

If a savings goal causes me to reconsider purchases, the metric is serving its purpose.

The problem begins when success in the metric separates from success in the life.

That's the test.

**Am I improving my life, or am I improving the evidence that I'm improving my life?**

Sometimes the answer is both.

Those are the best metrics.

The number goes up because the underlying reality is getting better.

I walk more, and my step count rises.

I save more, and my savings balance rises.

I practice consistently, and my streak continues.

Everything is aligned.

But sometimes the two begin pulling apart.

Maybe maintaining the exercise streak means training when my body needs rest.

Maybe hitting the calorie target causes me to make food choices that technically fit the number but leave me hungry and miserable.

Maybe maximizing productivity means squeezing every unstructured moment out of my day until I've optimized away the ability to think.

Maybe the sleep tracker itself makes me anxious about whether I'm sleeping correctly.

That last one is particularly impressive.

I can now lose sleep worrying about my sleep score.

Optimization has completed the circle.

This is why personal metrics need the same humility as organizational ones.

The metric exists for the purpose.

The purpose does not exist for the metric.

I don't walk so that my watch can display 10,000.

I track 10,000 because I want walking to be part of an active life.

I don't read so that an app can preserve my streak.

I use the streak because I want to read.

I don't manage my food so that a calorie tracker can turn green.

I track because I'm trying to improve my health.

The direction matters.

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment.**

Even here.

Especially here.

Start with what matters.

Use measurement to help you see it.

Then use judgment to decide what to do.

Sometimes judgment will say:

**You're 400 steps short. Go for the walk.**

Sometimes it will say:

**You're exhausted. Go to bed.**

The dashboard cannot know which one is right.

It knows the number.

And that's where we run into the next problem.

The personal dashboard can tell me an extraordinary amount about my life.

It just doesn't know much about what my life is actually like.

## The Dashboard Doesn't Know the Context

My watch knows how many steps I took yesterday.

It does not know why.

That's a fairly important limitation.

Suppose I normally walk 8,000 steps a day, but yesterday I only walked 4,200.

The number is accurate.

The target is clear.

Performance is down almost 50 percent.

If this were a corporate dashboard, someone might already be preparing a slide.

But perhaps yesterday a friend was going through something difficult and I spent three hours sitting across from him, listening.

My watch doesn't know that.

It just knows I sat there.

For three hours.

Frankly, from a fitness-tracker perspective, friendship is terribly inefficient.

Or maybe I usually complete eight tasks in a day, but today I completed two.

My productivity dashboard reports a significant decline.

What it cannot see is that I spent most of the day thinking deeply about a difficult problem.

Maybe that thinking prevented my team from spending the next three months building the wrong thing.

Two tasks completed.

Terrible day.

Or perhaps an unusually good one.

The dashboard can't tell.

This is the same problem we encountered with organizational dashboards.

Every measurement is an abstraction.

It takes a complicated reality and selects some part of it that can be counted.

That isn't a flaw.

It's what makes measurement useful.

My watch would be considerably less convenient if, every time I checked my step count, it displayed a complete account of everything that had happened in my life that day.

I just wanted to know whether I should go for a walk.

Compression is the point.

But compression always leaves something out.

And when the dashboard belongs to us, it can be surprisingly easy to forget what disappeared.

Take sleep.

A tracker might tell me I slept six hours and forty-two minutes.

Useful information.

But what does that number mean?

Maybe I stayed up too late scrolling through my phone.

Maybe I was caring for someone who was sick.

Maybe I was traveling.

Maybe I woke early because I was excited about something.

Maybe I had an important conversation that lasted until midnight.

Maybe I simply couldn't sleep.

The number is the same.

The life surrounding it is completely different.

The same thing happens with money.

A budgeting app can tell me I spent considerably more this month than last month.

That's worth noticing.

But maybe I became careless.

Or maybe the refrigerator died.

Maybe I took my family on a trip we'd been saving for.

Maybe I helped someone.

Maybe I paid an annual bill.

The spending number cannot distinguish between irresponsibility and generosity.

It cannot distinguish between impulse and intention.

It can tell me what happened financially.

It needs me to tell it what happened humanly.

This distinction becomes especially important when we use personal metrics to evaluate ourselves.

Because numbers have an extraordinary ability to strip away context while retaining authority.

**Screen time: up 23 percent.**

Bad.

Except perhaps I spent that time talking with my son.

**Workouts: down 30 percent.**

Bad.

Except perhaps I was injured and resting was exactly the right choice.

**Productivity: down.**

Bad.

Except perhaps I spent the week helping a struggling colleague.

**Weight: up two pounds.**

Bad.

Except perhaps almost nothing meaningful changed at all.

The number knows what the number knows.

Nothing more.

We've said that several times in this book because apparently I need to keep hearing it.

The strange thing about personal dashboards is that I possess the missing context.

At work, the executive looking at the dashboard may genuinely not know what happened on the ground.

They have to go find out.

But this is my life.

I was there.

I know why I only slept six hours.

I know why I spent the money.

I know why I missed the workout.

I know what I ate yesterday.

I know whether those three hours sitting still were wasted or deeply important.

And yet the number can still overrule me.

That's the authority of measurement again.

The dashboard looks objective.

My explanation feels subjective.

The watch has data.

I have a story.

And we've been trained to be suspicious of stories.

Sometimes rightly so.

Stories can become excuses.

I can explain almost anything if I'm sufficiently motivated.

I didn't skip the workout. I was practicing recovery.

I didn't overspend. I was investing in experiences.

I didn't procrastinate. I was allowing the problem to incubate.

At some point my inner manager may need to respond:

**Michael, you watched television for four hours.**

Fair enough.

This is why the answer cannot simply be, **Trust how you feel.**

We are capable of fooling ourselves.

That's one reason we started measuring in the first place.

But the opposite answer—**Trust the number**—isn't sufficient either.

The number has limitations too.

The tension is unavoidable.

The data can challenge my story.

My experience can contextualize the data.

I need both.

This is exactly what we asked managers to do with organizational dashboards.

Don't ignore the number.

Don't worship it.

Investigate.

Move between abstraction and reality.

Ask what the metric sees that you don't.

Ask what you see that the metric doesn't.

Then make a judgment.

Apparently I have to extend myself the same courtesy.

There is another reason this matters.

Some of the most important parts of a good life are almost invisible to personal dashboards.

Sitting with someone who is hurting.

Playing with your children.

Thinking.

Resting.

Listening.

Being present.

Doing something badly because you're learning it.

Taking a long dinner with people you love.

Walking slowly because you're not trying to get anywhere.

Doing absolutely nothing because absolutely nothing is what you need.

The dashboard may have very little to say about these things.

Or worse, it may classify them incorrectly.

The hour spent talking with a friend becomes inactivity.

The afternoon staring out the window while thinking becomes unproductive time.

The meal shared around a table becomes calories.

The vacation becomes reduced exercise consistency.

The late-night conversation becomes poor sleep hygiene.

The dashboard isn't wrong.

That's what makes this tricky.

Those things really do have measurable dimensions.

The meal contained calories.

The conversation cost sleep.

The afternoon contained very few completed tasks.

The watch accurately recorded that I barely moved.

But accuracy does not create completeness.

A measurement can be entirely correct about the part of reality it sees and completely silent about everything else.

This is where I have to remember something that was easier to see when we were talking about managers and employees.

**The dashboard is not the world.**

And my personal dashboard is not my life.

It's a view into my life.

Sometimes an extremely useful one.

It can reveal patterns I would otherwise miss.

It can challenge stories I tell myself.

It can warn me when my habits are drifting away from what I say matters.

But it cannot decide what matters.

That's still my job.

**My life provides the context. The number provides information. Judgment has to hold them together.**

And if I forget that, something subtle begins to happen.

I stop using numbers to understand my life.

I start experiencing my life through the numbers.

The walk becomes steps.

The workout becomes calories.

The book becomes a streak.

The night becomes a sleep score.

The day becomes a productivity total.

And eventually I have to ask a slightly ridiculous question:

Who exactly am I performing for?

## Who Exactly Am I Performing For?

There is something exhausting about having this many opportunities to succeed.

Or fail.

I can wake up in the morning and immediately discover whether I slept correctly.

Then I can weigh myself and find out whether yesterday's eating was acceptable.

My watch can tell me whether I've exercised enough.

My phone can tell me I've looked at my phone too much.

My budgeting app can tell me whether I've spent too much.

My task manager can tell me how many things I haven't done.

My reading app can remind me that I'm behind on my annual goal.

My meditation app can inform me that inner peace is currently on a four-day streak.

It's a lot of feedback for someone who has only been awake for twenty minutes.

No single one of these measurements is necessarily a problem.

That's what makes the cumulative effect easy to miss.

A step goal can be useful.

A sleep tracker can be useful.

A budget can be useful.

A food log can be useful.

A task list can be useful.

But stack enough useful measurements together and life can begin to feel like one long performance review.

Did I move enough?

Did I eat correctly?

Did I sleep enough?

Did I work hard enough?

Did I save enough?

Did I read enough?

Did I spend too much time on my phone?

Did I meditate today?

Did I drink enough water?

Did I remember to relax?

That last one is particularly important.

I need to make sure I'm relaxing efficiently.

This is optimization's strange destination.

We begin with things we genuinely care about.

Health.

Learning.

Financial security.

Meaningful work.

Rest.

Spiritual practice.

These are good things.

Then we create measurements to help us pursue them.

The measurements create targets.

The targets create expectations.

And before long, even the things that were supposed to make life richer can become things we're trying to perform correctly.

Exercise stops being moving my body and becomes completing the workout.

Reading stops being getting lost in a book and becomes finishing twenty-four books this year.

Meditation stops being sitting in silence and becomes maintaining a streak.

A walk stops being a walk.

It's 3,742 steps.

We don't necessarily lose the original experience.

I can enjoy a walk and collect the steps.

I can love a book and count it toward my annual goal.

But measurement adds another layer.

Some part of me is now watching.

That's an odd thing to do to yourself.

The experience is happening, and simultaneously I'm standing slightly outside the experience evaluating it.

How far?

How long?

How many?

How fast?

Does this count?

Did I hit the target?

Was today better than yesterday?

There are times when that observer is incredibly useful.

The observer notices patterns.

The observer creates discipline.

The observer remembers what I said I wanted when the rest of me would rather stay on the couch.

But the observer can also become a terrible companion.

Imagine going for a beautiful walk on a cool morning.

The air feels good.

The world is quiet.

You're not in a hurry.

Then you look at your watch.

Pace is down.

Now there is apparently a performance issue.

Nothing about the walk changed.

But your relationship to the walk did.

The experience acquired a score.

This is what measurement can do when it spreads across enough of life.

It subtly turns living into performing.

Which raises an obvious question.

**Who exactly am I performing for?**

My watch?

My spreadsheet?

The app?

Yesterday's version of myself?

Some imaginary panel of judges evaluating whether I have successfully optimized the human experience?

Nobody else may even know these numbers.

Nobody cares that I walked 8,413 steps instead of 10,000.

Nobody is going to call a meeting.

There will be no formal corrective action.

And yet I can still experience the miss.

That's because the audience has moved inside.

I have learned how to evaluate myself.

We've spent much of this book talking about organizational culture.

Culture teaches people what matters around here.

After enough repetition, nobody has to explicitly enforce every expectation.

People learn the rules.

Personal measurement can create a tiny culture of its own.

A culture of one.

What matters around here?

Exercise matters.

Good.

Saving matters.

Good.

Learning matters.

Good.

But something else can sneak in.

**Improvement matters.**

Not merely doing something valuable.

Improving it.

More.

Faster.

Better.

Higher.

Lower.

Depending, of course, on which direction the number is supposed to go.

Yesterday becomes the benchmark.

Last month becomes the comparison.

Last year becomes the baseline.

And suddenly maintaining something good can feel strangely unsatisfying.

The number didn't improve.

This is one of the hidden assumptions of optimization culture: there should always be another improvement available.

Another percentage point.

Another personal best.

Another habit.

Another system.

Another version of myself waiting to be unlocked.

There is nothing wrong with growth.

I want to grow.

There are parts of my life I very much want to change.

But growth and perpetual optimization are not the same thing.

Some things should improve.

Some things should be maintained.

Some things should be enjoyed.

Some things should simply be lived.

A dinner with someone I love does not need a performance indicator.

A hobby does not have to become a skill-development program.

A walk does not need to set a personal record.

Rest does not need to demonstrate return on investment.

There are moments when **enough** is a perfectly legitimate result.

That's difficult to remember in a culture built around more.

More productive.

More fit.

More efficient.

More disciplined.

More optimized.

Number go up.

The danger isn't that we have goals.

The danger is that the machinery of measurement can make every part of life feel like it needs one.

Then the dashboard that was supposed to help us live becomes another thing we have to satisfy.

Maybe the healthiest relationship with a metric is one in which I can occasionally disappoint it.

The watch says I didn't close my rings.

Okay.

The reading streak ended.

Okay.

The productivity total is low.

Maybe there's a reason.

Maybe there isn't.

I can look at the information without turning every number into a verdict.

Because the point of the dashboard was never to become good at dashboards.

The point was to help me live well.

That puts the hierarchy back where it belongs.

My life is the reality.

The numbers are information about it.

And sometimes the most human thing I can do is stop performing long enough to remember that I'm the one who hired them.

## Keep the Data, Lose the Boss

So what do we do with all of this?

Throw away the scale?

Delete the apps?

Take off the watch?

Return to a simpler time when we had no idea how many steps we took and judged the quality of our sleep by the primitive method of asking whether we felt tired?

No.

I like the data.

I want the data.

If I'm trying to lose weight, I want to know what I weigh.

If I'm trying to become more active, I want to know how much I'm moving.

If I'm trying to save money, I want to know where my money is going.

If I'm trying to improve my sleep, information about my sleep might help.

We've already established why measurement is so powerful.

It gives reality a way to talk back.

That's especially valuable when we're measuring ourselves because we are remarkably talented at constructing explanations for our own behavior.

The number can interrupt the story.

I think I've been eating reasonably.

Let's look.

I think I've been exercising regularly.

Let's look.

I think we're not spending that much eating out.

Let's definitely look.

The answer is not less reality.

It's a better relationship with the information reality gives us.

That means putting the number back in its proper place.

**My life is the reality. The number is information about it.**

That's the hierarchy.

The number doesn't get to become the boss.

It doesn't get to decide whether yesterday was a good day.

It doesn't get to decide whether the walk mattered.

It doesn't get to decide whether the conversation was worth missing sleep for.

It doesn't get to decide whether I'm succeeding as a human being.

It gets to contribute information.

That's plenty of responsibility for a number.

This sounds obvious when stated plainly.

But personal measurement has a way of quietly reversing the relationship.

I start with a goal.

I choose a metric to help me pursue it.

Then I begin serving the metric.

I walk because the watch wants steps.

I read because the app wants a streak.

I exercise because the ring isn't closed.

I feel guilty because the dashboard says I should.

The servant becomes the boss.

This is the same reversal we've been examining throughout the book.

The solution is not to fire the servant.

It's to remember who's in charge.

That changes the questions I ask.

Instead of:

**Did I hit the number?**

I can ask:

**What is the number telling me?**

Instead of:

**Why did I fail?**

I can ask:

**What happened?**

Instead of:

**How do I make this number better?**

I can ask:

**What am I actually trying to improve?**

Those questions sound softer.

They aren't.

In some ways they're more demanding.

It's easy to obey a number.

Numbers simplify decisions.

Under 2,000 calories: good.

Ten thousand steps: good.

Eight hours of sleep: good.

Budget met: good.

Target missed: bad.

Done.

Judgment is harder.

Judgment requires context.

It requires honesty.

It requires the ability to distinguish a legitimate explanation from an excuse.

It requires me to hold both the data and my experience without automatically allowing either one to win.

Sometimes the number needs to challenge me.

If I say I want to become more active and my step count has been declining for six months, that's worth confronting.

If I say I want to save money and my spending keeps increasing, the spreadsheet may be telling me something I don't particularly want to hear.

If I say I want to lose weight and the long-term trend keeps moving upward, I probably shouldn't respond by explaining that weight is merely an imperfect proxy.

Imperfect proxies can still contain important information.

That's why we use them.

The point isn't to explain away every bad number.

The point is to interpret it.

Other times, judgment needs to overrule the dashboard.

I need rest more than I need the workout.

I need the conversation more than I need the sleep score.

I need to enjoy the vacation more than I need perfect consistency.

I need to sit quietly more than I need to complete another task.

A healthy measurement system has to allow the metric to lose.

We said this about organizations.

It is just as important when the organization consists entirely of me.

If the number can never lose, it isn't information anymore.

It's authority.

And maybe that's the simplest way to think about personal measurement.

**Keep the data. Lose the boss.**

Let the scale measure.

Let the watch count.

Let the spreadsheet calculate.

Let the app record.

Then take the information and make a human judgment about what it means.

The device doesn't know what kind of day I had.

I do.

The device doesn't know what matters most to me.

I do.

The device doesn't know what I'm trying to become.

That is a question no sensor can answer.

This restores something important to the relationship.

Curiosity.

A number becomes much more useful when I can look at it without immediately turning it into praise or punishment.

Interesting.

Weight is up.

Why?

Interesting.

I've been sleeping less.

What's going on?

Interesting.

Spending has increased.

Where?

Interesting.

I'm moving less than I was three months ago.

What changed?

Now the dashboard is doing what a good dashboard should do.

It's creating questions.

It's sending me toward reality.

And reality sends me back to the dashboard with better questions.

The same loop we wanted at work applies at home.

The strange thing is that once we stop treating numbers as judgments, we may actually become better at using them.

I don't need to hide from the scale if the scale isn't a courtroom.

I don't need to manipulate the metric if the metric isn't my identity.

I don't need to explain every disappointing result if disappointing results are allowed to contain information.

I can look.

I can learn.

I can adjust.

That's what measurement was supposed to help me do in the first place.

But this raises another question.

If I'm going to measure myself—and I am—what should I measure?

Because not all personal metrics are equally useful.

Some numbers describe outcomes I care deeply about but can influence only indirectly.

Other numbers sit much closer to the choices I actually make.

Weight is an obvious example.

I care about my weight.

I can influence my weight.

But I cannot directly control what the scale says tomorrow morning.

There is no activity called **lose one pound** that I can schedule for Thursday afternoon.

What I can do is eat.

Walk.

Exercise.

Sleep.

Choose portions.

Choose foods.

Repeat those choices over time.

Those actions belong to me.

The outcome emerges from them, imperfectly and sometimes frustratingly.

That distinction matters.

Because if I'm going to hold myself accountable for something, perhaps I should be careful about choosing something I can actually do.

Maybe the best place to measure myself isn't at the end of the process.

Maybe it's closer to the place where I have agency.

And that is where we'll go next.
