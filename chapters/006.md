# 6 — You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I can schedule a walk for seven o'clock tomorrow morning. I can decide what I am going to eat for breakfast, put a workout on the calendar, and turn off the television early enough to give myself a reasonable chance of sleeping well. What I cannot put on the calendar is *lose one pound by Thursday*. I can do things that influence my weight, sometimes strongly, but I cannot instruct my body to display a particular number on a particular morning.

The distinction seems obvious until you notice how much of adult life is organized as though it weren't. We set goals to lose fifty pounds, get promoted, retire with a certain amount of money, or deliver a complicated project by June. These are perfectly reasonable things to want, and sometimes they give our efforts a useful direction. But none of them is an action we can simply perform.

This creates a peculiar problem for people who have become good at mastery. We have spent years learning that disciplined action changes reality, and it does. We plan, execute, observe, adjust, and frequently get something close to what we intended. Enough success can make the boundary between influence and control surprisingly difficult to see.

Then life refuses to cooperate. We follow the plan and the number moves the wrong way, or make a questionable decision and somehow get the result we wanted anyway. A careful project encounters a problem nobody anticipated, while another project with several obvious risks slips through them all and ships on time. The feedback loop still exists, but the connection between action and outcome is no longer as clean as it was when we were optimizing a piece of software.

This does not mean our choices are meaningless. Quite the opposite. Our choices shape what becomes likely, create conditions in which certain outcomes become possible, and sometimes dramatically alter the direction of our lives. The distinction I am after is smaller and, I think, more useful.

**Agency is real, but control is partial.**

### Lose One Pound on Thursday

Weight loss makes the distinction almost comically clear because the outcome is visible and the actions are ordinary. If I want to lose weight, there are plenty of things I can do today that make that outcome more likely. I can decide what I eat, go for a walk, exercise, prepare food ahead of time, and arrange my environment so the choices I want to make are easier to make. Those are actions because I can actually do them.

“Lose weight” is different. I cannot wake up tomorrow and spend thirty minutes losing weight in the same way I can spend thirty minutes walking. Weight loss is something that emerges from my behavior interacting with a living body over time. My choices matter enormously, but they do not give me direct access to the display on tomorrow morning's scale.

This is precisely what makes the morning performance review from the previous chapter so unreliable. Suppose I intended to eat a certain way yesterday, followed the plan, went for my walk, and did everything I had decided was mine to do. This morning the scale is up two pounds. If I interpret those two pounds as evidence that yesterday's decisions were bad, I have asked an outcome to answer a question it cannot answer very well.

The reverse is just as dangerous. I can spend a weekend eating far beyond what I intended, barely move, and step onto the scale Monday morning to discover that I weigh less. The number is not endorsing my weekend strategy. It is simply reporting the weight of a complicated biological system at that particular moment.

Over time, of course, the outcome matters. If months pass and my weight never moves in the direction I hoped, I should probably reconsider what I am doing. The scale can tell me whether the larger strategy appears to be working. What it cannot reliably tell me is whether I made a good decision at dinner last night.

Those are different questions, and they require different kinds of feedback.

This is where self-management can become unnecessarily cruel. If I planned to walk and chose not to walk, there is something useful to examine because the gap occurred near a place where I had agency. What happened? Was I tired, distracted, overcommitted, or simply unwilling to do what I had planned? Those questions might actually help me tomorrow.

But if I walked exactly as planned and the scale went up, interrogating myself more aggressively does not create additional control. It only converts uncertainty into blame.

### The World Gets a Vote Too

The same distinction appears at work, although corporate planning sometimes makes it harder to see. We put dates beside future outcomes because organizations need commitments, and commitments are useful partly because they force people to coordinate their actions. Eventually somebody has to say when the product is likely to ship. “Whenever reality feels ready” is not a particularly useful entry on a project plan.

But an estimate is still a statement about a future we do not control completely. A team can carefully examine the work, identify known dependencies, consider previous experience, account for risk, and arrive at a date everyone believes is reasonable. Then hardware arrives late, a requirement changes, an intermittent failure consumes three weeks, or an assumption that looked sensible during design turns out to be wrong.

The missed date tells us something. Perhaps the estimate was too optimistic, perhaps the team failed to identify a risk it should have seen, or perhaps the organization has a pattern of making commitments before it understands the work. We should investigate those possibilities rather than waving away every missed schedule as bad luck.

But sometimes the original decision was reasonable and the outcome was simply disappointing.

That possibility is surprisingly difficult to preserve in organizations because we usually know the outcome when we evaluate the decision. Once a project has failed, the warning signs that should have made failure obvious become much easier to identify. Once a project succeeds, the risks everyone took begin looking like evidence of bold leadership. History has a way of making uncertainty disappear after the fact.

The same thing happens in smaller ways throughout life. A good decision can produce a bad result, and a bad decision can get lucky. If we judge the quality of every choice entirely by what happened next, luck begins masquerading as wisdom and uncertainty starts looking like incompetence.

Outcomes matter because eventually our decisions have to work in the world. A company cannot remain in business by explaining that every failed product was based on excellent reasoning, just as I cannot pursue a health strategy indefinitely if my health continues getting worse. Results provide feedback about whether our understanding is good enough.

They just don't tell the whole causal story.

The world gets a vote too.

### Other People Get a Vote

Control becomes even more limited when the outcome we want depends on another person. I can prepare carefully for a difficult conversation, choose my words thoughtfully, listen rather than merely waiting to speak, and try to understand what the other person is telling me. I can apologize when an apology is needed and change my behavior when change is mine to make. What I cannot do is determine the other person's response.

This is easy to forget because relationships invite the same formula-seeking instinct as everything else. If I can just explain myself correctly, perhaps they will understand. If I say the right thing, perhaps they will forgive me. If I become sufficiently patient, supportive, persuasive, or wise, perhaps the relationship will arrive at the outcome I want.

Sometimes our actions do change the relationship, which is why effort matters. A sincere apology can open a door that defensiveness kept closed, and years of trustworthy behavior can repair things that words alone cannot repair. But another person is not the final variable in a formula I am entitled to solve. Their interior life remains their own.

Leadership works the same way. A manager can create clarity, remove obstacles, recognize good work, provide feedback, and build conditions in which people are more likely to succeed. Those actions matter, sometimes enormously. But the manager cannot reach inside another person and install motivation, agreement, creativity, or trust.

There is something relieving about accepting this, even though it also limits the fantasy of control. If I am responsible for producing another person's response, every interaction becomes a performance whose success depends upon getting the result I wanted. If I am responsible for what I bring to the interaction, I can take my part seriously while allowing the other person to have a part as well.

Participation requires accepting that the other participant gets a vote.

### Measure Where You Have Agency

This gives us a different way to think about personal measurement. Outcomes tell us whether the larger system is moving in the direction we hope, while measures closer to our behavior tell us whether we are doing the things we intended to do. We need both because they answer different questions.

If I am trying to improve my health, body weight may be an important outcome measure. I want to know whether the overall approach is working, and six months of disciplined effort with no meaningful improvement deserves investigation. But today's weight tells me relatively little about whether I made good choices yesterday.

For that question, I need to move closer to where I actually had a choice.

Did I go for the walk I intended to take? What happened around dinner? When evening arrived and I wanted something else to eat, what was I feeling and what did I do? If I didn't follow the plan, what made the choice difficult?

Those questions are much more interesting to me than *Why can't you get your act together?* They are also more demanding in a useful way. They require me to look at what actually happened rather than using a disappointing outcome as a general-purpose explanation of my character.

The same principle works at work. If a project slips, we need to know that it slipped. But if we want to understand whether a particular person acted responsibly, we have to move closer to the decisions that person could actually make. Did they communicate the risk when they discovered it? Did they ask for help when they needed it? Did they make a reasonable decision with the information available at the time?

A useful principle, then, is to **measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency**.

This is not a trick for making ourselves feel successful regardless of what happens. Outcomes eventually have to inform the process. If the scale never changes, the product never ships, or the relationship continues deteriorating, faithfully repeating the same behavior while congratulating ourselves on excellent process is not enough. The result may be telling us that our theory needs revision.

The distinction simply gives each kind of measurement the right job. Outcomes help us evaluate the strategy. Actions help us examine our choices. Confusing the two creates either helplessness—*nothing I do matters*—or an exaggerated sense of control—*everything that happened must have been caused by something I did.*

Neither is true.

I have influence without omnipotence, responsibility without total control.

### The Temptation of a Better Formula

There is an obvious response to all of this, particularly for someone with an engineering mind. If outcomes are noisy, perhaps I should stop focusing on them and build a better system around the inputs. Instead of obsessing over weight, I can track calories, protein, steps, workouts, sleep, water, meal timing, hunger, stress, and whatever else the latest application is prepared to place on a graph.

This can be useful. A few well-chosen behaviors can reveal patterns and give us something concrete to work with when a distant goal feels overwhelming. If I discover that my difficult eating almost always happens late at night when I am tired, that is considerably more actionable than staring unhappily at the scale the following morning.

But it is also possible to replace one demanding manager with a committee.

Instead of having one number available to disappoint me before breakfast, I can have seventeen.

The deeper temptation is not really the number of metrics. It is the hope underneath them: if I can identify the correct formula and execute it faithfully enough, perhaps I can guarantee the result. Engineering has trained me to love this possibility because in many domains that is exactly what good engineering tries to accomplish. Identify the important variables, reduce uncertainty, design the system well, and make its behavior increasingly predictable.

Human life contains many places where this works beautifully. Good habits change probabilities, careful preparation prevents avoidable problems, and thoughtful planning gives us far better odds than simply hoping everything works out. The lesson of this chapter is not that plans are illusions or outcomes are random.

It is that I am never the entire system.

My body has processes I do not consciously operate. Projects encounter circumstances nobody predicted. Other people bring histories, desires, fears, and choices that remain outside my control. I act inside a world already full of causes.

That does not make agency smaller. In some ways it makes agency clearer because I no longer have to claim responsibility for everything in order to take responsibility for something. I can choose, prepare, practice, plan, communicate, repair, adjust, and begin again. What I do genuinely changes what becomes possible.

Mastery taught me that my actions have consequences, and I would be foolish to abandon that lesson.

Life was beginning to teach me its companion:

**I am not the only cause.**
