# 6 - The Quantified Self

### We Have Dashboards for Ourselves

We are a numbers-obsessed culture, which is admittedly a strange complaint for someone who has spent most of his career as an engineer. Numbers are kind of our thing. Give us a complicated system and enough time, and eventually someone will create a dashboard for it.

What has changed is that measurement no longer stays at work. Somewhere along the way it escaped the laboratory, the factory, and the quarterly business review and followed us home, where it eventually climbed onto our wrists. Modern technology has given ordinary people access to a level of personal measurement that would have been difficult to imagine not very long ago.

We can track our weight, steps, heart rate, exercise, spending, screen time, and sleep. Sleep has become particularly impressive because it is apparently no longer sufficient to wake up and decide whether we feel rested. A device strapped to our wrist can estimate how much time we spent in various stages of sleep and assign the whole experience a score, allowing us to wake up feeling perfectly refreshed, look at our phone, and discover that we actually slept poorly.

It is a strange way to begin the morning.

The measurements have also become games. We have daily step goals, rings to close, streaks to maintain, badges to earn, and notifications that celebrate our achievements with the enthusiasm of someone who has just watched us qualify for the Olympics. This can lead to peculiar behavior, such as walking around the living room at eleven forty-five at night because going to bed with an incomplete circle suddenly feels like a small act of personal surrender.

None of this is inherently bad. Gamification works precisely because many worthwhile behaviors have delayed rewards. I can exercise today and receive almost none of the long-term health benefit today, but my watch can give me a little celebration immediately. If that celebration persuades me to get off the couch and walk another thousand steps, the technology has done something useful.

It also reveals something about how readily we respond to numbers. A vague aspiration like wanting to be healthier can be difficult to translate into action, whereas walking ten thousand steps gives me something concrete to do. Wanting to learn more is broad and somewhat intimidating; reading thirty books this year fits comfortably into a spreadsheet and provides the satisfying experience of watching the total advance from twenty-six to twenty-seven.

The number gives shape to the aspiration, and there is real value in that. It creates feedback, makes progress visible, and can reveal patterns that intuition alone might miss. This is why I don't want to turn the quantified self into another villain in a book that has already spent quite a bit of time making dashboards look suspicious.

The technology is remarkable, and the information can be genuinely useful because human beings are not especially reliable measurement instruments when the subject is ourselves. Ask me how I've been eating lately and I can consult a sophisticated scientific instrument known as *how I feel like I've been doing*. The result will often be something reassuringly imprecise like “pretty good.”

Then I track my food for a while and discover that “pretty good” apparently contained more calories than I remembered.

The numbers didn't create those calories. They made them visible, which is one of the reasons measurement can be so valuable. We remember the salad and forget the handful of chips, remember the long walk on Saturday more vividly than the five weekdays when we barely moved, and remember the month when we saved a lot of money without quite integrating the six months when we didn't.

Usually we aren't deliberately deceiving ourselves. We're approximating, and our approximations have a way of being generous.

Data can also see across time in ways memory cannot. Slow changes are especially difficult to notice because we adapt to them as they happen. A pound appears, then another; spending creeps upward; a habit that once seemed firmly established gradually disappears. Each individual change is small enough to accommodate, until eventually the accumulated change is much larger than anything we consciously decided to do.

A trend line can interrupt that process. It can show that I'm moving less than I think, spending more than I realized, or making progress during a period when nothing seems to be changing. This matters because many worthwhile changes are slow enough to be almost invisible from one day to the next. Exercise for a week and the mirror may have very little to report, while months of data can reveal a pattern that daily experience cannot.

So I want the information. If I'm trying to improve my health, finances, or habits, I would rather know what is actually happening than rely entirely on whatever story feels plausible at the moment.

The interesting problem begins after the information arrives.

At work, a corporation might track revenue, schedule, quality, and customer satisfaction. At home, I have weight, steps, sleep, savings, exercise, and screen time. The corporation has quarterly targets while I have daily ones, and the corporation periodically reviews its performance while I can review mine before I've had breakfast.

For the first part of this book, we've been looking at what happens when organizations measure people. Now we can remove the organization entirely. There is no executive imposing the metric, no manager setting the target, and no HR department deciding what counts as good performance. We buy the devices, install the apps, choose the goals, and decide which numbers deserve our attention.

Then, having constructed the entire measurement system ourselves, we proceed to live underneath it.

### The Scale Gives Me My Performance Review

I am trying to lose weight, and not a trivial amount of it, which makes the scale an obvious tool. If losing weight is one of my goals, it seems reasonable that I should occasionally find out how much I weigh. Refusing to look at the number would not make me enlightened; it would mostly make me less informed.

So I step on the scale.

If the number has gone down, I am immediately encouraged. Apparently I am a disciplined and responsible person whose thoughtful choices are finally producing the results they deserve. Perhaps I have figured this out. Perhaps this is the beginning of a new chapter in my life, and if the trend continues for another forty-eight hours I may be qualified to write a book about weight loss.

Then I step on the scale the following morning and the number has gone up.

The psychological transformation is impressive. Yesterday's emerging expert in personal health is suddenly wondering whether the diet works, whether his body works, and whether science itself may need to be reconsidered. Pants with elastic waistbands begin to look less like a temporary accommodation and more like a sensible long-term strategy.

This is an extraordinarily efficient performance-review system. I step on, wait a few seconds, and receive a precise numerical assessment without requiring a manager, a meeting, or even one of those awkward forms where everyone pretends that “development opportunity” doesn't mean what it obviously means.

The precision adds to the effect. The scale doesn't tell me that Michael appears to be making reasonable progress while still having several areas requiring attention. It says 247.6. There is even a decimal point, which gives the whole interaction an air of scientific seriousness.

The difficulty is that my body has not agreed to the performance-management system I've constructed for it. I would prefer a straightforward arrangement in which I eat well, exercise, and then lose weight in an orderly fashion, preferably on a schedule that allows me to update the spreadsheet every Friday. Bodies are less cooperative.

Weight fluctuates for all sorts of ordinary reasons. Water moves around, food moves through the digestive system, salt and carbohydrates matter, exercise can affect water retention, and even the time of day can move the number. Plenty of things can change my weight from one morning to the next without telling me very much about whether I gained or lost body fat during the previous twenty-four hours.

The scale doesn't know any of this, but in fairness, the scale isn't pretending that it does. Its job is much simpler: it tells me how much I weigh at that particular moment. It does not know whether I exercised yesterday, whether I made thoughtful food choices, whether I stopped eating when I was full, or whether I spent part of the evening heroically resisting something involving melted cheese.

All of the additional meaning comes from me.

This can create some wonderfully absurd situations. I can have a day in which I eat reasonably, exercise, and make choices that are completely aligned with what I'm trying to accomplish, only to wake up the next morning weighing more. Emotionally, the day can feel like a failure even though almost everything I actually controlled went well.

The reverse can happen just as easily. I can eat far more than I intended, barely move, and make choices that clearly aren't helping me build the habits I want. The next morning the scale is down a pound, which is delightful news if I am willing to accept the intriguing possibility that pizza was part of the plan all along.

If I managed an employee this way, I would hope someone eventually intervened. Imagine an employee follows the process, exercises good judgment, and does excellent work, only to have some noisy outcome metric move in the wrong direction. If I responded by saying I was disappointed in their performance, they would reasonably point out that they had done the right things.

We have spent several chapters exploring why this is a poor way to manage people. Then I walk into my bathroom and do it to myself before the sleep leaves my eyes.

What makes the situation psychologically interesting is the speed with which information becomes evaluation. The scale reports that my weight increased 1.4 pounds, but what I experience is something closer to *yesterday didn't work*. Those statements are related, but they are not equivalent, and the second one can have consequences the first one does not.

If I conclude that yesterday didn't work, I may become discouraged, restrict food more aggressively, decide the plan is ineffective, or abandon it altogether. A favorable fluctuation can teach the opposite lesson by making poor choices appear successful. In either case, the scale may be functioning perfectly; the questionable part is the amount of authority I have given a measurement of today's weight to evaluate yesterday's behavior.

This is the same problem we've encountered in organizations when a proxy acquires the authority of the thing it represents. Weight matters to me, and the long-term trend contains information I genuinely want. But there is no activity called *lose one pound* that I can place on my calendar for Thursday afternoon. I can choose what to eat, whether to exercise, and how consistently to follow the habits I'm trying to build, while my body produces the outcome on a timetable that I do not directly control.

We'll return to that distinction in the next chapter. For now, what interests me is that nobody imposed this arrangement on me. Nobody from corporate wellness is standing outside the bathroom with a clipboard. I bought the scale, chose the goal, decided when to measure, and supplied almost all of the meaning attached to the result.

Then I stood there feeling judged by it.

### I Am the Manager and the Employee

At work, we've been able to distinguish between the person doing the measuring and the person being measured. The manager looks at a number because they want information about what is happening, while the employee experiences the same number in the context of expectations, consequences, and authority.

At home, I get to occupy both roles.

I step onto the scale as the manager, looking for objective information. How are we doing? Is the strategy working? Are we making progress? Three seconds later I step off as the employee, wondering why management seems disappointed in me again.

There is something almost impressive about the efficiency of this arrangement. Most companies require an entire organizational structure to produce this much performance anxiety, while I can generate it by myself before coffee.

The same pattern can emerge with almost any kind of self-measurement. A fitness tracker begins as a way to understand how much I move, and then the daily goal gradually acquires the character of an expectation. Eventually I find myself walking around the house late at night because the ring isn't closed.

To be clear, walking around the house is still walking. If the tracker persuades me to move more, the metric has accomplished something useful. But something subtle has happened to the objective along the way. I began with a desire to become more active, while at eleven forty-five at night I may be pursuing the somewhat different goal of making a circle on a screen complete itself.

The distinction becomes clearer when the metric is farther removed from the thing I actually care about. Suppose I decide that reading is good and set a goal of fifty books for the year. The goal encourages me to read more, which may be exactly what I needed.

But now I am in December with forty-six books finished, looking at two books on the nightstand. One is a substantial book I've wanted to read for years. The other is short.

Suddenly page count has acquired an unexpected strategic importance.

Nothing forces me to choose the shorter book, and finishing fifty books may still represent a year in which I read more, learned more, and enjoyed reading enormously. But the measurement has introduced a second objective alongside the first. I wanted to become more widely read; now I also want the number to say fifty.

This is essentially the personal version of the organizational behavior we've already seen. Evidence that something is improving can become easier to optimize than the thing we originally hoped to improve. The difficulty is not that the evidence and the underlying goal are always opposed—they usually overlap, which is why the measurement was useful in the first place—but that they can occasionally separate without announcing that they have done so.

At work, someone else may apply pressure through a dashboard or target. With self-measurement, I can internalize the entire arrangement. I establish the expectation, watch the result, compare myself with the target, and provide my own running commentary about how management feels.

The remarkable thing is how much commentary a silent bathroom scale can generate.

The scale itself has never told me I should have exercised yesterday, that I shouldn't have eaten something, or that I was doing better last month. Those sentences come from somewhere else. The number supplies information, and I build an interpretation around it.

This becomes particularly important when the rest of life intrudes on the measurement system. Suppose I plan to take a fast five-mile walk one afternoon and run into a friend who needs to talk. We walk slowly for two miles instead.

From the perspective of my fitness plan, this may be a disappointing result. I covered less distance, spent less time at higher intensity, and burned fewer calories than I intended. From the perspective of the friendship, however, the afternoon may have gone exactly as it needed to.

That doesn't mean friendship should become a standing excuse for abandoning every workout plan. It simply means the fitness tracker does not contain enough information to evaluate the afternoon as a whole. It knows about movement, while I know something about the friend, the conversation, my health goals, and what seemed important in that particular moment.

The number contributes information to that judgment. It cannot make the judgment for me.

When I forget that distinction, self-measurement begins to feel less like having better information and more like living under supervision.

### Who Exactly Am I Performing For?

After tracking yourself for long enough, it is possible to develop a slightly peculiar relationship with recorded activity. A walk feels more legitimate because the watch recorded it, a workout feels more successful because an app gave it a favorable score, and reading feels productive because the annual total moved from twenty-six books to twenty-seven.

Forget to wear the watch during an hour of exercise and the rational part of my brain knows perfectly well that the exercise still happened. My cardiovascular system does not require confirmation from a cloud service before accepting the benefits. Yet the unrecorded workout can feel strangely incomplete, as though an event that produced no data somehow occurred less fully.

Modern life gives us an extraordinary number of ways to produce evidence that we are doing well. We can demonstrate that we exercised, slept, read, saved money, meditated, focused, reduced screen time, and maintained whatever streak currently deserves our loyalty. We can compare this week with last week and ourselves with strangers who apparently wake at four-thirty every morning to exercise, meditate, journal, read Marcus Aurelius, and prepare overnight oats before I have located my glasses.

Eventually it is worth asking who exactly all this evidence is for.

There is no quarterly review coming for my step count, and no board of directors is going to examine my meditation streak and conclude that I'm ready for increased responsibility. I created most of these reports myself, which makes me both the producer and the primary audience.

That doesn't make the reports useless. It does make me wonder what sort of relationship I want with them.

Optimization is deeply attractive, particularly to someone with an engineering temperament, because it offers a familiar way of dealing with complexity. Measure the system, identify the bottleneck, change something, and measure again. In engineering, this can work spectacularly well, so it is understandable that we eventually turn the instruments toward ourselves and wonder whether life might respond to the same method.

There is always something available for improvement. A better morning routine, better sleep, more exercise, more savings, less screen time, more reading, a longer meditation streak. Somewhere out there, apparently, is an optimal version of me, and perhaps if I collect enough data I will eventually locate him.

The difficulty is that a human life does not have a single optimization function.

Health matters, but so does friendship. Saving money matters, but so does enjoying some of what I've earned. Exercise matters, but so does rest. Reading matters, but it isn't obvious that fifty books necessarily represents a better year than forty. Even good things compete with other good things, and the wise allocation between them changes with circumstances.

A perfectly optimized morning routine may be an excellent way to start most mornings and a terrible way to spend the morning when someone you love needs you.

This is where perpetual improvement can become exhausting. A dashboard has no natural reason to tell us that the current result is sufficient, because if a number can move, the software can always show us another target. More steps, better sleep, more savings, more books, lower weight, higher productivity. There is always another increment available.

The decision that something is enough has to come from outside the metric.

That does not mean every current result should be accepted. There are areas of my own life that I very much want to change, and pretending that every number is fine would simply replace measurement obsession with another kind of self-deception. Sometimes the information is telling me that something genuinely needs attention.

But not every dimension of life needs to be maximized. Some things need improvement, some need maintenance, and some deserve intense attention for a season before becoming less important later. There are also experiences that become strangely diminished when we insist on converting them into evidence of progress.

A meal with friends doesn't need to prove anything. A walk can occasionally be slow. Playing music does not become more meaningful because I played more notes, and rest does not always need to defend its existence by demonstrating that it will make me more productive tomorrow.

The fact that something can be measured creates opportunities to learn and improve. It does not, by itself, create an obligation to optimize it indefinitely.

Our dashboards are unlikely to make that distinction for us, so we have to.

### Keep the Data, Lose the Boss

After all of this, I still want the scale.

I want fitness data, financial statements, spending histories, and other forms of measurement that can expose the distance between what I think I'm doing and what I'm actually doing. If I'm gaining weight while reassuring myself that everything is fine, I would rather have the scale disagree with me. If I'm spending much more than I realize or moving considerably less than I imagine, knowing that seems preferable to remaining comfortably mistaken.

The answer to an unhealthy relationship with data cannot simply be less reality.

What I want to change is the role the information plays after it arrives. A number can tell me what happened without immediately becoming a judgment about what kind of person I am or whether the entire plan is succeeding. That creates room for a question that is both simpler and, I think, more useful: what happened?

Maybe my weight went up because my behavior changed in ways I need to address. Maybe it went up because bodies fluctuate and nothing particularly meaningful happened. Maybe my activity dropped because I got lazy, or maybe it dropped because I spent the afternoon walking slowly with someone who needed to talk. Depending on the situation, the wise response could be more discipline, a change in strategy, or absolutely nothing.

The number cannot decide among those possibilities because the information required to do so exists outside the number. I have to bring in context, patterns over time, experience, and judgment.

That does not give me permission to invent whichever explanation feels nicest. Judgment can become self-deception just as easily as measurement can become tyranny, and one reason I want the data in the first place is that sometimes it will tell me something I would rather not hear. If my weight has been rising steadily for months, repeatedly explaining each individual increase as water fluctuation eventually becomes a rather creative use of context.

A healthier relationship with measurement has room for both possibilities. Sometimes the number reveals something that needs to change, while at other times the surrounding reality shows why the number deserves less authority than I initially gave it. Curiosity gives me a way to investigate that difference without having to choose between ignoring the data and putting myself on trial.

I can ask what happened, what the measurement actually tells me, what it might be missing, whether I'm looking at a meaningful pattern or ordinary noise, and whether there is anything I should do differently. Those questions keep the information connected to reality without requiring every fluctuation to become a verdict.

The scale can then return to being an instrument. The watch can tell me something about my activity without becoming a tiny middle manager attached to my wrist, and the spreadsheet can reveal a trend without requiring my life to organize itself around making the cells turn green.

This restores the relationship I wanted when I started measuring in the first place. My life does not exist to provide evidence for the dashboard; the dashboard exists to provide information about my life. The distinction sounds obvious when stated plainly, but it becomes surprisingly easy to lose once the numbers begin moving.

So I intend to keep the data. I need information that can challenge me, reveal patterns, and occasionally demonstrate that a reassuring story I've been telling myself is nonsense. But I don't need to turn every measurement into a performance review.

And that raises the question we'll take into the next chapter. If the outcome can be noisy, delayed, and partly outside my control, perhaps I should be more careful about which numbers I use to judge the choices I make today.
