# Chapter 6 — The Quantified Self

## We Have Dashboards for Ourselves

We are a numbers-obsessed culture.

I realize this is a strange complaint from someone who has spent his career as an engineer.

Numbers are kind of our thing.

But somewhere along the way, measurement escaped the laboratory, the factory, and the quarterly business review and followed us home.

Then it climbed onto our wrists.

Modern technology has given us an extraordinary ability to measure ourselves.

We can track our weight.

Our steps.

Our calories.

Our heart rate.

Our resting heart rate.

Our heart-rate variability.

Our sleep.

Not merely how long we slept, either. That would apparently be far too simple.

We can know how much time we spent in light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep. We can receive a sleep score in the morning informing us how successfully we performed the unconscious activity of being asleep.

You can wake up feeling perfectly refreshed, look at your phone, and discover that you slept poorly.

Good morning.

We can measure how far we run, how fast we run, our pace per mile, elevation gain, cadence, heart-rate zones, estimated calories burned, and how today's run compares with one we did seventeen months ago.

We can track how much water we drink.

How long we meditate.

How many books we read.

How many consecutive days we've studied Spanish.

How much time we spend looking at our phones, which we learn by looking at our phones.

We can track our spending, savings, investment returns, net worth, credit score, productivity, focus time, screen time, work time, downtime, and probably the amount of time we spend tracking all the other things.

Technology has made enormous leaps in what we can measure.

Sensors became smaller.

Computing became cheaper.

Storage became nearly invisible.

Our phones became permanent companions.

Wearable devices can collect information continuously without requiring us to do much of anything.

Things that once would have required specialized equipment can now be measured while we're standing in line for coffee.

This is genuinely remarkable.

But something else happened at the same time.

The measurements became scores.

And the scores became games.

Ten thousand steps.

Close your rings.

Maintain your streak.

Beat yesterday.

Reach the next level.

Hit your goal.

Earn a badge.

Congratulations.

Confetti.

Your watch is proud of you.

Personal technology didn't simply give us more information about ourselves. It learned something that every video game designer already knew:

Human beings really like making numbers go up.

Give me a number and I might look at it.

Give me a target and I'll try to hit it.

Give me a progress bar and I will experience a strange psychological need to fill it.

Give me a streak and suddenly missing Tuesday feels like destroying something I've spent forty-seven days building.

Nothing physically exists.

There is no streak.

The universe has not been carefully recording my daily language lessons on a stone tablet.

There is a number on a screen.

And yet I don't want to break it.

That's the power of gamification.

It takes behaviors we believe are good for us and gives them immediate feedback.

Exercise today, earn the points today.

Read today, preserve the streak today.

Walk another eight hundred steps, close the ring today.

The actual benefits of many healthy behaviors may take months or years to appear.

The little digital celebration arrives immediately.

That's clever.

And often helpful.

If a watch gets someone off the couch because they're 900 steps short of their goal, fantastic.

If a streak gets someone to practice a language every day, the streak has done something useful.

If watching a savings number increase encourages someone to spend less than they earn, number go up is doing its job.

This is not a chapter about throwing our watches into the ocean.

The interesting question is why all of this feels so natural to us.

Because the technology arrived inside a culture that was already primed for it.

We love optimization.

Optimization is one of the great promises of modern life.

There is always a better morning routine.

A better workout.

A better diet.

A better budgeting system.

A better productivity method.

A better way to sleep.

A better way to breathe.

A better way to organize our notes.

A better way to organize the notes about how we're organizing our notes.

Somewhere out there is apparently an optimal version of me, and if I can just collect enough data, perhaps I can finally locate him.

Optimization has become something close to a cultural god.

It promises that life can be improved if we can understand the variables.

Measure.

Analyze.

Adjust.

Repeat.

This works spectacularly well in some domains.

Engineers optimize systems because systems really can be optimized.

Change an algorithm and software gets faster.

Modify a manufacturing process and defects decrease.

Redesign a component and it uses less power.

Measure the result, change something, measure again.

It's intoxicating because it works.

Then we look at ourselves.

Surely the same principle applies.

So we turn the instruments inward.

We measure our bodies.

Our habits.

Our work.

Our money.

Our attention.

Our sleep.

Our leisure.

Sometimes even our spirituality.

And we start looking for opportunities to optimize.

There is something deeply appealing about this.

Numbers create a sense of control.

Life is complicated.

Numbers are clean.

**I want to be healthier** is vague.

**10,000 steps** is something I can do.

**I want to learn more** is vague.

**Read 30 books this year** gives me a target.

**I want to be financially secure** is terrifyingly broad.

**Increase savings by 10 percent** fits nicely into a spreadsheet.

The number gives shape to the aspiration.

It turns something fuzzy into something actionable.

And just as importantly, it tells us whether we're winning.

That's where the dashboard comes in.

We no longer simply have goals.

We have personal performance indicators.

The corporation has revenue, schedule, quality, and customer satisfaction.

I have weight, steps, sleep, savings, and screen time.

The corporation has quarterly targets.

I have daily ones.

The corporation has dashboards.

So do I.

The corporation reviews its performance.

I check mine before breakfast.

We've spent the first half of this book examining what happens when organizations measure human beings.

Now something interesting is about to happen.

We're going to remove the organization.

There is no executive.

No manager.

No HR department.

Nobody is forcing us to track anything.

We buy the devices ourselves.

We install the apps.

We choose the targets.

We create the dashboards.

And then we live underneath them.

We have brought the measurement system home.

## This Is Actually Pretty Amazing

After all of that, I should probably say something important.

This stuff is amazing.

Seriously.

I can make fun of my watch for grading my sleep, but the fact that a device strapped to my wrist can collect information about my body throughout the night is extraordinary.

I can step on a scale in the morning and get a reasonably accurate measurement of my weight in seconds.

I can go for a walk carrying a device that knows where I went, how far I traveled, how quickly I moved, and roughly how hard my body was working.

I can open an app and see years of financial history.

I can look at patterns in my spending.

I can see how often I exercise.

I can track what I eat.

I can compare what I intended to do with what I actually did.

For most of human history, ordinary people had access to almost none of this information.

Now we carry an astonishing collection of measurement instruments in our pockets.

And the reason we keep using them isn't simply because we're obsessed with numbers.

It's because the numbers can tell us things we need to know.

Human beings are not particularly reliable measurement instruments.

We remember selectively.

We estimate badly.

We notice dramatic events and forget ordinary ones.

We construct stories about ourselves that may be directionally correct and numerically ridiculous.

Ask me how much I've been eating lately and I can give you an answer based on a complicated scientific instrument known as **how I feel like I've been doing**.

I've been pretty good.

What does *pretty good* mean?

No idea.

But it feels reassuring.

Then I track my food.

Oh.

Apparently *pretty good* included more calories than I remembered.

The numbers didn't create the calories.

They made them visible.

This is one of the great gifts of measurement.

It gives reality a way to disagree with us.

We've encountered this before.

At work, a manager can believe a team is performing poorly until the data reveals otherwise. An engineer can believe a change made software faster until the benchmark says it didn't. A company can believe customers love a product until customer data suggests a more complicated story.

Personal measurement can do exactly the same thing.

We can believe we're active.

Then we count our steps.

We can believe we're sleeping enough.

Then we look at when we actually go to bed.

We can believe we're not spending very much on restaurants.

Then we categorize three months of transactions and discover that apparently we have been personally supporting the local food-service economy.

The number pushes back.

That's useful because self-deception doesn't always look like deception.

Usually we're not consciously lying to ourselves.

We're approximating.

We remember the salad.

We forget the handful of chips.

We remember Saturday's long walk.

We forget that we barely moved Monday through Friday.

We remember the month when we saved a lot of money.

We don't quite integrate the six months when we didn't.

Our minds are telling stories.

The data is keeping receipts.

Neither one necessarily has the complete picture.

But the receipts matter.

This is especially valuable when change happens slowly.

If I gained twenty pounds overnight, I would notice.

Among other things, I would have several questions.

But bodies don't usually work that way.

A pound appears.

Then another.

Weight fluctuates.

Clothes stretch.

We adjust.

The new normal becomes normal remarkably quickly.

Months later, we can find ourselves somewhere we never consciously decided to go.

Measurement interrupts that process.

A scale can say:

**Something is changing.**

A bank balance can say:

**This trajectory isn't sustainable.**

A fitness tracker can say:

**You're moving considerably less than you think.**

A screen-time report can say:

**You may want to sit down before looking at this.**

These aren't moral judgments.

They're information.

And sometimes information is exactly what we need.

Measurement also makes progress visible.

This matters because meaningful change is often painfully slow.

Exercise for one day and you probably won't look dramatically different in the mirror.

Save money for one week and retirement remains inconveniently far away.

Practice a musical instrument for twenty minutes and you may sound approximately as bad as you did twenty minutes earlier.

But accumulate the data and something becomes visible.

Three months.

Six months.

A year.

The trend appears.

That's motivating.

The number can preserve evidence of progress during periods when progress is difficult to feel.

This is one reason personal dashboards can be so powerful.

They extend our perception.

They help us see across time.

Memory gives us moments.

Data can give us patterns.

And patterns can help us make better decisions.

That's the positive case for the quantified self.

It isn't trivial.

It isn't foolish.

And it isn't something I want to dismiss.

If I'm trying to improve my health, I want information.

If I'm trying to improve my finances, I want information.

If I'm trying to understand my habits, I want information.

Intuition matters, but intuition without feedback can become a wonderfully sophisticated way of telling ourselves what we'd like to hear.

Numbers can challenge that.

They can introduce rigor where memory is fuzzy.

They can expose trends before those trends become crises.

They can tell us whether the thing we thought was working appears to be working.

They can help us learn.

So keep the scale.

Keep the watch.

Keep the spreadsheet.

Keep the app.

Measurement is a wonderful tool.

But something happens after the number appears.

I don't merely learn that I weigh a certain amount.

I react to it.

The number goes down.

I feel good.

The number goes up.

I feel bad.

Suddenly the scale isn't merely measuring my body.

It's evaluating my morning.

And that's where things start getting interesting.

## The Scale Gives Me My Performance Review

I am trying to lose weight.

A lot of weight.

This makes the scale an obvious tool.

If the goal is to lose weight, it seems reasonable to occasionally determine how much I weigh.

So I step on the scale.

The scale thinks for a moment.

Then it delivers the verdict.

**Number go down.**

Excellent.

I am a disciplined and responsible human being whose wise choices are finally producing the results they deserve.

Perhaps I have finally figured this out.

Perhaps this is the beginning of a new chapter in my life.

Perhaps I should write a book about weight loss.

Twenty-four hours later, I step on the scale again.

**Number go up.**

I am a failure.

Apparently everything is broken.

The diet doesn't work.

My body doesn't work.

Science may not work.

Yesterday I was a model of disciplined self-improvement. Today I am considering whether pants with elastic waistbands represent a more realistic long-term strategy.

This is the most brutally efficient performance review system ever invented.

Step on.

Wait three seconds.

Receive feedback.

No manager required.

And the scale has one particularly compelling feature.

It gives me a number.

Not a vague assessment.

Not:

**Michael appears to be making reasonable progress but should continue focusing on several development opportunities.**

No.

247.6.

Very precise.

It even includes a decimal point.

We've already established what decimal points do to the human mind.

They make things look serious.

The problem is that my body has not agreed to the performance-management system I've constructed for it.

I would like the arrangement to be simple.

Eat well.

Exercise.

Lose weight.

Repeat.

Preferably in that order and preferably on a schedule that allows me to update the spreadsheet every Friday.

Instead, weight fluctuates.

Water moves around.

Food moves through the digestive system.

Salt matters.

Carbohydrates matter.

Exercise can affect water retention.

The time of day matters.

A dozen things can move the number in either direction without telling me much about whether I lost body fat yesterday.

The scale doesn't know any of this.

To be fair, the scale isn't claiming to.

The scale has one job.

It measures how much I weigh at this particular moment.

That's it.

It does not display:

**Congratulations. You made thoughtful food choices yesterday.**

It does not know whether I exercised.

It does not know whether I ate vegetables.

It does not know whether I stopped eating when I was full.

It does not know whether I spent the evening fighting the desire to order something involving melted cheese.

It knows my weight.

**The number knows what the number knows. Nothing more.**

I am the one who adds the rest.

This produces some wonderfully absurd situations.

I can have an excellent day.

I eat reasonably.

I exercise.

I make choices completely aligned with what I'm trying to accomplish.

Then I wake up the next morning and weigh more.

And emotionally, it feels like failure.

Or I can have a terrible day.

I eat far more than I intended.

I barely move.

I make choices that clearly move me away from the habits I'm trying to build.

The next morning I step on the scale.

Down a pound.

Success!

Apparently the pizza was part of the plan.

If I were managing an employee this way, I hope someone would intervene.

Imagine an employee does everything we asked.

They follow the process.

They exercise good judgment.

They do excellent work.

Then some noisy outcome metric moves in the wrong direction and I say:

**I'm disappointed in your performance.**

The employee would reasonably respond:

**But I did the right things.**

And I would point solemnly at the dashboard.

**The number went up.**

We spent several chapters examining why that is a bad way to manage people.

Then I walk into my bathroom and do it to myself before breakfast.

This is where personal measurement becomes psychologically interesting.

The number starts as information.

But information can become evaluation remarkably quickly.

I don't simply observe:

**My weight increased 1.4 pounds since yesterday.**

I experience:

**Yesterday didn't work.**

Those are not the same statement.

One is measurement.

The other is interpretation.

And the interpretation can influence what I do next.

Maybe I get discouraged.

Maybe I restrict food more aggressively.

Maybe I decide the plan isn't working.

Maybe I abandon it entirely.

Or perhaps the number goes down and creates the opposite problem.

Maybe I loosen up.

Maybe I decide yesterday's choices were apparently fine.

Maybe I learn the wrong lesson from a favorable fluctuation.

The measurement hasn't failed.

The scale is probably working perfectly.

The problem is the authority I've given it.

I've asked a measurement of today's weight to evaluate yesterday's behavior.

Those things are related.

They are not identical.

This is the same mistake organizations make when they collapse a proxy into the thing it represents.

Weight matters to me.

I'm not going to solve the problem by declaring the scale an oppressive instrument of numerical tyranny and throwing it away.

If I want to lose a significant amount of weight, the long-term trend in my weight is useful information.

I want to know it.

But there is no activity called **lose one pound** that I can add to my calendar for Thursday afternoon.

I can't directly perform weight loss.

I can make choices.

My body produces outcomes.

Those outcomes happen on their own timetable.

That's an important distinction, and we'll come back to it.

For now, I'm interested in something stranger.

Nobody imposed this measurement system on me.

Nobody from corporate wellness is standing outside the bathroom with a clipboard.

I bought the scale.

I chose the goal.

I decided when to measure.

I decided what the number means.

And then I stood there feeling judged by it.

At work, we've been able to distinguish between two people.

The measurer creates the system.

The measuree lives underneath it.

Here, that distinction disappears.

I am both of them.

## I Am the Manager and the Employee

At work, the roles were relatively clear.

Someone creates a metric.

Someone else lives underneath it.

Management wants visibility.

Employees experience expectations.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences an incentive.

This distinction helped us understand why the same metric could look so different depending on which side of the dashboard you were standing.

Then I stepped on the scale.

Now there is only one person in the room.

Me.

I chose the metric.

I chose the target.

I bought the equipment.

I collect the data.

I review the results.

And when performance is disappointing, I know exactly where to find the employee responsible.

This is remarkably efficient.

Most companies require an entire management structure to create this much anxiety.

I can do it by myself before breakfast.

I am the manager.

I am the employee.

And apparently neither of us is particularly pleasant to work with.

Think about how strange this is.

Manager Me establishes the objective:

**Lose weight.**

Employee Me agrees.

Seems reasonable.

Manager Me creates the measurement system.

We'll weigh regularly.

We'll track progress.

We'll establish targets.

We'll look for trends.

Excellent.

Then the numbers start coming in.

Employee Me has several good days.

Manager Me is pleased.

**Great work. Keep it up.**

Then the scale jumps two pounds.

Manager Me would like a word.

Employee Me attempts to provide context.

**Weight fluctuates. I ate well yesterday. I exercised. This is probably water.**

Manager Me has reviewed the dashboard.

**I hear what you're saying. But the number went up.**

We have seen this meeting before.

The only difference is that now both people are in my head.

This is one of the peculiar consequences of self-measurement.

We can internalize the entire organizational system.

Targets.

Performance reviews.

Rewards.

Disappointment.

Comparison.

Optimization.

Even surveillance.

Except now surveillance is remarkably convenient because we voluntarily strap the monitoring equipment to our own bodies.

My watch doesn't need permission to check whether I'm moving.

I paid for it to do that.

Then it reminds me that I've been sitting too long.

Thank you, tiny middle manager attached to my wrist.

Again, this can be useful.

Sometimes I really should stand up.

The interesting part is how quickly an external measurement can become an internal voice.

The device doesn't actually care.

The scale isn't disappointed.

My watch isn't concerned about my career trajectory.

The calorie-tracking app does not lie awake at night wondering whether I'm reaching my potential.

They're displaying information.

**I supply the judgment.**

That's important.

Because it's tempting to blame the technology.

We say the watch makes us anxious.

The scale makes us feel bad.

The productivity app makes us feel behind.

Sometimes technology is deliberately designed to create psychological pressure. Gamification works precisely because designers understand motivation.

But the deeper mechanism doesn't require an app.

Give me a notebook and a pen and I can become my own micromanager.

I can create a checklist.

Establish targets.

Track streaks.

Assign scores.

Compare this week with last week.

Circle disappointing results.

Maybe add a little red ink if the system isn't sufficiently hostile.

The technology makes measurement easier.

The human mind supplies the meaning.

And because I am both sides of the relationship, something else happens.

There may be nobody around to challenge the metric.

In an organization, an employee can at least theoretically say:

**I don't think this number captures what happened.**

A good manager might listen.

A coworker might provide context.

A team might push back on a bad target.

There is another perspective in the system.

When I'm measuring myself, I can become a closed loop.

I create the standard.

I fail the standard.

I judge the failure.

Then I use the judgment as evidence that the standard was correct.

Nobody gets invited to the meeting who might ask:

**Is this actually helping?**

This is especially easy because self-discipline is usually considered a virtue.

We admire people who hold themselves accountable.

And we should.

There is enormous value in taking responsibility for our choices rather than constantly explaining them away.

If I consistently eat more than I intended, declaring that calories are merely a social construct is unlikely to move me toward my goals.

Reality matters.

Accountability matters.

The numbers can help.

But accountability and punishment are not the same thing.

Neither are rigor and hostility.

A good manager doesn't improve performance by treating every disappointing result as evidence of a defective employee.

Why would I expect that strategy to work better when the employee is me?

This is where the lessons from the workplace become unexpectedly personal.

Earlier we said a healthy manager should be able to look at a bad metric and ask:

**What happened?**

Not:

**Who failed?**

The same principle applies here.

The scale goes up.

What happened?

I missed my exercise target.

What happened?

I spent more than I planned.

What happened?

I broke the streak.

What happened?

That question creates information.

**What's wrong with me?** creates something else.

There is a difference between investigating our behavior and prosecuting ourselves for it.

One produces learning.

The other mostly produces a very unpleasant workplace with only one employee.

And I can't quit.

This doesn't mean lowering every standard until success is guaranteed.

Manager Me does not need to become the kind of manager who responds to six months of missed targets with:

**You're doing amazing. No notes.**

Sometimes I need to change my behavior.

Sometimes the data is telling me exactly what I don't want to hear.

Sometimes I made a commitment to myself and didn't keep it.

That deserves honesty.

But if the purpose of measurement is improvement, then the response to the number should serve improvement.

That's the standard.

Does this way of measuring myself help me learn?

Does it help me make better choices?

Does it help me notice patterns?

Does it help me become the person I'm trying to become?

Or have I created a personal bureaucracy whose primary function is documenting my inadequacy?

That's a question worth asking.

Because once I become both measurer and measuree, the relationship between those two roles matters.

I can become my own coach.

I can become my own analyst.

I can become my own teacher.

Or I can become the worst manager I've ever had.

And whichever one I choose, I'm taking them home with me.
