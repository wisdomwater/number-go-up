# 6 — You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I can schedule a walk for seven o'clock tomorrow morning. I can decide what I am going to eat for breakfast, put a workout on the calendar, and turn off the television early enough to get a reasonable amount of sleep. What I cannot put on the calendar is “lose one pound by Thursday.” I can do things that influence my weight, sometimes strongly, but I cannot instruct my body to display a particular number on a particular morning. The distinction seems obvious until you notice how much of adult life is organized as though it weren't.

We set goals for things we cannot directly do. Lose fifty pounds. Get promoted. Earn a certain amount of money. Sell ten thousand copies of a book, retire with three million dollars, or deliver a complicated project by June. Goals like these can provide direction, but none of them is an action we can perform.

This creates a peculiar problem for people who have become good at mastery. We have spent years learning that disciplined action changes reality, and it does. We plan, execute, measure, adjust, and frequently get something close to what we intended. Enough success can make the boundary between influence and control surprisingly difficult to see.

Then life refuses to cooperate. We follow the plan and the number moves the wrong way, or we make a mistake and somehow get the outcome we wanted anyway. Someone less qualified gets the promotion, a careful project encounters an unforeseeable problem, or a book we poured ourselves into disappears without attracting much attention. The feedback loop still exists, but the connection between action and outcome is no longer as clean as it was when we were optimizing a piece of software.

This does not mean our choices are meaningless. Quite the opposite. Our choices shape what becomes likely, create conditions in which certain outcomes become possible, and sometimes dramatically alter the direction of our lives. Agency is real, but control is partial.

## Lose One Pound on Thursday

Weight loss provides an almost comically clear example because the goal and the action are so easy to confuse. If I want to lose weight, there are plenty of things I can do today that make that outcome more likely. I can choose what I eat, decide whether to exercise, go for a walk, prepare food ahead of time, and arrange my environment so that the easier choices are generally the ones I intended to make. These are actions because I can actually perform them.

“Lose weight” is different. I cannot wake up tomorrow and spend twenty minutes losing weight in the same way I can spend twenty minutes walking. Weight loss is an outcome that emerges from many actions interacting with my body over time. I can influence the process substantially without being able to specify what the scale will say tomorrow morning.

The same distinction appears in less personal places. I can write a book, edit it carefully, choose a cover, publish it, talk about it, and make it easy for interested people to buy. I cannot perform the action “sell ten thousand copies,” because the final step requires thousands of other people to decide that they want the thing I made. Sales are an outcome produced by my actions interacting with a world that contains other actors.

Career goals work this way too. I can learn new skills, take difficult assignments, build relationships, communicate well, help my team, and make sure my work is visible. I cannot schedule a promotion for the third quarter. The decision depends on budgets, organizational needs, available positions, other candidates, my manager's judgment, and circumstances I may never know existed.

Yet our planning systems routinely place actions and outcomes beside one another as though they belonged to the same category. “Exercise three times a week” sits next to “lose twenty pounds,” while “complete the design” sits next to “ship in June.” Both kinds of goals can be useful, but they describe different relationships with reality. One tells me what I intend to do; the other tells me what I hope will happen.

This distinction matters because we tend to evaluate ourselves using both as though they were equally under our control. If I planned to walk and did not walk, there is a straightforward relationship between intention and behavior worth examining. If I walked exactly as planned and the scale moved up, the relationship is much less direct. Treating both results as equivalent evidence about my discipline confuses the part of the system I operate with the outcome produced by the system as a whole.

## The World Gets a Vote Too

The frustrating thing about outcomes is that they contain more causes than we do. My behavior influences my weight, but so do water, digestion, sodium, glycogen, inflammation, sleep, hormones, medication, and the ordinary fluctuations of a living body. Over a sufficiently long period, consistent behavior may create a clear trend. On any particular morning, the scale can behave like a coworker who attended a completely different meeting.

This is why short-term outcomes can be such poor judges of decisions. I can make a good decision and receive a bad result. I can make a terrible decision and get lucky. If I judge the quality of the decision entirely by what happened next, luck begins masquerading as wisdom.

Engineering provides plenty of examples. A team can make a thoughtful estimate based on everything it knows, then discover a dependency nobody realized existed. Hardware arrives late, a requirement changes, an intermittent failure consumes weeks, or an assumption that looked perfectly reasonable during design turns out to be wrong. The missed schedule may tell us something important without proving that the original estimate was irresponsible.

The reverse is equally important. A project can ship on time despite poor planning because several risks happened not to materialize. An investor can make a reckless bet and earn a large return. A person can ignore every health intention for a weekend and still step on the scale Monday morning to discover that the number went down.

Outcomes therefore contain information, but interpreting that information requires judgment. A disappointing result should make us curious about the process, just as a surprisingly good result should not automatically validate everything that preceded it. We can ask whether the plan was reasonable, whether we followed it, what circumstances intervened, and what the result teaches us for next time. The outcome gets a vote, but it does not get to rewrite history.

This can be difficult for people trained in environments where results carry enormous weight. Businesses cannot simply announce that everyone made excellent decisions while the company ran out of money, and a project that repeatedly misses commitments has a problem regardless of how thoughtful the explanations are. Results matter because eventually our actions have to work in the world. The challenge is learning from outcomes without pretending that every outcome is a clean measure of the choices that produced it.

## Other People Are Not Variables

Control becomes even more limited when the desired outcome depends on another person. I can prepare carefully for an interview, communicate my experience honestly, ask thoughtful questions, and present myself as well as I know how. Then someone else decides whom to hire. No amount of excellence on my side eliminates their agency on the other.

This sounds obvious in an interview because the decision-maker is visible. It becomes harder to remember in relationships, where we may feel that the right words ought to produce the response we hope for. I can apologize sincerely, acknowledge the harm I caused, and change my behavior. I cannot make another person forgive me.

Parents learn a particularly difficult version of this. They can love their children, establish boundaries, provide opportunities, model values, make sacrifices, and still watch those children grow into adults who make choices they would not have chosen. The alternative would be raising people without agency, which is not what most parents actually want. Love creates influence without granting ownership of the outcome.

Leadership has the same limitation. A manager can create conditions in which people are more likely to succeed, but cannot reach inside an employee and install motivation. A leader can explain a strategy clearly and still have someone disagree with it. The fact that another person's response matters to my goal does not turn that person into a variable I am entitled to control.

This is where some of our most cherished goals become especially complicated. I can work on my marriage, write something I believe matters, help a friend, raise a child, build a team, or offer an apology. In every case, what happens next depends partly on people whose interior lives remain their own. Participation requires accepting that the other participant gets a vote.

There is humility in that recognition, but also relief. If I am responsible for producing another person's response, every interaction becomes a performance whose success depends on obtaining the desired result. If I am responsible instead for what I bring to the interaction, I can take my responsibilities seriously without pretending I control the entire relationship. The outcome still matters, but it is shared territory.

## Measure Where You Have Agency

This distinction gives us a useful way to think about measurement. Outcome measures tell us whether the larger system is moving in the direction we hope, while measures closer to our actions tell us whether we are doing the things we intended to do. We need both because they answer different questions. The trouble comes when we use the first to judge the second without considering everything that happened between them.

If I am trying to improve my health, body weight may be an important outcome measure. I still need to know whether the overall approach is working because six months of disciplined behavior with no meaningful improvement deserves investigation. But today's weight tells me relatively little about whether I made good choices yesterday. For that question, I need to look closer to where I actually had agency.

The same applies at work. A team should care whether the product ships, whether customers can use it, and whether the software is reliable. Those outcomes are the reason the work exists. But if we want to understand why a particular engineer made a particular decision, aggregate product results may be several steps too far away.

A useful principle is to **measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency**. If I said I would walk today, did I walk? If I planned a certain way of eating, what happened when evening arrived? If I committed to doing careful work, communicating a risk, or having a difficult conversation, did I do the part that was mine?

This does not guarantee the desired outcome. I can do everything I intended today and still receive disappointing news tomorrow. But it gives me a more honest place from which to learn because I am examining behavior where choice was actually available. Responsibility becomes more precise rather than disappearing.

There is also a certain kindness in this precision, though kindness is not the same as letting ourselves off the hook. If I did not do what I intended, I can examine that directly without declaring my entire goal a failure. What happened, what made the behavior difficult, and what might I change next time? The question becomes practical rather than prosecutorial.

Over time, outcome and process still have to meet. If the scale never changes, the product never ships, the business never earns money, or the relationship continues deteriorating, faithfully executing the same process is not enough. Outcomes tell us when our theory needs revision. Agency tells us where we can begin revising it.

## The Limits of the Formula

There is an obvious temptation here, especially for someone with an engineering mind. If outcomes are noisy, then perhaps the solution is simply to construct a better system around the inputs. Instead of obsessing over weight, I can track calories, protein, steps, workouts, sleep, water, meal timing, hunger, stress, and whatever else the latest application is prepared to place on a graph. The outcome dashboard becomes a process dashboard.

This can be useful up to a point. Better information can reveal patterns, and a few well-chosen behaviors may give us something concrete to focus on when a distant goal feels overwhelming. But it is possible to replace one demanding manager with a committee. Instead of being judged by a single number, I now have seventeen opportunities to disappoint myself before breakfast.

The deeper issue is not which metric belongs on the dashboard. It is the dream underneath the dashboard: if I can identify the correct formula and execute it faithfully enough, perhaps I can guarantee the result. Engineering trains us to love this possibility because in many domains it is exactly what we are trying to achieve. Specify the conditions precisely enough, control the important variables, and make the system behave predictably.

Life contains many places where this works beautifully. Follow the recipe and the bread will probably resemble bread, maintain the car and it will probably last longer, save consistently and you will probably accumulate more money than if you spend everything. Reliable cause and effect is not an illusion. It is simply not the whole environment in which a human life unfolds.

Bodies change, markets move, companies reorganize, technologies become obsolete, people make choices, accidents happen, and opportunities arrive that nobody put on the roadmap. We influence these environments while simultaneously being influenced by them. The future emerges from our actions interacting with causes we control, causes we partially influence, and causes we never see coming.

Perhaps this is why a steering wheel is a better image than a blueprint. I can choose a direction, pay attention to the road, make corrections, and become a better driver. I cannot own the road, control every other driver, guarantee the weather, or know what will be around the next bend. The existence of uncertainty does not make steering pointless; it is the reason steering requires attention.

There is another consequence that will matter later in this book. If the future cannot be completely specified, then not every surprise is evidence that the plan failed. Sometimes the unexpected thing is worse than what we wanted, and sometimes it is better. A conversation takes an unplanned turn, an opportunity appears through a failure, or a relationship becomes important for reasons nobody could have scheduled.

For someone who has spent decades becoming competent, this can be uncomfortable. Competence teaches us to anticipate, prepare, reduce risk, and improve the odds, and those remain valuable abilities. But maturity may require another kind of competence alongside them: knowing where our agency ends without surrendering the agency we actually have. We can act seriously without imagining ourselves to be the only cause.

Mastery taught me that my actions have consequences. That lesson changed my life, and I would be foolish to abandon it. I can choose, practice, prepare, plan, influence, repair, and begin again, and what I do genuinely changes what becomes possible.

Life was beginning to teach me the companion lesson: **I am not the only cause.**
