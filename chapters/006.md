# Chapter 6

## The Quantified Self

### We Have Dashboards for Ourselves

We are a numbers-obsessed culture.

I realize this is a strange complaint from someone who has spent his career as an engineer. Numbers are kind of our thing. But somewhere along the way, measurement escaped the laboratory, the factory, and the quarterly business review and followed us home.

Then it climbed onto our wrists.

Modern technology has given us an extraordinary ability to measure ourselves. We can track our weight, steps, heart rate, sleep, spending, screen time, and exercise. We can even measure how much time we spend looking at our phones, which we learn by looking at our phones.

Sleep has become particularly impressive. It's no longer enough to wake up and decide whether we feel rested. A device strapped to our wrist can inform us how much time we spent in various stages of sleep and assign the whole experience a score. You can wake up feeling perfectly refreshed, look at your phone, and discover that you slept poorly.

Good morning.

The measurements also became games. Walk ten thousand steps. Close the ring. Maintain the streak. Hit the goal. Earn the badge. If necessary, walk around the living room at eleven forty-five at night because apparently going to bed with an incomplete circle would be an act of personal surrender.

None of this is inherently bad. Gamification works because it gives immediate feedback to behaviors whose real benefits may take much longer to appear. Exercise today and the health benefits may take months or years to accumulate, but your watch can celebrate immediately. If that little celebration gets someone off the couch for another thousand steps, wonderful.

It also reveals something about us: human beings really like making numbers go up.

Give me a vague aspiration and I may struggle with it. "I want to be healthier" is difficult to act on. Ten thousand steps gives me something concrete to do. "I want to learn more" is broad; reading thirty books this year fits nicely into a spreadsheet.

The number gives shape to the aspiration, and that can be useful. It creates feedback and lets us see patterns that intuition alone may miss.

This is one reason I don't want to make the quantified self into another villain. The technology is remarkable, and the information can be genuinely valuable. Human beings are not especially reliable measurement instruments when the subject is ourselves.

Ask me how I've been eating lately and I can consult a sophisticated scientific instrument known as *how I feel like I've been doing*.

Pretty good.

Then I track my food.

Oh.

Apparently "pretty good" contained more calories than I remembered.

The numbers didn't create the calories. They made them visible.

This is one of the great gifts of measurement: it gives reality a way to disagree with us. We remember the salad and forget the handful of chips. We remember Saturday's long walk more clearly than the five weekdays when we barely moved. Usually we're not deliberately lying to ourselves; we're approximating, and our approximations are generous.

Our minds tell stories. The data keeps receipts.

Data can also see across time in a way memory cannot. Slow changes are particularly difficult to notice because we adapt to them. A pound appears, then another. Spending creeps upward. A habit gradually disappears. Each individual change is small enough to become the new normal.

A trend line can make the pattern visible. Memory gives us moments; data can give us patterns, and patterns can help us make better decisions.

So the problem isn't that we've started measuring ourselves.

The interesting part is what we did next.

The corporation has revenue, schedule, quality, and customer satisfaction. I have weight, steps, sleep, savings, and screen time. The corporation has quarterly targets; I have daily ones. The corporation reviews its performance every few months.

I check mine before breakfast.

We've spent the first half of this book examining what happens when organizations measure human beings. Now we're going to remove the organization. There is no executive, no manager, no HR department, and nobody forcing us to track anything.

We buy the devices. We install the apps. We choose the targets. We create the dashboards.

And then we live underneath them.

**We have brought the measurement system home.**

### The Scale Gives Me My Performance Review

I am trying to lose weight.

A lot of weight.

This makes the scale an obvious tool. If the goal is to lose weight, it seems reasonable to occasionally determine how much I weigh.

So I step on the scale.

Number go down.

Excellent. I am a disciplined and responsible human being whose wise choices are finally producing the results they deserve. Perhaps I have figured this out. Perhaps this is the beginning of a new chapter in my life.

Perhaps I should write a book about weight loss.

Twenty-four hours later, I step on the scale again.

Number go up.

Apparently everything is broken. The diet doesn't work. My body doesn't work. Science itself may be in question. Yesterday I was a model of disciplined self-improvement; today I'm wondering whether pants with elastic waistbands represent a more realistic long-term strategy.

The scale is the most brutally efficient performance review system ever invented. Step on, wait three seconds, receive feedback. No manager required.

It even gives me a precise number.

Not, "Michael appears to be making reasonable progress but should continue focusing on several development opportunities."

No.

247.6.

There's a decimal point, so we know this is serious.

The difficulty is that my body has not agreed to the performance-management system I've constructed for it. I would prefer a straightforward arrangement in which I eat well, exercise, and lose weight, preferably in that order and on a schedule that allows me to update the spreadsheet every Friday.

Bodies are less cooperative.

Weight fluctuates. Water moves around. Food moves through the digestive system. What I ate yesterday matters, as does exercise and the time of day. Plenty of things can move the number without telling me very much about whether I gained or lost body fat during the previous twenty-four hours.

The scale doesn't know any of this, but to be fair, it isn't claiming to. The scale has one job: it measures how much I weigh at that particular moment.

It does not know whether I exercised yesterday. It doesn't know whether I ate thoughtfully, stopped eating when I was full, or spent the evening heroically resisting something involving melted cheese.

It knows my weight.

**The number knows what the number knows. Nothing more.**

I am the one who adds the rest.

That can produce wonderfully absurd situations. I can have an excellent day, eat reasonably, exercise, and make choices completely aligned with what I'm trying to accomplish. The next morning I weigh more, and emotionally the day feels like a failure.

Or I can have a terrible day. I eat far more than I intended, barely move, and make choices that clearly aren't helping me build the habits I want. The next morning I step on the scale and discover that I'm down a pound.

Success!

Apparently the pizza was part of the plan.

If I managed an employee this way, I would hope someone intervened. Imagine an employee does everything we asked. They follow the process, exercise good judgment, and do excellent work. Then some noisy outcome metric moves in the wrong direction and I announce that I'm disappointed in their performance.

They might reasonably point out that they did the right things.

I would point solemnly at the dashboard.

We have spent several chapters examining why that's a poor way to manage people.

Then I walk into my bathroom and do it to myself before breakfast.

This is where personal measurement becomes psychologically interesting. The number starts as information, but information can become evaluation remarkably quickly.

The scale reports that my weight increased 1.4 pounds.

I experience: *Yesterday didn't work.*

Those are not the same statement.

One is measurement. The other is interpretation, and that interpretation can affect what I do next. I might get discouraged, restrict food more aggressively, decide the plan isn't working, or abandon it altogether. A favorable fluctuation can create the opposite problem by teaching me that yesterday's choices were apparently fine.

The scale hasn't failed. It's probably working perfectly.

The problem is the authority I've given it.

I've asked a measurement of today's weight to evaluate yesterday's behavior. Those things are related, but they are not identical.

Weight still matters to me. I'm not going to solve this by declaring the scale an oppressive instrument of numerical tyranny and throwing it out the window. If I'm trying to lose a significant amount of weight, the long-term trend contains information I want.

But there is no activity called "lose one pound" that I can put on my calendar for Thursday afternoon.

I can make choices.

My body produces outcomes.

We'll return to that distinction in the next chapter. For now, something stranger interests me.

Nobody imposed this measurement system on me.

Nobody from corporate wellness is standing outside my bathroom with a clipboard. I bought the scale. I chose the goal. I decided when to measure and what the number would mean.

Then I stood there feeling judged by it.

### I Am the Manager and the Employee

At work, we've been able to distinguish between two perspectives. The measurer looks at information. The measuree experiences what that information means when it is attached to expectations, targets, and authority.

At home, I get to be both.

I step onto the scale as the manager. I want objective information. How are we doing? Are we making progress? Is the strategy working?

Three seconds later I step off as the employee.

Why are you looking at me like that?

There is something almost impressive about the efficiency of the arrangement. Most companies require an entire management structure to create this much anxiety. I can do it by myself before breakfast.

**I am the manager. I am the employee. And apparently neither of us is particularly pleasant to work with.**

The same thing can happen with almost any form of self-measurement. A fitness tracker begins as a way to understand how much I move. Then the daily goal becomes an expectation. Soon I find myself walking circles around the house because the ring isn't closed.

To be clear, walking around the house is still walking. If the tracker got me to move more, the metric accomplished something useful.

But notice the subtle change in the objective. I started with *I want to be more active*. At eleven forty-five at night, the objective may have become *I need the circle to close*.

The representation has become the task.

This is Goodhart's Law in sweatpants.

It becomes more interesting when the metric is farther removed from what we actually care about. Reading is good, so I set a goal of fifty books this year. The goal encourages me to read. Great.

Then I find myself choosing shorter books because they move the number faster.

Did I become more educated, curious, thoughtful, or wise?

Possibly.

I definitely finished fifty books.

The same question can creep into exercise, productivity, finances, and almost anything else we measure:

**Am I improving my life, or am I improving the evidence that I'm improving my life?**

Those aren't always in conflict. Often the evidence moves because the life really is improving. That's why we started measuring.

The danger comes when the evidence becomes easier to optimize than the underlying life.

At work, someone else may be applying pressure through a target or dashboard. With self-measurement, I don't need anyone else. I can internalize the whole system. I set the expectation, watch the result, compare myself with the target, and administer the consequences.

I can even supply the performance commentary.

You should have exercised.

You shouldn't have eaten that.

You're behind.

You were doing better last month.

A bathroom scale is remarkably quiet considering how much dialogue it can produce.

And that's worth noticing, because the scale itself isn't saying any of those things.

I am.

The same distinction applies when life refuses to cooperate with the dashboard. Suppose I planned a fast five-mile walk and instead ran into a friend who needed to talk. We walk slowly for two miles.

From the tracker's perspective, this is disappointing. Less distance. Lower intensity. Fewer calories.

From the perspective of friendship, the afternoon may have been considerably more valuable than the workout I planned.

Friendship can be terribly inefficient.

This isn't an argument for ignoring the workout plan every time someone wants to chat. It's simply a reminder that the dashboard cannot contain the context in which the number occurred.

My life provides that context. The number provides information. Judgment has to hold them together.

When those roles get reversed, self-measurement starts to feel less like information and more like supervision.

### Who Exactly Am I Performing For?

There is a strange moment that can occur after you've tracked yourself long enough.

You start performing for the tracker.

The walk feels more legitimate because it was recorded. The workout feels better because the watch gave you a score. Reading feels productive because it moved the annual count from twenty-six to twenty-seven.

Forget to wear the watch, and an hour of exercise can feel vaguely as if it didn't happen.

The rational part of my brain knows better. My cardiovascular system does not require a cloud service to acknowledge the workout before accepting the benefits.

And yet the recorded activity feels different.

The evidence matters.

Modern life gives us an extraordinary number of ways to produce evidence that we're doing well. We can demonstrate that we exercised, slept, read, saved, meditated, focused, and reduced screen time. We can compare today with yesterday, this month with last month, and ourselves with strangers who apparently wake at four-thirty every morning to exercise, meditate, journal, read Marcus Aurelius, and prepare overnight oats before I have located my glasses.

At some point, a reasonable question appears.

Who exactly am I performing for?

There is no quarterly review coming.

No board of directors is going to examine my step count.

Nobody is going to place my meditation streak in a talent discussion and conclude that I'm ready for increased responsibility.

I created the report, and I'm the primary audience.

That doesn't make the report useless. It does raise a question about what kind of relationship I want with it.

Optimization is deeply attractive because it promises that life can be improved if we can identify the variables. Engineers know the pleasure of this particularly well. Measure a system, find the bottleneck, change something, measure again.

It works.

Then we turn the instruments toward ourselves.

Somewhere out there is apparently an optimal version of me, and if I can collect enough data, perhaps I can finally locate him.

The trouble is that a human life doesn't have a single optimization function.

Health matters, but so does friendship. Saving matters, but so does enjoying some of what I've earned. Exercise matters, but so does rest. Reading matters, but there is no obvious reason why fifty books automatically represents a better life than forty.

Even good things compete with other good things.

A perfectly optimized morning routine can become a terrible way to spend a morning if someone you love needs you.

This is where the logic of perpetual improvement becomes exhausting. If a number can improve, why shouldn't it? More steps. Better sleep. More savings. More books. Longer streak. Lower weight. Higher productivity.

The number always contains another number.

There is no natural place where a dashboard says, "This is fine. Please go live your life."

We have to supply that judgment too.

**Enough is a perfectly legitimate result.**

That doesn't mean giving up on growth. There are areas of my life I very much want to change, including my health. Sometimes the current number isn't enough, and pretending otherwise would just be another way of hiding from reality.

But not every dimension of life needs to be maximized.

Some things need improvement. Some need maintenance. Some need attention for a season and then less attention later. And some of the most important parts of life become worse when we approach them as optimization problems.

A meal with friends does not need a performance score.

A walk doesn't always need a pace.

Music does not become more meaningful because I played more notes.

Rest does not have to justify itself by improving tomorrow's productivity.

The ability to measure something creates the possibility of improvement. It does not create an obligation to improve it forever.

That's a distinction our dashboards are unlikely to make for us.

### Keep the Data, Lose the Boss

After all this, I still want the scale.

I want the fitness data too. I want financial statements and spending histories. I want information that can expose the difference between what I think I'm doing and what I'm actually doing.

The answer to a bad relationship with data is not less reality.

If I'm gaining weight while telling myself I'm doing fine, I want the scale to disagree with me. If I'm spending far more than I think, I want the bank statement. If I believe I'm active but spend most of the day sitting, I'd rather know.

Measurement can be an antidote to self-deception.

What I don't need is for every piece of information to become a tiny manager living inside my head.

A number can tell me what happened without deciding what it means about me. That leaves room for a much healthier question.

Instead of *What's wrong with me?*, I can ask:

**What happened?**

Maybe my weight went up because my behavior changed. That's worth knowing. Maybe it went up because bodies fluctuate and nothing meaningful happened at all.

Maybe my activity dropped because I got lazy. Maybe it dropped because I spent the afternoon with someone who needed me. Maybe I need more discipline, or maybe the wise response to today's data is to change absolutely nothing.

The number alone cannot decide among those possibilities.

I supply the judgment.

That doesn't mean I get to invent whatever explanation makes me feel best. Judgment isn't permission to turn every bad number into a heartwarming story about context. The whole reason for keeping the data is that sometimes it tells me something I don't want to hear.

A healthy relationship with measurement has room for both possibilities. The number may reveal something I need to change. Or the context may reveal why the number deserves less authority than I initially gave it.

Curiosity gives us somewhere to stand between denial and prosecution.

What happened?

What does this number actually tell me?

What might it be missing?

Is there a pattern here, or am I reacting to noise?

Do I need to change something?

Those are questions I can work with.

They put the measurement back in its proper role. The scale becomes an instrument again instead of a judge. The watch becomes a source of information instead of a tiny middle manager attached to my wrist.

Most importantly, the hierarchy gets restored.

My life is not evidence submitted to a dashboard.

The dashboard is evidence about my life.

**My life is the reality. The number is information about it.**

That is enough reason to keep the data. Numbers can challenge me, surprise me, reveal patterns, and occasionally tell me that the story I've been telling myself is nonsense.

Good.

I need that.

They just don't need to conduct my performance review.

Keep the data.

Lose the boss.
