# Chapter 4 — You Get What You Measure

## Of Course We Want Behavior to Change

Let's give metrics some credit.

They can motivate people.

That's not a bug.

It's often the point.

We've spent a lot of time talking about what happens when people know they're being measured. They pay attention. They change their behavior. They begin making different decisions.

It's tempting to describe all of this as a problem. But imagine the alternative.

An organization decides software quality matters. It begins tracking defects. Engineers become more conscious of defects and start paying closer attention to testing.

Good.

A company realizes customers are waiting too long for support. It starts measuring response time. Support teams notice and begin looking for ways to respond faster.

Also good.

A development organization believes artificial intelligence could make certain kinds of work more efficient. It starts paying attention to whether employees are experimenting with AI tools. People who might otherwise have ignored the technology give it a try.

That might be exactly what leadership hoped would happen.

Measurement focuses attention. Attention can change behavior. Changed behavior can produce better outcomes.

This is not some unfortunate side effect we discovered after inventing the dashboard.

It's one of the reasons we built the dashboard.

Organizations want to improve. People want to improve. Improvement requires change.

We talk about having a growth mindset. We encourage continuous improvement. We look for inefficiencies and try to remove them. We identify weaknesses and work on them. We experiment with new tools, new processes, and new ways of working.

All of that requires behavior to change.

You cannot optimize a system while insisting that everyone continue doing exactly what they've always done.

Something has to move.

That's where measurement becomes powerful.

Suppose a team wants to reduce the amount of time it takes to release software. Before changing anything, it measures the current process.

Twenty-three days.

Then the team changes something.

Nineteen days.

Interesting.

Change something else.

Fourteen days.

Now the metric isn't merely describing the process. It's providing feedback.

Did that help?

Did that make things worse?

Are we moving in the direction we intended?

Without measurement, improvement can become surprisingly subjective.

“We're definitely faster now.”

Are we?

“It feels faster.”

Excellent. I'll update the quarterly report.

**Velocity: feels faster.**

Measurement gives us a way to challenge our impressions. It lets reality push back. And once people can see the feedback, it can become motivating.

Anyone who has watched a progress bar knows this.

You walk 8,000 steps. Your watch says you're close to your goal.

Fine.

You walk around the block.

The little circle closes.

Something inside your brain experiences an embarrassingly sincere sense of accomplishment because a tiny digital ring is now complete.

Ridiculous?

Maybe.

Effective?

Apparently.

Human beings respond to feedback. We respond to goals. We respond to visible progress. We like knowing whether our effort is producing results.

A useful metric can turn something vague like:

**We should improve quality**

into something more concrete:

**Defects have fallen 25 percent over the last six months.**

Now we can see movement.

We can ask what caused it. We can reinforce practices that seem to be working. We can recognize people who helped.

We can set another goal.

This is one reason metrics become so attractive in organizations. They don't merely describe improvement. They can participate in it.

And there is nothing inherently wrong with that.

Sometimes discussions about metrics become so focused on manipulation and gaming that we forget something important:

**Motivation is not the enemy.**

We want people to care about outcomes. We want teams to notice problems. We want organizations to learn. We want behavior to change when the current behavior isn't producing what we need.

If measuring something helps that happen, wonderful.

The question is not whether a metric influences behavior.

The question is:

**Which behavior does it influence?**

If measuring response time motivates a support team to remove unnecessary delays, the metric is doing useful work.

If it motivates support agents to rush customers off the phone before their problems are solved, something has gone wrong.

If measuring defects motivates engineers to improve testing, excellent.

If it motivates people to argue that a bug isn't technically a bug because classifying it as a bug would hurt the numbers, we have wandered into different territory.

If measuring AI adoption encourages people to experiment with tools they might otherwise have ignored, the metric may have helped overcome inertia.

If six months later employees are feeding pointless tasks into AI because everyone knows the adoption number needs to go up, we should probably ask a few questions.

In each case, the metric succeeded at motivating behavior. The problem wasn't that people responded to it.

**The problem was what they learned to do in order to make it move.**

This is where the conversation about measurement needs more nuance. A metric can be motivational without being manipulative.

A target can focus effort without becoming tyrannical.

A dashboard can encourage improvement without replacing judgment.

But none of this happens automatically. The metric has to remain connected to the outcome that gave it meaning in the first place.

That's the relationship we need to watch.

Because usually, when a metric is first introduced, there is a perfectly reasonable reason for choosing it. The behavior we're measuring really does seem connected to the outcome we want.

More testing really can improve quality.

Faster response really can improve customer experience.

Using a new tool really can produce new efficiencies.

The correlation isn't imaginary. In fact, that's precisely why we chose the metric.

And that's where things get interesting.

Because once we've found something that seems to produce a better outcome, corporate culture knows exactly what to do next.

We optimize it.

## The Correlation Was Real

Most bad metrics probably didn't begin as bad ideas.

Someone noticed something.

Teams that test their software more thoroughly tend to have fewer defects.

Salespeople who spend more time talking with customers tend to sell more.

Projects that receive regular code reviews tend to produce better software.

Employees who use a new tool effectively sometimes finish certain kinds of work faster.

These relationships can be real.

Hopefully, before an organization builds an entire performance system around one of them, someone has done more than notice a pattern over lunch.

There may be research.

There may be historical data.

There may be controlled experiments.

There may be years of operational experience showing that certain behaviors tend to produce certain outcomes.

This matters because it's easy to tell stories about ridiculous corporate metrics as though they were invented by someone who had recently suffered a head injury.

Sometimes that's probably unfair.

The original reasoning may have been quite good. Suppose an engineering organization studies its development process and discovers something interesting.

Teams that perform thorough code reviews have significantly fewer defects after release.

That makes sense.

Another engineer looking at the code may catch mistakes the original author missed. Review creates an opportunity to question assumptions. Knowledge spreads between engineers. Design problems surface earlier, when they're cheaper to fix.

So leadership says:

**We should make sure code gets reviewed.**

Excellent.

That's a behavior.

And if the evidence is strong enough, encouraging that behavior may be a very good idea.

Maybe the organization introduces a requirement that production code receive review before being merged.

Behavior changes.

More code gets reviewed.

Defects decline.

The intervention worked. This is measurement doing exactly what we want it to do.

We observed reality. We identified a behavior associated with a better outcome. We encouraged the behavior. The outcome improved.

Everyone may now enjoy a brief moment of organizational happiness.

Unfortunately, someone is eventually going to ask for a dashboard.

How do we know people are doing code reviews?

Good question.

We need a metric.

Perhaps we measure the percentage of changes that receive review.

Now we have a proxy for the behavior.

That's still useful.

If 97 percent of changes are being reviewed, we have some reason to believe the practice has become widespread.

If the number suddenly falls to 62 percent, we might want to investigate.

So far, so good.

Then comes the subtle transition.

We stop saying:

**Code review tends to improve software quality, so we want engineers to review important changes carefully.**

And begin saying:

**Code review compliance must remain above 95 percent.**

Those sentences sound almost identical inside an organization.

They are not.

The first statement is about behavior and purpose The second is about a number.

Once the number becomes the requirement, the optimization problem changes.

An engineer no longer needs to ask:

**Did this code receive a meaningful review?**

The system asks:

**Did this code receive a review that counts?**

Those can be very different questions.

Perhaps clicking “Approve” counts.

Perhaps a review lasting thirty seconds counts.

Perhaps asking someone to glance at a trivial change counts exactly as much as a thoughtful review of a complicated architectural decision.

The dashboard cannot necessarily tell the difference. It knows that a review occurred. And the organization asked for reviews. So the number looks fantastic.

This is the strange journey of a proxy.

It begins with something real.

**Thoughtful code review tends to improve software quality.**

Then we need a way to observe whether code review is happening. So we create a measurable representation.

**Percentage of changes reviewed.**

Then we want improvement.

So we create a target.

**95 percent.**

Then we want accountability.

So we attach consequences.

Now everyone knows the number matters.

At each step, the decision makes sense. That's what makes this problem interesting.

There doesn't have to be a villain.

Nobody has to announce:

**Today we shall replace meaningful engineering practice with meaningless bureaucracy.**

The transformation happens gradually. Behavior becomes measurement Measurement becomes target.

Target becomes requirement.

And somewhere along the way, the thing we can count begins acquiring more authority than the thing we actually wanted.

This is why correlation matters.

A good proxy should have some meaningful relationship to the reality it represents.

If there is no relationship, the metric is simply bad.

But even a very good relationship has limits.

Suppose we discover that engineers on healthy teams tend to review code carefully.

That does not necessarily mean forcing the review count upward will produce equally healthy teams.

The direction matters.

This is an old problem in reasoning.

People carrying umbrellas are more likely to be standing in the rain.

Handing out more umbrellas does not cause additional rain.

Corporate metrics are usually more complicated than umbrellas and rain, but the underlying caution remains useful.

A relationship between two things does not automatically tell us what will happen when we deliberately manipulate one of them.

Sometimes it works.

Sometimes it works spectacularly.

Sometimes it works for a while.

And sometimes people discover how to move the measurable thing without producing the outcome that originally made it valuable.

That's why I think there is an important difference between **encouraging a behavior** and **enforcing a number**.

If research tells us that a particular behavior reliably improves an outcome, encourage the behavior.

Teach it.

Make room for it.

Remove obstacles to it.

Explain why it matters.

Build it into the way work gets done when appropriate.

Hold people accountable for doing it when the stakes justify that.

But be careful about replacing the behavior with its numerical shadow.

Because the moment the shadow becomes the requirement, people have a new problem to solve.

They no longer have to figure out how to produce the outcome.

They have to figure out how to produce the number.

And these are smart people.

We hired them because they're good at solving problems.

They work inside organizations that constantly tell them to become more efficient.

Reduce waste.

Increase throughput.

Automate repetitive work.

Find the shortest path.

Optimize.

Then we give them a metric and say:

**This is how we'll know you're succeeding.**

We should not be shocked by what happens next.

They optimize it.

## Then We Discover Optimization

Of course they do.

What else did we expect?

Modern organizations spend an extraordinary amount of energy teaching people to optimize.

We optimize processes.

We optimize schedules.

We optimize supply chains.

We optimize software.

We optimize staffing.

We optimize costs.

We optimize customer funnels, cloud infrastructure, meeting schedules, inventory levels, advertising campaigns, manufacturing lines, and probably the location of the coffee machine.

Optimization is practically a corporate virtue.

Find the waste.

Remove unnecessary steps.

Automate repetitive work.

Do more with less.

Identify the bottleneck.

Find a better way.

These are not things we reluctantly tolerate from employees. They're things we put on performance reviews.

We want people who look at a process and say:

**There has to be a more efficient way to do this.**

Then we introduce a metric, tell everyone it matters, attach it to goals and performance conversations, and act surprised when those same people start looking for the most efficient way to make the metric move.

That's not strange behavior.

That's training.

Human beings have always adapted to the environments around them. We learn where food is available. We learn which paths are easier. We learn which behaviors produce rewards and which produce pain. We learn what the people around us expect.

A corporate environment is no different.

People learn.

If staying late gets praised, people stay late.

If finishing early gets rewarded with more work, people learn not to finish quite so visibly.

If promotions depend on individual accomplishments, people make sure their individual accomplishments are visible.

If missing a project commitment produces an unpleasant executive review, teams become very thoughtful about what they're willing to commit to.

If AI adoption is measured, employees find reasons to use AI.

This does not require anyone to be dishonest.

It requires them to be awake.

They are observing the environment and adjusting their behavior accordingly.

In another context, we might call that intelligence.

The problem is that organizations sometimes draw a moral distinction between the optimization they intended and the optimization they actually produced.

Consider project estimates.

Leadership wants predictable delivery.

Reasonable.

So teams estimate how long work will take, and management begins tracking whether those estimates are met.

Also reasonable.

Then missed commitments receive attention.

Nobody necessarily announces a punishment. Maybe there isn't one.

But missed dates appear in red.

Questions get asked.

Explanations are required.

Leaders become concerned.

Teams that consistently hit their dates look well managed.

Everyone notices.

What happens next?

People get better at estimating.

Hopefully.

But there's another way to improve the metric.

Estimate conservatively.

If you think something will take six weeks, ask for eight.

If there are significant unknowns, add some room.

If being early produces a pleasant conversation and being late produces an unpleasant one, there is no advanced degree in behavioral economics required to predict what happens next.

Eventually management may notice that estimates seem padded.

Now we're frustrated.

**Why is everyone sandbagging their estimates?**

Well.

We asked for predictability.

We measured predictability.

We rewarded predictability.

And people found a way to become more predictable.

The number improved.

This is where words like **gaming** can become misleading.

Sometimes people absolutely game systems dishonestly. They falsify data. They hide problems. They manipulate classifications. They deliberately misrepresent what happened.

That's different.

But much of what gets called gaming is simply adaptation.

An employee understands the rules and behaves rationally within them.

Imagine a customer support center that tracks average call time.

The original reasoning may be sound. Customers don't want to spend forty-five minutes resolving something that should take five. Long call times may indicate confusing processes, poor training, inadequate tools, or unnecessary bureaucracy.

So management tracks the number.

Agents notice.

Perhaps they're told that calls should average under eight minutes.

Most agents probably don't immediately begin plotting against the company.

They try to get faster.

They learn the software better.

They become more concise.

They find shortcuts.

Excellent.

The metric is working.

Then an unusual customer calls with a complicated problem.

Solving it properly will take twenty-five minutes.

Now the agent faces two goals.

**Help the customer.**

And:

**Keep the call short.**

For the first eight minutes, those goals may point in roughly the same direction.

Then they separate.

At that moment, the metric reveals what kind of incentive it has become.

Maybe the agent stays on the phone and solves the problem.

Maybe they transfer the customer.

Maybe they ask the customer to call another department.

Maybe they solve the immediate symptom instead of the underlying problem.

Maybe they start looking for ways to end the conversation.

Again, we don't need a bad person.

We just need competing incentives.

This is one reason metric problems are so difficult to solve through appeals to character.

We can tell employees:

**Don't optimize the metric. Do what's right.**

But that creates an interesting arrangement.

The organization creates the incentive.

The employee is responsible for resisting it.

We design the game, publish the score, attach consequences to winning, and then depend on everyone exercising sufficient moral wisdom to ignore the score whenever it points in the wrong direction.

That seems like a lot to ask of a dashboard.

And it seems like an unnecessary burden to place on people.

A better question is:

**What behavior have we made rational?**

That's different from asking what behavior we intended.

Nobody intended for support agents to rush customers.

Nobody intended for engineers to produce unnecessary code.

Nobody intended for teams to pad estimates.

Nobody intended for employees to use AI where it adds no value.

But intentions don't create incentives.

Systems do.

And people live inside systems.

Managers do too.

This is worth remembering because it's easy to tell this story as though management creates metrics and employees respond to them.

The hierarchy is usually messier than that.

Your manager has a metric.

Their manager has a metric.

Their organization has a target.

The division has a commitment.

The executive has promised something to th.e board.

The pressure travels downward, changing shape as it goes.

A director told to increase AI adoption creates an organizational goal.

A manager turns the organizational goal into a team expectation.

A team lead starts asking about AI usage.

An employee finds another task to run through an LLM.

Everyone may be behaving reasonably at their own level.

And the final result may still be absurd.

That's the danger of local rationality.

A behavior can make perfect sense from where a person is standing while contributing to an outcome almost nobody wanted.

The employee isn't necessarily broken.

The manager isn't necessarily foolish.

The executive isn't necessarily malicious.

Everyone may simply be responding to the incentives closest to them.

Which means that when a behavior appears across an organization, we should be cautious about immediately diagnosing a people problem.

Lazy.

Resistant.

Uncooperative.

Risk-averse.

Obsessed with visibility.

Unwilling to innovate.

Maybe.

People are capable of all sorts of things.

But if hundreds of otherwise reasonable people begin behaving in roughly the same strange way, there is another possibility worth investigating.

Maybe they learned something about the system.

Maybe the behavior makes sense inside the environment we've created.

Before asking:

**What's wrong with these people?**

Ask:

**What have we made rational?**

Because people optimize.

That's what we wanted.

The real challenge is making sure the shortest path to a better number still passes through the outcome we actually care about.

And the harder we push on the number, the more important that becomes.

## Correlation Meets Pressure

A metric doesn't usually break the moment someone starts optimizing it.

That's worth emphasizing.

If a company discovers that faster customer response tends to produce happier customers, encouraging employees to respond faster may actually produce happier customers.

If code review tends to reduce defects, increasing code review may reduce defects.

If engineers who learn to use AI effectively become more productive at certain tasks, encouraging more engineers to experiment with AI may produce real gains.

The relationship can hold.

For a while, perhaps it holds beautifully.

This is one reason metric-driven improvement can be so convincing. We introduce the measure, behavior changes, and the outcome improves.

Look.

It worked.

And maybe it did.

The interesting question is what happens next.

Organizations rarely respond to successful optimization by saying:

**Wonderful. That seems about right. Let's leave it there.**

We tend to ask for more.

If reducing average customer response time from twelve hours to six improved customer satisfaction, perhaps three hours would be better.

If three hours is better, perhaps one hour.

If one hour is good, perhaps fifteen minutes.

If 70 percent code review coverage improved quality, perhaps 90 percent would improve it more.

If 90 percent is good, 95 percent must be better.

If teams using AI 20 percent of the time are more productive, perhaps we should target 40 percent.

Then 60.

Number go up.

This is where optimization begins interacting with pressure.

At low levels of pressure, the easiest way to improve the metric may be to improve the underlying behavior.

Suppose we want engineers to perform more meaningful code reviews.

Initially, that's easy.

Some code isn't being reviewed at all.

So we review it.

The metric improves.

And quality may improve with it.

Great.

Then we push the target higher.

Most important code is already being reviewed.

Now we're looking for additional opportunities.

Maybe smaller changes get reviewed too.

Still reasonable.

The number rises again.

Then the target rises again.

Eventually we reach a point where nearly all of the obvious, valuable behavior has already been captured.

But the metric is still expected to improve.

Now things get interesting.

The organization has exhausted the cheap, meaningful ways to move the number.

People begin searching for other ways.

Not necessarily dishonest ways.

Just other ways.

Maybe trivial changes get separated into additional reviews.

Maybe automated changes count.

Maybe people request reviews on things that don't really need them.

Maybe the definition of “reviewed” becomes whatever the reporting system can detect.

Maybe everyone clicks the right button because the button is what the dashboard sees.

The number keeps moving.

But each additional unit of improvement tells us less about the outcome we originally cared about.

This pattern appears all over the place.

Take exercise.

If someone is sedentary, going from 1,000 steps a day to 5,000 may represent a substantial increase in physical activity.

Going from 5,000 to 8,000 may still be useful.

But if a person's fitness tracker announces that 10,000 steps is the goal, something interesting happens at 9:30 at night when the screen says 9,742.

The person may walk around the kitchen.

I've done things like this.

There is nothing wrong with walking around the kitchen.

Movement is movement.

But notice what happened.

Earlier, the step count was a proxy for an active life.

Now I am performing an activity specifically because it generates steps.

The causal direction has changed.

I am no longer walking and therefore accumulating steps.

I am walking **in order to accumulate steps**.

Again, that isn't necessarily bad.

The extra 258 steps are probably good for me.

But the distinction matters because once the metric itself becomes the reason for the behavior, the connection between the behavior and the original outcome needs to be watched more carefully.

The same thing happens at work.

At first:

**We did useful work, and the metric improved.**

Later:

**We need the metric to improve, so what work will make that happen?**

Those sentences can lead to very different places.

Pressure accelerates this transition because pressure changes the problem people are trying to solve.

Imagine a manager saying:

“We're tracking AI adoption because we want to understand whether these tools are helping people.”

That's one environment.

Now imagine:

“Our AI adoption target for this quarter is 70 percent, and every organization will be reporting progress weekly.”

That's another.

Same technology.

Same metric.

Different problem.

In the first environment, an engineer might ask:

**Would AI help me here?**

In the second, the engineer may begin asking:

**What can I use AI for?**

Those questions sound similar.

They are not.

The first starts with the work and asks whether the tool is useful.

The second starts with the metric and searches for work that will satisfy it.

The organization may still get value. People might discover useful applications they otherwise would have missed.

That's precisely why the intervention can work.

But as the pressure rises, so does the incentive to find increasingly marginal uses.

Eventually someone is asking an LLM to rewrite a three-word meeting title because, technically, that's AI usage.

**Synergy achieved.**

The organization has not necessarily hired cynical employees.

It has created an optimization problem.

And human beings are solving it.

This is why the relationship between a metric and an outcome can weaken as pressure increases.

The original correlation may have been genuine.

People who naturally use AI frequently for appropriate tasks may really be more productive.

Salespeople who naturally have more substantive customer conversations may really sell more.

Engineers who naturally engage in thoughtful code review may really produce better software.

But once we force the measurable activity upward, we begin adding cases that don't resemble the cases that created the correlation.

The first hundred customer conversations may have been valuable.

The next hundred, created specifically to hit a quota, may not be.

The original code reviews may have been thoughtful.

The additional reviews created to reach 99 percent compliance may be ceremonial.

The original AI usage may have reflected genuine utility.

The additional usage needed to hit the target may reflect creativity in finding things for AI to do.

This is where personal optimization produces an organizational consequence.

Each individual is solving the problem in front of them.

Hit the target.

Meet the expectation.

Avoid the red box.

Get the work done without creating unnecessary trouble for yourself.

Those are reasonable goals.

But when thousands of people make those reasonable adjustments, the population we're measuring changes.

The number no longer means quite what it meant before.

An AI interaction before the target may have happened because someone found AI useful.

An AI interaction after the target may have happened because someone found AI useful **or because AI usage was the target**.

The dashboard sees both as an AI interaction.

The human context has changed.

This creates an uncomfortable possibility.

The more successful we become at driving a metric upward, the less confident we may be that the metric still represents the thing that originally made us want it to go up.

Not always.

But enough that we should pay attention.

Because optimization has consequences.

At the individual level, optimization can look perfectly rational.

At the organizational level, thousands of individually rational decisions can accumulate into something nobody deliberately designed.

More meetings.

More reviews.

More tickets.

More activity.

More usage.

More things that count.

Everyone is doing what makes sense from where they're standing.

And the dashboard is glowing.

This is where we have to widen our field of view.

Because the question is no longer simply whether an individual can improve their number.

Of course they can.

The question is what happens to the larger system when everyone does.

That is where local optimization can become global absurdity.

## Local Optimization, Global Absurdity

There is a problem in engineering that shows up almost everywhere once you learn to recognize it.

You can improve every part of a system and somehow make the system worse.

This sounds impossible until you've worked in a sufficiently large organization.

Imagine a software project involving several groups.

The development team wants to deliver features quickly.

The quality team wants to prevent defects.

The security team wants to reduce risk.

The operations team wants stability.

Finance wants to control costs.

Management wants predictable schedules.

None of these goals are ridiculous.

In fact, I'd be concerned about an organization that didn't care about them.

So each group does what we've asked it to do.

Development optimizes delivery speed. It breaks work into smaller pieces, reduces unnecessary process, and pushes changes through quickly.

Quality optimizes defect prevention. It adds test requirements, review gates, validation steps, and additional checks before release.

Security optimizes risk. It introduces security reviews, scanning tools, approval requirements, and restrictions around sensitive systems.

Operations optimizes stability. It limits deployment windows, requires rollback plans, and becomes understandably suspicious of anything described as a “small change.”

Finance optimizes cost. It questions expensive infrastructure, reduces unused capacity, and asks whether every tool subscription is really necessary.

Management optimizes predictability. It asks teams to commit to dates, report progress, identify risks, and avoid surprises.

Everyone is doing their job.

This is the problem.

Each group sees a different part of reality, carries different responsibilities, and experiences different consequences when something goes wrong.

If a security vulnerability escapes, the security organization gets asked questions.

If a release crashes production, operations gets asked questions.

If the project is late, development and management get asked questions.

If spending exceeds the budget, finance gets asked questions.

So everyone learns to protect the thing they're responsible for.

Of course they do.

We've already established that people adapt to incentives. Organizations do the same thing at larger scales.

The security team doesn't experience a three-week delay the same way the product team does.

The product team doesn't experience a security vulnerability the same way the security team does.

Finance doesn't experience technical debt the same way engineering does.

Engineering doesn't experience an unexpected budget increase the same way finance does.

Each group has its own dashboard.

Each dashboard tells a true story.

And those stories can point in opposite directions.

This is local optimization.

A part of the system improves according to the goals visible from that part of the system.

Sometimes that's exactly what we need.

But a locally optimal decision is not necessarily a globally optimal decision.

Imagine that security discovers it can reduce risk by requiring an additional approval for every production deployment.

Security risk declines.

Green.

The approval adds two days to the average release.

Delivery speed gets worse.

Red.

Development responds by bundling more changes into each release so teams don't have to go through the approval process as often.

Delivery efficiency improves.

Green.

But now each deployment contains more changes, which makes releases harder to troubleshoot and increases operational risk.

Operations gets worse.

Red.

Operations responds by requiring more extensive pre-deployment testing.

Stability improves.

Green.

Release time gets worse again.

Red.

Management notices that schedules are becoming unpredictable and adds more planning checkpoints.

Predictability improves.

Green.

Engineers now spend more time in planning meetings.

Productivity declines.

Red.

Someone eventually schedules a meeting to discuss why productivity is declining.

Attendance is mandatory.

This is how organizations achieve greatness.

The joke works because the underlying problem is real.

Each response makes sense locally.

Nobody woke up intending to create a slow, bureaucratic development process.

Security reduced risk.

Operations protected stability.

Management improved predictability.

Quality prevented defects.

Finance controlled spending.

Development found ways around bottlenecks.

Every group may be able to point to its dashboard and demonstrate improvement.

And the overall system may become miserable.

This is one of the reasons organizational problems can survive for so long.

The evidence that something is wrong is distributed across the system.

No single dashboard necessarily contains it.

Security sees fewer vulnerabilities.

Finance sees lower spending.

Operations sees fewer incidents.

Management sees more predictable commitments.

Meanwhile, an engineer trying to make a modest change needs eleven approvals, three planning meetings, two security reviews, a deployment exception, and the blessing of someone named Greg who appears to exist solely inside an approval workflow.

The system is telling two different stories.

From above:

**Controls are working.**

From inside:

**I am trying to change six lines of code.**

Both may be true.

That's what makes global optimization difficult.

The organization isn't trying to maximize one thing.

It's trying to balance many things that matter simultaneously.

Speed matters.

Quality matters.

Security matters.

Cost matters.

Reliability matters.

Employee development matters.

Customer experience matters.

Long-term maintainability matters.

Sometimes improving one requires accepting a little less of another.

There is no dashboard that can make those tradeoffs disappear.

We can create a scorecard.

We can assign weights.

We can color-code everything.

We can build an executive summary with twelve boxes and arrows.

Eventually someone still has to exercise judgment.

This is especially important because metrics tend to create boundaries around responsibility.

If I'm responsible for security, I naturally care about the security metric.

If you're responsible for delivery, you naturally care about the delivery metric.

Neither of us necessarily owns the space between them.

But the space between them is often where the real system lives.

How much security is appropriate for this particular risk?

How much delivery speed are we willing to sacrifice?

How much redundancy is worth the cost?

How much testing is enough?

How much process protects us, and when does the process itself become the problem?

Those are not purely numerical questions.

They're judgment questions informed by numbers.

And judgment requires someone to care about the whole.

This is where leadership becomes more than making every box on the dashboard green.

Sometimes the right decision makes one of the boxes red.

Perhaps a critical security problem requires delaying a release.

Delivery gets worse.

Good.

Perhaps an urgent customer problem justifies accepting slightly more operational risk.

Risk gets worse.

Maybe that's the right trade.

Perhaps investing in architecture slows feature development for three months but prevents years of accumulating technical debt.

Short-term productivity gets worse.

The system may be getting healthier.

If every organization is required to optimize its own metric independently, these decisions become difficult.

Nobody wants to volunteer for red.

Red requires explanation.

Red attracts attention.

Red gets discussed in meetings.

Green lets everyone move on with their lives.

So local optimization naturally pushes organizations toward decisions that protect local metrics, even when the larger system would benefit from a tradeoff.

This creates one of the stranger possibilities in metric-driven organizations:

**Every team can succeed while the organization fails.**

Every group can meet its target.

Every dashboard can improve.

Every manager can explain the progress their organization has made.

And customers can still be unhappy.

The product can still be late.

Employees can still be exhausted.

Innovation can still slow.

The organization can become extraordinarily efficient at producing results that don't add up to the outcome anyone wanted.

That's why the question **“Did the metric improve?”** is never quite enough.

We also have to ask:

**What happened to the system?**

Did security improve by transferring unreasonable costs to development?

Did development improve speed by transferring defects to customers?

Did finance reduce costs by creating technical problems that will cost more later?

Did management improve predictability by encouraging teams to stop taking worthwhile risks?

Did one number go up because another important thing quietly went down?

The point isn't that local metrics are bad.

Local metrics are necessary.

A security organization needs visibility into security. Finance needs visibility into cost. Engineering needs visibility into quality and delivery.

But someone has to keep looking across the boundaries.

Someone has to ask whether all these individually sensible optimizations are producing a sensible whole.

Because systems don't experience metrics separately.

Customers don't experience our organizational chart.

They experience the product.

Employees don't experience our quarterly scorecard as a collection of independent indicators.

They experience the environment those indicators create.

And the organization ultimately has to live with the combined result.

That's the difference between optimizing a number and optimizing a system.

The number asks:

**How do we make this better?**

The system asks:

**What happens to everything else when we do?**

Both questions matter.

But when they conflict, the system has to win.

## The System Gets What It Asked For

When enough people start behaving strangely in the same way, pay attention.

They may be telling you something.

Organizations have a tendency to describe unwanted behavior as a problem with the people exhibiting it.

Why are teams sandbagging their estimates?

Why aren't employees taking enough risks?

Why are people rushing through training?

Why are engineers finding pointless uses for AI?

Why does everyone care so much about making their work visible?

Why won't people take ownership?

Sometimes the answer really is the people.

Human beings are capable of laziness, dishonesty, selfishness, fear, stubbornness, and every other inconvenient trait we've been carrying around since long before someone invented the quarterly business review.

People remain responsible for their choices.

But if the same behavior begins appearing across teams, departments, and organizations, another possibility deserves serious consideration.

Maybe the behavior makes sense.

Maybe the people have correctly understood the system.

This is where remembering that people are rational becomes important.

Not perfectly rational.

Not morally perfect.

Not infinitely wise.

Rational in the much more ordinary sense that people tend to notice what produces rewards and what produces consequences.

We learn.

If one path is painful and another is easier, we notice.

If one kind of work gets recognized and another disappears, we notice.

If one number gets discussed every week and another outcome gets mentioned twice a year in an all-hands meeting, we notice.

If our manager says:

**Quality is our highest priority**

but every difficult conversation is about schedule, we notice that too.

People are remarkably good at discovering the actual rules of a system.

The official rules may be printed on posters.

The actual rules are revealed by consequences.

This distinction matters because organizations often communicate two things at once.

They tell people what they want in words.

Then they tell people what they want through incentives.

**Take thoughtful risks.**

But don't miss your commitments.

**Focus on quality.**

But ship on Friday.

**Use AI where it adds value.**

Also, here's your weekly AI adoption score.

**Spend the time necessary to help the customer.**

Average call time must remain below eight minutes.

**We value collaboration.**

Your performance rating is based primarily on your individual accomplishments.

When those messages conflict, people have to decide which one to believe.

Usually, they believe the one with consequences attached.

That is not a failure of comprehension.

They understood perfectly.

This is why widespread behavior can be diagnostic.

Suppose management notices that project estimates are becoming increasingly conservative.

The first explanation might be:

**Teams are sandbagging.**

Maybe.

But before concluding that hundreds of employees have simultaneously experienced a decline in professional courage, it might be worth examining what happens when a team misses a commitment.

Does the miss appear on a dashboard?

Does it turn red?

Does leadership ask for an explanation?

Does the manager have to defend it?

Does the team acquire a reputation for poor execution?

Does the next planning cycle include an uncomfortable conversation about predictability?

Now ask what happens when a team commits to eight weeks and finishes in six.

Probably something considerably less memorable.

The team has learned something.

Being early is mildly pleasant.

Being late is painful.

Eight weeks starts looking pretty good.

We can call this sandbagging if we want.

But the label doesn't explain the behavior.

The incentive does.

Or imagine an organization that wants employees to experiment with AI.

Leadership believes, reasonably, that these tools could improve productivity. So the organization begins tracking adoption.

Initially, the measurement may simply help leadership understand what's happening.

Then targets appear.

Then teams compare adoption rates.

Then managers receive reports.

Then employees begin hearing that adoption needs to increase.

Six months later, someone notices that employees are using AI for tasks that clearly don't require AI.

What happened?

One interpretation is:

**Employees are gaming the metric.**

Another is:

**Employees understood the assignment.**

We told them AI usage mattered.

We measured it.

We reported it.

We established a target.

We made managers accountable for it.

Then employees found ways to increase it.

The system got what it asked for.

This doesn't mean leadership wanted pointless AI usage.

Of course it didn't.

Leadership wanted productive AI usage.

But productive is the difficult part.

Usage is the measurable part.

So the measurable part acquired the target.

And people optimized accordingly.

This is why it can be unfair to create a system that rewards one behavior and then depend on employees to continually exercise enough independent judgment to resist the reward.

We tell people:

**Don't just optimize the metric. Do what's right.**

That sounds reasonable.

Sometimes it is necessary.

But notice the arrangement we've created.

The organization defines the score.

The organization publishes the score.

The organization attaches consequences to the score.

Then the employee becomes responsible for knowing when to ignore the score.

If thousands of people consistently have to work around the measurement system in order to do good work, perhaps the measurement system deserves some attention.

A better question is:

**What behavior have we made rational?**

Not:

**What behavior did we intend?**

Intentions matter, but incentives reveal the environment people actually inhabit.

If we reward speed while talking about quality, we should expect people to notice speed.

If we punish missed estimates while talking about boldness, we should expect caution.

If we count output while praising invisible work, we should expect visible output.

If we target tool usage while talking about judgment, we should expect tool usage.

This doesn't absolve anyone of responsibility.

A bad incentive does not justify lying.

A poorly designed metric does not excuse falsifying data, hiding defects, misleading customers, or behaving unethically.

Personal responsibility remains.

But organizational responsibility remains too.

You don't get to design an environment that predictably pushes people in one direction and then treat their movement in that direction as an inexplicable moral failure.

That's especially true when the behavior becomes widespread.

One person doing something strange might be a person problem.

A thousand people doing the same strange thing might be a system problem.

At the very least, it's worth investigating.

This is one of the reasons leaders should become curious when employees develop workarounds.

A workaround is information.

So is sandbagging.

So is excessive escalation.

So is reluctance to take risks.

So is an obsession with visibility.

So is suspiciously perfect compliance.

These behaviors may be annoying.

They may also be telling us where the system hurts.

Imagine a company that requires every project to pass through a complicated approval process.

Employees discover ways around it.

Leadership responds by tightening enforcement.

Employees discover new ways around it.

Leadership tightens enforcement again.

Eventually the organization concludes:

**People just don't respect process.**

Possibly.

Or perhaps the people closest to the work have discovered that following the process exactly makes it unnecessarily difficult to get work done.

That doesn't automatically mean the process should disappear.

The employees may be missing risks visible to leadership.

The process may exist because of legal, financial, security, or operational concerns they don't fully see.

We've already talked about the danger of assuming the frontline possesses the whole truth.

It doesn't.

But neither does the process.

The workaround is a signal.

Investigate it.

Why are reasonable people repeatedly choosing this path?

What friction are they avoiding?

What consequence are they protecting themselves from?

What does the behavior tell us that the dashboard doesn't?

Those questions are more useful than immediately assigning a character flaw.

Because systems teach.

People learn.

And then the system receives the behavior it taught people to produce.

That's why widespread undesirable behavior should create curiosity before condemnation.

If people keep optimizing the wrong thing, perhaps they misunderstood the goal.

Perhaps they need better training.

Perhaps expectations need to be clearer.

Perhaps accountability really is necessary.

But perhaps they understood the system perfectly.

Perhaps the number we chose is pulling harder than the behavior we say we want.

Perhaps we have made the wrong thing rational.

And if that's true, telling people to try harder won't solve it.

The system has to change.

Because eventually every organization gets very good at producing what it consistently rewards.

The uncomfortable part is discovering that this may not be the same thing it says it wants.
