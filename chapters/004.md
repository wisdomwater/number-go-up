# 4: You Get What You Measure

### Of Course We Want Behavior to Change

At this point, it would be easy to conclude that the safest metric is one nobody knows about. Measure quietly. Hide the dashboard. Whatever you do, don't let the employees see it.

That would solve one problem by creating several others, because we measure things partly because we want behavior to change.

Suppose a software organization takes twenty-three days, on average, to move a completed change into production. That seems slow, so the team looks at the process, discovers several unnecessary handoffs, and removes them. Now it takes nineteen days. They make another improvement and get it down to fourteen.

This is measurement doing exactly what we want it to do. Without the number, the organization might have a vague sense that releases are getting faster. *Velocity: feels faster.* With measurement, we have feedback. We can change something, observe what happens, learn from the result, and adjust again.

That feedback loop is one of the great strengths of measurement. It's how athletes improve performance, businesses improve processes, scientists test ideas, and engineers make systems faster and more reliable. The fact that measurement changes behavior isn't the problem. Often, changing behavior is the entire point.

The question is which behavior it changes.

Consider code review. Software engineers frequently ask other engineers to review their code before it becomes part of the product. A good reviewer may find a bug, notice a security problem, suggest a simpler design, or ask a question that exposes an assumption the author hadn't considered.

Thoughtful code review can improve software. Suppose an organization notices that teams using code review consistently have fewer defects. That's useful information, so management encourages more code review. Defects decline.

Wonderful. We've found a behavior correlated with an outcome we care about, encouraged the behavior, and watched the outcome improve.

Then someone asks for a dashboard.

### The Correlation Was Real

The request for a dashboard isn't unreasonable. If code review is helping, management wants to know whether it's happening consistently. Maybe some teams have adopted the practice while others haven't. Maybe review rates are falling under schedule pressure.

So we begin measuring code-review compliance. Perhaps 40 percent of changes are reviewed. We encourage teams to improve, and now it's 65 percent. Quality improves. We keep going: 80 percent, then 90 percent.

Eventually the organization establishes a target: code-review compliance must remain above 95 percent.

Something subtle has happened along the way. Originally, we were asking whether a change received a meaningful review. Now the system is increasingly asking whether the change received a review that counts.

Those questions overlap, but they are not identical. A careful thirty-minute discussion between two engineers might be enormously valuable but fail to satisfy whatever mechanism records an official review. Meanwhile, a two-second approval in the correct tool may count beautifully.

The original correlation wasn't fake. That's important. Code review really may have improved quality. Management didn't invent an arbitrary number and force everyone to worship it. The metric emerged from something that worked.

This is how many problematic metrics begin. They begin by being useful.

A behavior correlates with an outcome. We encourage the behavior. The outcome improves. The metric appears to confirm that we've found a lever, so naturally we pull harder.

This may work particularly well at first. Moving code review from 40 percent to 70 percent could capture a great deal of obvious value as changes that previously received no second set of eyes now get meaningful scrutiny. Moving from 70 to 85 percent might still help.

Eventually, though, the easy and valuable opportunities have mostly been captured while the target keeps going. At that point, the organization has two ways to produce further improvement. It can do the difficult work of finding additional ways to improve the underlying reality, or it can discover cheaper ways to improve the proxy.

Human beings are quite good at discovering the cheaper route. This doesn't require dishonesty. It requires optimization.

We do the same thing with project estimates. Organizations want predictable delivery because customers make plans, other teams have dependencies, and managers need to know when things will arrive. Teams therefore estimate their work, and we compare those estimates with actual delivery.

Then missed commitments turn red. Red numbers get discussed. Teams that consistently hit commitments look good, and eventually something predictable happens: estimates become more conservative.

Management looks at those estimates and asks why everyone is sandbagging.

Well, we asked for predictability. We measured predictability. We rewarded predictability. People became more predictable.

The metric worked—just perhaps not entirely in the way we intended.

This is the central challenge of optimization. A proxy can be genuinely connected to something we care about and still become less useful as we push harder on it. Our ability to optimize the proxy can eventually outrun the proxy's ability to represent the outcome.

The shortest path to a better number no longer necessarily passes through the thing we were originally trying to improve.

And organizations are very good at finding short paths.

### Local Optimization, Global Absurdity

The problem gets even more interesting when different parts of an organization optimize different things.

Imagine a software company. Development wants to deliver features quickly. Quality wants fewer defects. Security wants less risk. Operations wants stability. Finance wants lower cost. Management wants predictability.

None of these goals is stupid. In fact, I'd be concerned about a company that didn't care about them.

So each group builds processes and measurements around the things it is responsible for. Security notices that some releases contain vulnerabilities, so a new security review is added before release. Security risk goes down.

Good.

Unfortunately, releases now take longer.

Development adapts. Instead of sending many small changes through security review, teams begin bundling changes together so they can go through the approval process less often. Development's efficiency improves, but the larger releases are harder to test and riskier to deploy.

Operations notices. It adds additional pre-release testing. Stability improves, but release time gets longer again.

Management notices that projects are becoming harder to predict, so it adds checkpoints. Teams now provide more frequent status updates, planning reviews, and milestone reports. Predictability improves while engineers spend more time in meetings.

Eventually someone schedules a meeting to discuss why productivity is declining.

Attendance is mandatory.

Nobody had to behave irrationally for us to get here. Security did its job. Operations did its job. Development adapted to the environment. Management responded to declining predictability. Every group can point to a legitimate concern, and some may even be able to point to a dashboard demonstrating improvement.

Security incidents are down. Operational failures are down. Planning compliance is up.

And the overall system may be miserable.

This is local optimization. Each part of the organization improves the thing visible from where it stands, while the combined effect makes the larger system worse.

Software engineers encounter this problem constantly in technical systems. You can make one component faster while slowing down the application as a whole. You can reduce memory usage in one place by creating more work somewhere else. The component metric improves while the product does not.

Organizations behave the same way, except organizational components have meetings, incentives, budgets, reporting structures, and vice presidents.

Suppose a senior executive tells the organization that delivery speed needs to improve. Again, reasonable. The division leader asks directors what they're doing to improve speed. Directors ask managers. Managers look for things their teams can change.

Eventually an engineer gets asked why a bug took nine days to close.

Nobody in that chain needs to be malicious. The executive is trying to improve competitiveness. The director is responding to an organizational priority. The manager is trying to improve team performance. The engineer is trying to solve a difficult bug.

Each person is acting reasonably from where they stand, but the pressure changes as it travels. "Improve delivery speed" becomes "reduce cycle time," which eventually becomes "why is this number red?"

The engineer learns something about the environment: close bugs faster.

Next time two bugs are waiting—one easy and one difficult—the easy one has acquired an advantage that has nothing to do with its importance to the product.

We saw this kind of adaptation in Chapter 2. What's different here is that the employee isn't the only one adapting. Managers adapt to the measures above them. Departments adapt to the goals assigned to them. Functions optimize their own responsibilities. The organization becomes a network of locally rational responses.

Then we look at the result and wonder why the system behaves strangely.

A useful question in these situations is: **What behavior have we made rational?**

That's more interesting than immediately asking why people are gaming the system. If an entire sales organization delays deals until the next quarter, something may be wrong with the incentive structure. If engineers systematically avoid difficult bugs, perhaps the way we reward closure time deserves attention. If managers keep adding schedule padding, perhaps we've made missing a commitment more dangerous than making an accurate estimate.

Behavior is information too.

This doesn't mean people are never responsible for bad choices. Sometimes an engineer avoids difficult work because they don't want to do it. Sometimes a manager pads a schedule because they want an easy target. Sometimes someone manipulates a metric deliberately.

People remain accountable for their behavior. But when the same behavior appears repeatedly across many otherwise capable people, it is worth examining the environment before concluding that the organization has mysteriously hired an entire population of bad actors.

The system may be teaching people how to survive inside it.

Sometimes, though, the behavior we actually want is precisely the behavior that makes the metric look worse.

### Sometimes the Metric Needs to Lose

Return to the customer-service example from Chapter 2. The organization wants calls resolved efficiently, and eight minutes is the target.

One day a representative receives a complicated call. The customer has a real problem, and solving it requires looking through several systems, explaining what happened, correcting an error, and making sure the customer understands what to do next. Thirty-seven minutes later, the problem is solved. The customer is happy.

The call-duration metric is not.

What should happen next? Someone should probably ask why the call took thirty-seven minutes. That's what the metric is for.

Maybe the representative needs more training. Maybe the internal tools are terrible. Maybe the customer had to repeat information that should already have been available. Maybe twenty of those thirty-seven minutes were unnecessary.

Or maybe this customer simply needed thirty-seven minutes of help.

In that case, the metric needs to lose.

That's a surprisingly important test of a measurement system: **Can it lose?** Can an employee make the number worse and still have made the right decision? Can a team miss a target and still have exercised excellent judgment? Can a manager look at a red box, understand what happened, and say, "Yes. That's exactly what I wanted you to do"?

If the answer is no, the metric is no longer merely informing judgment. It has replaced judgment.

This does not mean targets are optional suggestions we can ignore whenever they become inconvenient. That would be a wonderfully comfortable interpretation of this book. "I know my numbers are terrible, but Michael said reality is complicated."

Nice try.

Sometimes the number is bad because the situation is bad. A thirty-seven-minute call really may have taken far too long. A project may miss its schedule because the team planned poorly. A bug may take three weeks because nobody treated it with enough urgency.

Accountability still matters. The difference is that accountability begins by asking what happened. A metric acting as a verdict begins with the assumption that the bad number has already answered the question.

**A metric is information, not a moral judgment.**

A red number tells us that reality differed from an expectation. We still need to understand why. That investigation may lead to coaching, process improvement, a different target, or a conclusion that someone exercised exactly the judgment we hoped they would exercise.

Sometimes healthy systems produce ugly numbers.

Consider a hospital trying to reduce patient wait times. A critically ill patient arrives and requires an unusual amount of attention, causing other patients to wait longer. The wait-time metric gets worse.

That's not evidence that waiting is good. It's evidence that the larger purpose mattered more than the target in that particular moment. We wanted the hospital to optimize wait times right up until doing so conflicted with caring properly for a patient.

At that point, the target should lose.

This is where judgment enters the system, and judgment makes management harder. "Eight minutes" is easy to communicate. "Use eight minutes as a meaningful target, except when the circumstances justify something else, and then be prepared to explain your reasoning" is considerably messier.

The second version is also much closer to how the world actually works.

A rule is clean. A dashboard can be reviewed quickly. Judgment requires someone to understand context, weigh tradeoffs, and occasionally explain why a bad-looking number was the result of a good decision.

That doesn't scale as neatly, but neither does reality.

We should also notice that judgment was present long before the employee made the thirty-seven-minute call. Someone chose the metric. Someone decided that eight minutes was desirable. Someone chose how the number would be reported and what consequences would follow from missing it.

Those were judgments too.

Once embedded in a dashboard, however, those earlier judgments can become almost invisible. The eight-minute target begins to look like a fact about the world rather than a decision someone made about how the world ought to work.

Allowing the metric to lose is therefore not abandoning rigor. It is acknowledging that the measurement was always part of a larger act of judgment.

### Optimize for the Point

Suppose a software team has committed to releasing a product on Friday. On Wednesday, the team discovers a serious architectural problem.

They now have a choice. They can ship Friday and accept the risk, or delay the release and fix the problem properly.

The schedule can tell us that the team committed to Friday. It can tell us how much work remains and perhaps how the delay affects other commitments. All of that matters.

What the schedule cannot decide is whether the architectural problem is serious enough to justify missing the date. It cannot tell us whether the team is being appropriately cautious or unnecessarily perfectionistic. It cannot know what a failure would cost customers.

Judgment has to hold those things together.

This is why, when a metric begins producing strange behavior, it helps to walk backward from the number toward the reason we started measuring it.

Suppose we're measuring test coverage. Why?

Because we want engineers to test the software.

Why do we care about testing? Because testing helps us find problems before customers do. We want to find those problems because we want reliable software, and we want reliable software because people depend on what we're building.

There it is.

The point.

Now we can walk forward again. Does this particular test help us build more reliable software? If so, write it. If we're writing a meaningless test solely to move coverage from 94.8 percent to 95 percent, perhaps the number can lose.

The purpose stays. The proxy is negotiable.

Optimization has a way of reversing that order. We begin with something that matters, find something measurable that appears to help, and improve the number. The outcome improves, which gives us confidence in the metric, so we push harder.

Eventually making the number better can become an objective of its own. The original purpose doesn't disappear; it simply recedes into the background.

That's how a company can sincerely value quality while creating a system that rewards shipping too quickly. A customer-service organization can sincerely value customers while pressuring representatives to end calls. A software organization can sincerely value thoughtful engineering while making difficult bugs dangerous to pick up.

Nobody has to stop caring about the point. They only have to become more accountable for the proxy than for the thing the proxy represents.

The way back is not to stop optimizing. Optimization is useful. The way back is to keep the optimization connected to its purpose.

The shortest path to a better number should still pass through the outcome we actually care about. When it doesn't, something has to give. We may need to change the metric, adjust the incentive, add context, or simply allow judgment to overrule the number.

That requires something from both sides of the dashboard.

Managers have to create room for a person to say, "I know this number looks bad, but here's what happened," without making the sentence feel like the opening statement at a trial. Employees have to explain the bad number honestly and accept that the explanation may not be good enough.

Context is not an escape from accountability, and measurement is not an escape from judgment. The two have to remain in conversation.

When a metric and lived experience disagree, we don't have to decide in advance which one deserves to win. We can ask what the number sees that the person doesn't, and what the person sees that the number cannot. Then we make a judgment about the thing we actually care about.

That's what the metric was supposed to help us do in the first place.

A company doesn't ultimately need faster bug closure. It needs better software. A hospital doesn't ultimately need shorter wait times. It needs to care for patients well. A customer-service organization doesn't ultimately need shorter calls. It needs to help customers effectively.

The measurements matter because those larger things matter first.

That means we can track them seriously. We can set targets. We can use numbers to challenge comfortable stories and hold people accountable when accountability is needed. None of that requires us to pretend the proxy and the purpose are the same thing.

And every once in a while, when the two come apart, we have to be willing to let the metric lose.

The number gets a seat at the table because it has something important to tell us. It just doesn't get the final word.

**Reality does.**
