# 5 - The View From Above

> *A map is useful because it leaves things out.*

## Altitude Changes What You Can See

The farther you rise above a system, the more of it you can see at once and the less detail survives. From the ground, a road is cracks in the pavement, traffic lights, storefronts, and the particular pothole you know to avoid. From high enough above, it becomes a line connecting one place to another. Neither view is false; they are useful for different reasons.

Organizations work the same way. An engineer may spend hours inside one failure, reading logs, tracing code, checking specifications, and trying to understand which assumption broke. A manager responsible for several teams cannot live at that depth all day. The questions change with altitude: how many failures do we have, where are they clustering, which ones threaten the schedule, and is the overall system getting healthier?

Answering those questions requires compression. Hundreds of details become a status, a trend line, a risk level, perhaps a red, yellow, or green box on a slide. Something is lost in that process, but the loss is not necessarily a mistake. If every summary preserved every detail, the summary would eventually become a slightly less convenient copy of the company.

That is why maps work. They leave out trees, potholes, smells, weather, conversations, and nearly everything that makes a place feel like a place. In exchange, they reveal relationships that are difficult to notice while standing on the sidewalk. Abstraction trades texture for shape, and sometimes shape is exactly what we need.

The difficulty begins when we forget that a trade was made. A manager looking across the system may miss the single detail that changes the conclusion, while an engineer buried in one problem may miss a larger pattern forming around it. The useful question is not whether we should choose the map or the territory, but which altitude the decision requires.

## One Bug, One Thousand Sightings

Graphics drivers undergo an enormous amount of testing. Writing a new feature might account for ten percent of the work surrounding it; much of the remaining effort goes into proving that the thing actually works, figuring out why it does not, and repeating that process until everyone is willing to let customers discover the remaining surprises. At any given time, we might have a few hundred active bug sightings, and sometimes closer to a thousand.

At ground level, one sighting can consume hours or days. You find the log files, sift through traces, compare what happened against the architectural specification, inspect the code, and reconstruct a chain of events from evidence that has made no particular effort to be helpful. Debugging is messy, tedious work, and it rewards a peculiar combination of stubbornness and the willingness to stare at the same few lines until they finally confess.

A manager looking across a thousand sightings has a different problem. A thousand sightings almost certainly do not mean a thousand independent defects. Twenty failures may be different expressions of the same root cause, one subsystem may suddenly account for a disproportionate share of the trouble, or several teams may unknowingly be investigating different symptoms of one underlying issue. At that altitude, going deep into every log file would not demonstrate admirable diligence; it would make the job impossible.

The manager therefore looks for patterns while the engineer looks for causes. One asks what is happening across the product, while the other asks what happened in this particular failure. The bug that consumed two days of an engineer's attention may eventually become one item in a category on somebody else's chart, but that compression can reveal something the engineer could not see from inside the individual problem.

Neither view is more real than the other. They are descriptions of the same system at different resolutions. One gives us mechanism and the other gives us shape.

## Different Altitudes, Different Stories

Problems begin when we confuse those stories with one another. A broad trend can tell us where to look without telling us why something is happening. A deep technical investigation can explain exactly why one failure occurred while telling us almost nothing about whether the product as a whole is improving. Both contain truth, and both leave things outside the frame.

Healthy organizations allow the perspectives to correct one another. Breadth helps determine where depth is needed, while depth keeps the broad picture from becoming too pleased with its own cleanliness. A manager who never descends into the details can end up making decisions from a map that has drifted away from the territory, while an engineer who never looks up can solve a local problem beautifully and still miss the larger risk.

Altitude is therefore not a hierarchy of truth. The person farther up the organization does not automatically possess a more enlightened perspective, nor does the person touching the code possess privileged access to every question that matters. Altitude determines which details remain visible and which ones have been intentionally allowed to fade.

The trick is learning when to move.

## The Right Question at the Wrong Time

One of the more intense moments in a hardware program is power-on, when new silicon comes back from the fab and we begin finding out what we actually built. Early systems are often unstable while configuration details are dialed in and assumptions that looked perfectly respectable on paper are introduced to physical reality. There is a little ceremony to it and a little chaos, rather like trying to teach a newborn giraffe to run a graphics benchmark.

Because those systems are fragile, we work through a rigorous checklist. The tests begin simply and increase in complexity as confidence grows. The order matters because each successful step tells us something about what is safe to attempt next. Skipping ahead can turn one understandable failure into several mysteries stacked on top of one another.

During one power-on, I was working through that checklist when our group director came into the lab and asked for the performance score from a 3D graphics benchmark we had targeted for improvement. We had not reached that point yet, and I told him so. He was persistent enough that we finally tried it anyway.

The benchmark hung the system, and we spent roughly the next five hours getting the machine back into a useful state. From where I was sitting, this was maddening because the process we had just bypassed existed precisely to prevent this sort of adventure. We had traded methodical progress for several hours of recovery work, and we still did not have a trustworthy benchmark score.

But the director's question was not foolish. That performance number mattered to the product, and he had customers, platform teams, schedules, and other leaders waiting for evidence that the silicon was delivering what had been promised. I was concerned with the sequence required to produce a meaningful number; he was concerned with whether the number existed yet because its absence was becoming information of its own.

The metric was important. The system simply was not ready to answer the question safely.

## Coming Back Down

What bothered me most about that experience was not merely losing five hours. It was the feeling that the work required to reach the benchmark had disappeared from view. From the dashboard altitude, the missing item was a performance number; from inside the lab, that number sat at the end of a careful sequence of configuration, testing, recovery, and gradually increasing confidence.

This is one of abstraction's quieter dangers. A clean status slide makes the work underneath it look cleaner than it is. The red, yellow, or green box does not show the failed boots, the log files, the cautious sequencing, the abandoned theories, or the people who spent half the night getting a system back into a state where it could produce the next number. It was never intended to show those things, but after staring at dashboards long enough it becomes surprisingly easy to forget that they are there.

That is where altitude becomes a question of respect as much as information. People doing difficult work need leaders who are willing, at least occasionally, to come down from the dashboard and see the conditions under which the numbers are being produced. Not because the director needs to become the engineer, but because understanding the texture of the work changes how you interpret the abstraction that eventually emerges from it.

Leadership cannot live on the ground floor. If every director personally followed every debug sequence, very little directing would occur. But there is a difference between not knowing every detail and forgetting that there are details worth respecting.

The healthiest movement goes both ways. Leaders rise high enough to see the landscape, then descend often enough to remember what the contour lines have erased.

## When Pain Needs a Number

On another project, we were given an aggressive assignment: rewrite a large part of our driver in about six months. We knew immediately that fast feedback would be essential because the schedule left little room for waiting half a day to discover that yesterday's code did not work. Unfortunately, our existing test infrastructure had been designed for a different kind of problem.

The automation system was built to drive bulk testing across hundreds of machines. It was good at throughput, but our project needed something nimble enough to serve three systems under rapid development. The path from a code change to a useful test result could take six to twelve hours, which meant that a mistake made in the morning had a decent chance of introducing itself sometime around dinner.

The automation team had understandable priorities of its own. They were keeping a large farm healthy and dealing with a backlog of service requests, and the improvements we wanted were estimated to take a couple of quarters. Our six-month project would be largely finished by the time our fast feedback system arrived, which is technically one way to make a problem go away.

From our altitude, the pain was obvious. We lived inside the delay every day and could see how much it was slowing the work. But "this is painful" is not especially portable information when a manager has to justify spending people and money, particularly if that justification has to survive several layers of management on its way to approval.

So we needed numbers.

The difficulty was that we did not yet have the system we wanted to measure against. To demonstrate how much faster a home-grown approach could be, we first had to build enough of that approach to prove it. That meant putting in evenings and weekends creating the thing before we had the data showing that creating the thing was worthwhile.

Once it existed, the comparison was almost embarrassing. The old flow could require six hours or more to deliver a result; our smaller system could turn one around in roughly ten minutes. Suddenly the problem that had been obvious to us for months became visible in a form that could travel.

There is a certain corporate comedy in having to build the solution before you can gather the evidence required to justify building the solution. Still, management's demand for data was not irrational. Leaders cannot fund every frustration presented to them, and eventually they have to compare one team's painful bottleneck against another team's equally sincere claim that something else deserves attention first.

The number gave the pain a passport.

## What Has Not Yet Been Measured

This creates a tension that is easy to miss from either altitude. Asking for evidence is responsible because money, people, and attention are limited. A manager who approves every request on the strength of "trust me, this is terrible" will eventually discover that the company contains more terrible things than budget.

At the same time, *not yet quantified* is not the same thing as *not important*. Some problems first become visible as friction, confusion, repeated waiting, or the collective groan of people who have been living with a system for too long. The formal metric often arrives later, after someone has investigated the problem deeply enough to understand what should even be measured.

Dashboards are naturally better at showing known quantities than emerging realities. They can display the six-hour test delay once someone has instrumented it, but they are less capable of registering the dozens of little adaptations engineers have already made because they know the system is slow. People closest to the work can often feel a failure before the organization has developed a clean vocabulary for describing it.

This does not mean intuition should overrule evidence whenever someone feels strongly enough. It means data has a history. Someone had to notice the problem, formulate a question, build the instrumentation, gather the observations, and interpret what the number meant before the smooth line ever appeared on a chart.

Pristine charts hide their own origin stories. Good judgment remembers that every clean number emerged from somebody's messy reality.

## The Right Altitude

A few principles are worth carrying forward. Abstraction is necessary because nobody can hold the whole organization in view at once. Maps, dashboards, status colors, and metrics allow us to see patterns that disappear when we stay too close to the ground, but every increase in altitude trades some texture for that broader shape.

Neither view is complete. The person closest to the work may understand the mechanism while missing the wider pattern, and the person looking across the system may recognize the pattern while missing the effort, uncertainty, and judgment hidden beneath it. Good decisions depend less on choosing one perspective than on knowing when the current perspective is no longer enough.

Broad signals can tell us where attention belongs, but consequential judgments often require a descent back into the territory. The greater the consequence, the more important it becomes to understand what the abstraction left outside the frame. Sometimes the yellow box is all the information needed; sometimes somebody needs to go downstairs and ask why it is yellow.

Numbers do not arrive fully formed on dashboards. People notice problems, gather evidence, build instrumentation, interpret results, and compress all of that effort into something another person can absorb in a few seconds. The map is useful precisely because it is not the territory.

Wisdom is knowing when to look at the map, when to trust it, and when to fold it up for a while and go see the road for yourself.
