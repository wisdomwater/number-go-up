# 4 — The View From Above

There is a point in almost every career when the work begins to look different because you are no longer standing in the same place. Early on, you may know one piece of a system in extraordinary detail: the code you wrote, the customer you support, the machine you operate, or the project sitting directly in front of you. As responsibility grows, the field of view expands, but something has to give. You see more things and fewer details about each of them. This is not necessarily a loss of competence; it is a change in altitude.

An engineer may spend an afternoon tracing a bug through thousands of lines of code, learning exactly which sequence of events causes a failure that appears once every several hours. The manager responsible for that engineer may know only that an important bug has been open for three weeks and threatens a milestone. A director looking across several teams may see the entire project represented as yellow, while an executive looking across dozens of projects may see one yellow box among a sea of green and red. All of these descriptions can be true at the same time. They are simply views taken from different heights.

The higher view has obvious advantages. From the ground, I can see the individual trees, but I may have no idea where the forest ends or which direction the river runs. From above, those larger patterns become visible, while the texture of the bark disappears. The ground reveals detail; altitude reveals pattern. Neither perspective gives us the whole landscape.

This creates one of the recurring tensions of organizational life. The people closest to the work often feel that leaders do not understand what is really happening, while leaders sometimes feel that people closest to the work cannot see the larger picture. Both complaints may contain some truth because the two groups are being asked to see different things. The challenge is not deciding which altitude is correct, but recognizing what becomes visible and what disappears as we move between them.

## You Can't Be Everywhere

Direct experience stops scaling surprisingly quickly. A manager with five employees can have regular conversations with each of them, understand much of their work, and develop a reasonably detailed sense of what is happening. Give that manager fifty people and the relationship changes, even if they are exceptionally attentive. Give an executive fifty thousand employees and the idea that they should simply “talk to people more” begins to encounter the inconvenient limits of arithmetic.

There are only so many conversations a person can have in a day. Even if an executive spent every working hour talking with employees, the overwhelming majority of the organization would remain people they had never met doing work they had never personally observed. The same problem applies to products, customers, projects, budgets, risks, and technical decisions. At sufficient scale, direct experience cannot provide enough information to govern the whole.

So information travels upward in compressed form. An engineer describes a technical problem to a lead, the lead summarizes it for a manager, the manager reports the project status to a director, and eventually the situation may arrive before an executive as a colored indicator on a slide. Along the way, details disappear because they have to. If every level preserved every detail from the level below it, the executive presentation would require several fiscal quarters to complete.

This can be funny when you are living at the bottom of the compression stack. You may have spent two weeks learning that a particular failure requires a specific firmware version, a certain workload, an unusual timing condition, and what appears to be an interaction between two components owned by different teams. You explain all of this carefully to your manager. Somewhere three organizational layers later, your life's work becomes: **GPU issue — yellow.**

It is tempting to feel insulted by that reduction, but the yellow box may be doing exactly what it was designed to do. The executive probably does not need the register values, reproduction steps, or history of every hypothesis the team has eliminated. They need to know whether the project is at risk, how significant the risk is, and whether a decision or additional resource is required. The detail that makes the problem understandable to the engineer may make the larger organization impossible to understand.

This is the basic trade of altitude. As we rise, individual features disappear and larger patterns emerge. A leader may not understand why a particular bug took three weeks, but they may notice that bug duration has increased across six teams over the past year. The engineer can see the first reality with extraordinary clarity, while the leader may be the only person positioned to see the second.

## The Genius of Abstraction

Abstraction is one of the great tools of human thought because it allows us to work with complexity we could never hold directly. A road map does not show every tree, driveway, mailbox, crack in the pavement, or person walking down the street. If it did, finding the route from Sacramento to San Francisco would become considerably harder. The map becomes useful by deciding what not to show.

Organizations work the same way. Revenue compresses millions of transactions into a number. Headcount turns thousands of individual careers into a description of organizational size, while a schedule reduces countless technical decisions and dependencies into a sequence of dates. Each abstraction discards enormous amounts of information, but the discarded information is precisely what allows the remaining information to become visible.

Imagine an executive dashboard that attempted to solve this problem by including everything. Every employee's current work, every customer interaction, every defect, every technical decision, every risk, every budget transaction, and every conversation relevant to every project would be available in real time. We might congratulate ourselves on eliminating abstraction. Unfortunately, we would have recreated the company.

The dashboard works because it is smaller than the reality it represents. Its purpose is not to contain the organization but to reveal particular patterns within it. Revenue may reveal whether customers are buying, defect trends may reveal whether quality is changing, and schedule status may reveal where delivery is at risk. None of those measurements has to explain everything in order to be useful.

This is worth emphasizing because criticism of metrics can become too easy when we forget the problem they solve. Managers do not use abstractions because they lack appreciation for human complexity. They use abstractions because complexity is real, time is limited, and decisions still have to be made. The alternative to imperfect information is rarely perfect information; more often, it is making the same decision while knowing even less.

Good abstraction is therefore an achievement. It identifies which features of a complicated reality matter for the decision in front of us and temporarily allows the others to recede. The trouble begins when the temporary disappearance becomes permanent in our thinking. What the dashboard cannot show can slowly become what the organization forgets to see.

## Managers Need the Map. Employees Live in the Territory.

Imagine a project that has turned yellow. From the director's perspective, this means the schedule is at risk, a dependency may not land in time, and the team needs a recovery plan. Those are legitimate concerns because other projects may depend on this one, customers may be waiting, and commitments may already have been made. The yellow status is doing useful work by attracting attention to something that might otherwise be missed.

Inside the project, however, yellow has a much richer history. Perhaps an engineer discovered that an assumption made six months earlier was wrong and had the judgment to stop rather than continue building on it. Another engineer may be caring for a dying parent and working at a pace that is entirely reasonable for the life they are currently living. A dependency may have slipped because another team found a safety or quality issue that everyone agrees was worth fixing properly.

None of these details necessarily changes the project status. The schedule can still be at risk, and pretending otherwise would not help anyone. But they may change what the status *means* and therefore what a wise response looks like. A project delayed by poor execution and a project delayed because someone discovered a serious design flaw can have the same color on a dashboard while requiring very different conversations.

This is where the view from above and the view from the ground can collide. The employee living inside the full-resolution story may hear the simplified description and think, *They have no idea what's happening here.* The leader looking across twenty projects may hear the detailed explanation and think, *I understand that this one is complicated, but I still have nineteen other projects to manage.* Both reactions are understandable.

The phrase I keep returning to is simple: **Managers need the map. Employees live in the territory.** The manager cannot abandon the map because the territory is too large to experience directly. The employee cannot abandon the territory because it is where the work actually happens. Healthy organizations need ways for information to travel in both directions.

That last part matters. We often think of organizational information as something that moves upward: engineers provide status, managers aggregate it, executives make decisions. But context also has to move downward, and detail sometimes needs to travel back upward when a decision requires it. The map should tell us where to look, not persuade us that looking is unnecessary.

## The Right Altitude for the Decision

Not every decision requires the same amount of information. If I want to know whether to take an umbrella this afternoon, a weather app showing a high probability of rain is probably enough. If I am designing a bridge expected to withstand a hundred years of weather, “looks rainy” is not an acceptable meteorological model. The appropriate resolution depends on what we are trying to decide.

Organizations sometimes get into trouble when they use information gathered at one altitude to make decisions that belong at another. An aggregate trend in bug duration may be excellent evidence that a team should examine its development process. The same trend may be poor evidence that a particular engineer is underperforming because one difficult bug remained open for three weeks. The number did not become less accurate; the decision became more specific than the information supporting it.

This is especially important when decisions become consequential for individuals. A dashboard can help identify where a conversation is needed, but it may not contain enough information to determine what that conversation should conclude. Performance ratings, promotions, reorganizations, and staffing decisions affect people whose work contains far more detail than any organizational metric can represent. The greater the consequence, the stronger the case for descending closer to the territory before deciding what the map means.

The same principle works in the other direction. An engineer may know every technical reason why a particular feature needs another month, while a leader knows that the product will miss a market window if it waits. The technical detail matters, but it does not automatically contain the business decision. Sometimes moving upward reveals constraints that are invisible from the ground.

Wisdom therefore requires movement between altitudes. We zoom out to see patterns, then zoom in when the pattern raises a question. We listen to the person closest to the work, then step back far enough to understand how that work interacts with everything around it. Neither direction is a retreat from reality; both reveal different parts of it.

This is harder than declaring one perspective superior because it requires judgment. There is no universal rule telling us exactly when a dashboard is sufficient and when we need another hour of conversation. We have to ask what decision is being made, how consequential it is, and what information may have been lost in compression. The map can tell us where we are, but sometimes we have to get out of the car.

## What Disappears From the Dashboard

Some of the most valuable work in an organization is remarkably difficult to see from above. An experienced engineer notices a design problem during an informal conversation and prevents months of future rework. A manager spends an hour listening to an employee who is considering leaving and helps them find a path forward. A teammate quietly teaches someone enough about a system that the next five problems are solved without assistance.

These things are not necessarily impossible to measure. We could count mentoring hours, record design interventions, survey employee retention, or create a field for “catastrophes prevented before lunch.” With sufficient determination, organizations can attach numbers to almost anything. The more interesting question is whether turning every valuable act into a reporting category would help us understand the work or simply change the work into something easier to report.

Prevention remains a particularly stubborn example. When an engineer finds a problem before it becomes a defect, there is no customer complaint, no escalation, and perhaps no bug at all. The organization's measurement system may contain no record of the thing that did not happen. Meanwhile, the person who heroically resolves a visible crisis may receive well-deserved recognition for work that leaves a much brighter trail through the data.

This does not mean the crisis solver is less valuable or that prevention should always be rewarded more highly. It means visibility and value are not identical. Some valuable work produces abundant evidence, while other valuable work changes the future by preventing evidence from ever being created. A measurement system can report only what its structure allows it to notice.

The same is true of qualities we readily say we value. Judgment, trust, patience, courage, generosity, curiosity, and the ability to calm a room at the right moment all affect how organizations function. We can gather evidence about them, and sometimes we should. But the fact that something influences the work does not guarantee that it will appear clearly on the dashboard used to summarize the work.

This creates a subtle cultural pressure. What appears on the dashboard is discussed regularly, while what does not appear depends on someone remembering to bring it into the conversation. The visible becomes systematic and the invisible becomes anecdotal. Over time, it is easy for the things we can see consistently to feel more substantial than the things we encounter only in stories.

Again, none of this requires us to throw away the dashboard. The executive still has twenty projects to understand before lunch, and yellow remains a perfectly useful color. The lesson is simply to remember what happened before the color appeared. A complicated reality was compressed so that someone standing far away could see an important part of it.

The danger comes when we spend so much time at altitude that the representation begins to feel like the reality itself. We forget that every yellow box contains people, conversations, technical surprises, acts of judgment, mistakes, recoveries, and circumstances that were intentionally removed so the larger pattern could become visible. The map becomes familiar enough that we stop noticing how much territory is missing.

For most of our working lives, this bargain is unavoidable. We measure because we need to coordinate, simplify because we need to understand, and optimize because we want things to improve. Those habits become part of how competent adults navigate complicated organizations. After enough years, we barely notice that we are doing it.

Then, at five o'clock, we close the laptop and go home.

The dashboard comes with us.
