# Chapter 4 — You Get What You Measure

## Of Course We Want Behavior to Change

Let's give metrics some credit.

They can motivate people.

That's not a bug.

It's often the point.

We've spent a lot of time talking about what happens when people know they're being measured. They pay attention. They change their behavior. They begin making different decisions.

It's tempting to describe all of this as a problem. But imagine the alternative.

An organization decides software quality matters. It begins tracking defects. Engineers become more conscious of defects and start paying closer attention to testing.

Good.

A company realizes customers are waiting too long for support. It starts measuring response time. Support teams notice and begin looking for ways to respond faster.

Also good.

A development organization believes artificial intelligence could make certain kinds of work more efficient. It starts paying attention to whether employees are experimenting with AI tools. People who might otherwise have ignored the technology give it a try.

That might be exactly what leadership hoped would happen.

Measurement focuses attention. Attention can change behavior. Changed behavior can produce better outcomes.

This is not some unfortunate side effect we discovered after inventing the dashboard.

It's one of the reasons we built the dashboard.

Organizations want to improve. People want to improve. Improvement requires change.

We talk about having a growth mindset. We encourage continuous improvement. We look for inefficiencies and try to remove them. We identify weaknesses and work on them. We experiment with new tools, new processes, and new ways of working.

All of that requires behavior to change.

You cannot optimize a system while insisting that everyone continue doing exactly what they've always done.

Something has to move.

That's where measurement becomes powerful.

Suppose a team wants to reduce the amount of time it takes to release software. Before changing anything, it measures the current process.

Twenty-three days.

Then the team changes something.

Nineteen days.

Interesting.

Change something else.

Fourteen days.

Now the metric isn't merely describing the process. It's providing feedback.

Did that help?

Did that make things worse?

Are we moving in the direction we intended?

Without measurement, improvement can become surprisingly subjective.

“We're definitely faster now.”

Are we?

“It feels faster.”

Excellent. I'll update the quarterly report.

**Velocity: feels faster.**

Measurement gives us a way to challenge our impressions. It lets reality push back. And once people can see the feedback, it can become motivating.

Anyone who has watched a progress bar knows this.

You walk 8,000 steps. Your watch says you're close to your goal.

Fine.

You walk around the block.

The little circle closes.

Something inside your brain experiences an embarrassingly sincere sense of accomplishment because a tiny digital ring is now complete.

Ridiculous?

Maybe.

Effective?

Apparently.

Human beings respond to feedback. We respond to goals. We respond to visible progress. We like knowing whether our effort is producing results.

A useful metric can turn something vague like:

**We should improve quality**

into something more concrete:

**Defects have fallen 25 percent over the last six months.**

Now we can see movement.

We can ask what caused it. We can reinforce practices that seem to be working. We can recognize people who helped.

We can set another goal.

This is one reason metrics become so attractive in organizations. They don't merely describe improvement. They can participate in it.

And there is nothing inherently wrong with that.

Sometimes discussions about metrics become so focused on manipulation and gaming that we forget something important:

**Motivation is not the enemy.**

We want people to care about outcomes. We want teams to notice problems. We want organizations to learn. We want behavior to change when the current behavior isn't producing what we need.

If measuring something helps that happen, wonderful.

The question is not whether a metric influences behavior.

The question is:

**Which behavior does it influence?**

If measuring response time motivates a support team to remove unnecessary delays, the metric is doing useful work.

If it motivates support agents to rush customers off the phone before their problems are solved, something has gone wrong.

If measuring defects motivates engineers to improve testing, excellent.

If it motivates people to argue that a bug isn't technically a bug because classifying it as a bug would hurt the numbers, we have wandered into different territory.

If measuring AI adoption encourages people to experiment with tools they might otherwise have ignored, the metric may have helped overcome inertia.

If six months later employees are feeding pointless tasks into AI because everyone knows the adoption number needs to go up, we should probably ask a few questions.

In each case, the metric succeeded at motivating behavior. The problem wasn't that people responded to it.

**The problem was what they learned to do in order to make it move.**

This is where the conversation about measurement needs more nuance. A metric can be motivational without being manipulative.

A target can focus effort without becoming tyrannical.

A dashboard can encourage improvement without replacing judgment.

But none of this happens automatically. The metric has to remain connected to the outcome that gave it meaning in the first place.

That's the relationship we need to watch.

Because usually, when a metric is first introduced, there is a perfectly reasonable reason for choosing it. The behavior we're measuring really does seem connected to the outcome we want.

More testing really can improve quality.

Faster response really can improve customer experience.

Using a new tool really can produce new efficiencies.

The correlation isn't imaginary. In fact, that's precisely why we chose the metric.

And that's where things get interesting.

Because once we've found something that seems to produce a better outcome, corporate culture knows exactly what to do next.

We optimize it.

## The Correlation Was Real

Most bad metrics probably didn't begin as bad ideas.

Someone noticed something.

Teams that test their software more thoroughly tend to have fewer defects.

Salespeople who spend more time talking with customers tend to sell more.

Projects that receive regular code reviews tend to produce better software.

Employees who use a new tool effectively sometimes finish certain kinds of work faster.

These relationships can be real.

Hopefully, before an organization builds an entire performance system around one of them, someone has done more than notice a pattern over lunch.

There may be research.

There may be historical data.

There may be controlled experiments.

There may be years of operational experience showing that certain behaviors tend to produce certain outcomes.

This matters because it's easy to tell stories about ridiculous corporate metrics as though they were invented by someone who had recently suffered a head injury.

Sometimes that's probably unfair.

The original reasoning may have been quite good. Suppose an engineering organization studies its development process and discovers something interesting.

Teams that perform thorough code reviews have significantly fewer defects after release.

That makes sense.

Another engineer looking at the code may catch mistakes the original author missed. Review creates an opportunity to question assumptions. Knowledge spreads between engineers. Design problems surface earlier, when they're cheaper to fix.

So leadership says:

**We should make sure code gets reviewed.**

Excellent.

That's a behavior.

And if the evidence is strong enough, encouraging that behavior may be a very good idea.

Maybe the organization introduces a requirement that production code receive review before being merged.

Behavior changes.

More code gets reviewed.

Defects decline.

The intervention worked. This is measurement doing exactly what we want it to do.

We observed reality. We identified a behavior associated with a better outcome. We encouraged the behavior. The outcome improved.

Everyone may now enjoy a brief moment of organizational happiness.

Unfortunately, someone is eventually going to ask for a dashboard.

How do we know people are doing code reviews?

Good question.

We need a metric.

Perhaps we measure the percentage of changes that receive review.

Now we have a proxy for the behavior.

That's still useful.

If 97 percent of changes are being reviewed, we have some reason to believe the practice has become widespread.

If the number suddenly falls to 62 percent, we might want to investigate.

So far, so good.

Then comes the subtle transition.

We stop saying:

**Code review tends to improve software quality, so we want engineers to review important changes carefully.**

And begin saying:

**Code review compliance must remain above 95 percent.**

Those sentences sound almost identical inside an organization.

They are not.

The first statement is about behavior and purpose The second is about a number.

Once the number becomes the requirement, the optimization problem changes.

An engineer no longer needs to ask:

**Did this code receive a meaningful review?**

The system asks:

**Did this code receive a review that counts?**

Those can be very different questions.

Perhaps clicking “Approve” counts.

Perhaps a review lasting thirty seconds counts.

Perhaps asking someone to glance at a trivial change counts exactly as much as a thoughtful review of a complicated architectural decision.

The dashboard cannot necessarily tell the difference. It knows that a review occurred. And the organization asked for reviews. So the number looks fantastic.

This is the strange journey of a proxy.

It begins with something real.

**Thoughtful code review tends to improve software quality.**

Then we need a way to observe whether code review is happening. So we create a measurable representation.

**Percentage of changes reviewed.**

Then we want improvement.

So we create a target.

**95 percent.**

Then we want accountability.

So we attach consequences.

Now everyone knows the number matters.

At each step, the decision makes sense. That's what makes this problem interesting.

There doesn't have to be a villain.

Nobody has to announce:

**Today we shall replace meaningful engineering practice with meaningless bureaucracy.**

The transformation happens gradually. Behavior becomes measurement Measurement becomes target.

Target becomes requirement.

And somewhere along the way, the thing we can count begins acquiring more authority than the thing we actually wanted.

This is why correlation matters.

A good proxy should have some meaningful relationship to the reality it represents.

If there is no relationship, the metric is simply bad.

But even a very good relationship has limits.

Suppose we discover that engineers on healthy teams tend to review code carefully.

That does not necessarily mean forcing the review count upward will produce equally healthy teams.

The direction matters.

This is an old problem in reasoning.

People carrying umbrellas are more likely to be standing in the rain.

Handing out more umbrellas does not cause additional rain.

Corporate metrics are usually more complicated than umbrellas and rain, but the underlying caution remains useful.

A relationship between two things does not automatically tell us what will happen when we deliberately manipulate one of them.

Sometimes it works.

Sometimes it works spectacularly.

Sometimes it works for a while.

And sometimes people discover how to move the measurable thing without producing the outcome that originally made it valuable.

That's why I think there is an important difference between **encouraging a behavior** and **enforcing a number**.

If research tells us that a particular behavior reliably improves an outcome, encourage the behavior.

Teach it.

Make room for it.

Remove obstacles to it.

Explain why it matters.

Build it into the way work gets done when appropriate.

Hold people accountable for doing it when the stakes justify that.

But be careful about replacing the behavior with its numerical shadow.

Because the moment the shadow becomes the requirement, people have a new problem to solve.

They no longer have to figure out how to produce the outcome.

They have to figure out how to produce the number.

And these are smart people.

We hired them because they're good at solving problems.

They work inside organizations that constantly tell them to become more efficient.

Reduce waste.

Increase throughput.

Automate repetitive work.

Find the shortest path.

Optimize.

Then we give them a metric and say:

**This is how we'll know you're succeeding.**

We should not be shocked by what happens next.

They optimize it.
