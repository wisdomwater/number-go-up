# 4 — The View From Above

There is a point in almost every career when the work begins to look different because you are no longer standing in the same place. Early on, you may know one piece of a system in extraordinary detail: the code you wrote, the customer you support, the machine you operate, or the project sitting directly in front of you. As responsibility grows, the field of view expands, but something has to give. You see more things and fewer details about each of them. This isn't necessarily a loss of competence. It is a change in altitude.

An engineer may spend an afternoon tracing a failure through thousands of lines of code, learning exactly which sequence of events causes something to go wrong once every several hours. The manager responsible for that engineer may know only that an important technical problem has been open for three weeks and threatens a milestone. A director looking across several teams may see the entire project represented as yellow, while an executive looking across dozens of projects may see one yellow box among a sea of green and red.

All of these descriptions can be true at the same time. The engineer knows things the executive doesn't know, but the executive can also see things the engineer cannot. From the ground, I can see individual trees but may have no idea where the forest ends; from above, the river and ridgeline become obvious while the texture of the bark disappears. The ground reveals detail. Altitude reveals pattern.

This creates one of the recurring tensions of organizational life. The people closest to the work sometimes look upward and wonder how leadership can possibly make good decisions without understanding what is really happening, while leaders look downward and wonder why people can't see the larger constraints affecting the decision. Both frustrations can be legitimate because the two groups are being asked to see different things. The challenge is not deciding which altitude is correct, but knowing what each altitude makes visible.

### You Can't Be Everywhere

Direct experience stops scaling surprisingly quickly. A manager with five employees can have regular conversations with each of them, understand much of their work, and develop a reasonably detailed sense of what is happening. Give that manager fifty people and the relationship changes, even if they are exceptionally attentive. Give an executive fifty thousand employees and the suggestion that they should simply “talk to people more” begins to encounter the inconvenient limits of arithmetic.

There are only so many conversations a person can have in a day. Even if an executive spent every working hour talking with employees, most of the organization would remain people they had never met doing work they had never personally observed. The same problem applies to products, customers, budgets, technical decisions, schedules, and risks. At sufficient scale, nobody gets to know the whole thing firsthand.

So information travels upward in compressed form. An engineer describes a technical problem to a lead, the lead summarizes it for a manager, the manager reports the project status to a director, and eventually the situation may arrive before an executive as a colored indicator on a slide. Details disappear at every step because they have to. If every level preserved everything known at the level below it, the executive review would require several fiscal quarters to complete.

This can be funny when you are living near the bottom of the compression stack. You may have spent two weeks learning that a failure requires a particular firmware version, a certain workload, an unusual timing condition, and what appears to be an interaction between components owned by two different teams. You explain the whole thing carefully to your manager, perhaps accompanied by diagrams and several sentences containing acronyms you have long since stopped realizing are acronyms. Somewhere three organizational layers later, your life's work becomes:

**GPU issue — yellow.**

It is tempting to feel insulted by that reduction, especially after spending two weeks becoming the world's leading expert on this particular failure. But the yellow box may be doing exactly what it was designed to do. The executive probably does not need the register values, reproduction steps, or history of every hypothesis the team has eliminated. They need to know whether the project is at risk, how significant the risk is, and whether a decision or additional resource is required.

The detail that makes the problem understandable to the engineer may make the larger organization impossible to understand. That is the trade of altitude. As we rise, individual features disappear and larger patterns emerge. The engineer may be the only person who understands exactly why this failure occurs, while a leader looking across several organizations may be the only person positioned to notice that six projects are struggling with the same dependency. Neither person is seeing more reality. They are seeing different parts of it.

### The Necessary Art of Compression

This ability to compress information is one of the reasons large organizations can exist at all. Thousands of people can work on different pieces of a product without requiring everyone to understand everything everyone else knows. Teams create interfaces, schedules, summaries, specifications, forecasts, and status reports that allow information to cross boundaries. Each representation preserves what seems important for the next decision and leaves the rest behind.

Imagine trying to eliminate that loss entirely. An executive dashboard could contain every employee's current work, every technical decision, every customer interaction, every defect, every risk, every budget transaction, and every conversation relevant to every project. We could congratulate ourselves on finally achieving perfect organizational visibility. Unfortunately, we would have recreated the company.

A useful summary has to be smaller than the thing it summarizes. That sounds obvious, but it is easy to forget when we are the person whose favorite detail disappeared. The engineer wants the subtle technical distinction preserved because it matters enormously from where they are standing; the executive needs the distinction removed because twenty other projects are waiting. Neither desire is unreasonable.

This is why criticism of dashboards can become too easy. Managers do not usually reduce complicated work to a handful of indicators because they are incapable of appreciating complexity or secretly wish human beings were spreadsheet cells. They do it because complexity is real, time is limited, and decisions still have to be made. The alternative to imperfect information is rarely perfect information; more often, it is making the same decision while knowing even less.

Good compression is therefore a form of judgment. It asks what this person needs to know, at this moment, to make this decision. The answer changes depending on where the person stands and what responsibility they carry. A useful abstraction is not the smallest amount of information possible. It is enough information for the job at hand. The difficulty comes when the job changes but the resolution doesn't.

### Managers Need the Map. Employees Live in the Territory.

Imagine that yellow project again. From the director's perspective, yellow means the schedule is at risk, another team may be depending on it, and someone needs to understand whether a recovery plan is possible. Those are legitimate concerns. Commitments have been made, other work may be waiting, and pretending the schedule is green would help no one.

Inside the project, however, yellow has a history. Perhaps the team discovered that an assumption made six months earlier was wrong and had the judgment to stop rather than continue building on it. Perhaps another group found a quality problem that everyone agrees was worth fixing properly, even though the discovery cost three weeks. Perhaps the original schedule was simply more optimistic than anyone realized at the time.

None of those details makes the project less yellow. The schedule can still be at risk. But the details may completely change what a wise response looks like.

A team that is late because it ignored known problems may need something very different from a team that is late because it uncovered an important problem nobody knew existed. A project that needs more people is different from one where adding people would merely create additional meetings for the existing people to attend. The status tells us where attention may be needed; the story helps us decide what kind of attention.

This is where the view from above and the view from the ground can collide. The employee living inside the full-resolution story hears the simplified description and thinks, *They have no idea what's happening here.* The leader looking across twenty projects hears ten minutes of technical explanation and thinks, *I understand that this is complicated, but I still have nineteen other projects to manage.* Both reactions make sense.

The phrase I keep returning to is simple: **Managers need the map. Employees live in the territory.** The manager cannot abandon the map because the territory is too large to experience directly. The employee cannot abandon the territory because it is where the work actually happens. A healthy organization needs both perspectives without pretending they are interchangeable.

That means information has to move in both directions. We tend to imagine organizational communication as an upward process: employees provide status, managers aggregate it, and executives make decisions. But context also has to travel downward, so the person doing the work understands the larger constraints, and detail sometimes has to travel back upward when the decision requires more resolution. Good leadership is partly knowing when a yellow box is enough and when it is time to ask someone to tell you the story behind it.

### The Right Altitude for the Decision

Not every decision deserves the same amount of information. If I want to know whether to carry an umbrella this afternoon, a weather app showing a high probability of rain is probably sufficient. If I am designing a bridge expected to withstand a hundred years of weather, “looks rainy” is not an acceptable meteorological model. The appropriate resolution depends upon what I am trying to decide.

Organizations get into trouble when information gathered at one altitude is used to make a decision that belongs at another. A broad quality trend might be enough to tell a leader that several teams should examine their development process. It probably isn't enough to tell that leader exactly what a particular engineer should have done differently on Tuesday afternoon. The information didn't suddenly become inaccurate; the decision became more specific than the information supporting it.

The same issue becomes especially important when decisions affect individual people. A high-level view may tell us where a conversation is warranted, but performance, promotion, staffing, or organizational decisions often require information that disappeared before the issue reached the dashboard. The greater the consequence and the more specific the judgment, the stronger the reason to descend closer to the ground.

The principle works in the other direction too. An engineer can understand every technical reason a feature needs another month and still be unaware that waiting a month would miss a market window that changes the entire business case. More technical detail will not answer that question because the missing information exists at a different altitude. Sometimes the person on the ground needs to look up.

This is one of the reasons experienced leaders become good at moving between levels of resolution. They zoom out far enough to see patterns, then descend when a pattern raises a question the summary cannot answer. They listen to the person closest to the work and then step back far enough to understand how that work interacts with everything around it. The skill isn't choosing the correct altitude once and remaining there. It is knowing when to move.

There is no formula for this, which is mildly inconvenient in a book about our love of formulas. Judgment enters precisely because no dashboard can tell us how much dashboard is appropriate for every decision. We have to consider the consequence, the uncertainty, the people involved, and what may have disappeared during compression. Sometimes the map is sufficient. Sometimes we have to get out of the car.

### What We Learn to See

Spend enough years inside organizations and this way of thinking becomes remarkably natural. We learn to move between detail and summary without consciously noticing the transition. A technical discussion becomes a status update, a status update becomes a project risk, and a project risk becomes one item in a portfolio. We understand that none of these descriptions is complete, but each can be useful from the right position.

This ability is part of professional maturity. Early in a career, I may care intensely about whether my particular piece of work is correct; later, I also have to care whether it arrives when someone else needs it, whether the larger product works, whether the customer wants it, and whether the organization can afford to build it. My field of concern expands. Some details have to become background so other realities can come into view.

The danger is subtle because nothing has obviously gone wrong. The dashboard works. The project review works. The abstractions allow thousands of people to coordinate work no single person could understand completely. We become so fluent in turning complicated realities into manageable representations that eventually it feels like the responsible way to approach almost anything.

We learn to ask what the goal is, how we will know whether we're making progress, which indicators deserve attention, and what action should follow when the number moves. Those are excellent questions. They help us build products, run organizations, solve problems, and become people others trust with increasingly complicated responsibilities.

After twenty or thirty years, we barely notice ourselves asking them. Then the workday ends. We close the laptop, drive home, and carry the same mind with us.
