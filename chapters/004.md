# 4 - You Get What You Measure

### Of Course We Want Behavior to Change

At this point, it would be easy to conclude that the safest metric is one nobody knows about. Measure quietly, keep the dashboard hidden, and whatever you do, don't let the people being measured discover that the number exists.

That would solve one problem by creating several others, because one of the reasons we measure things is precisely that we want behavior to change. Suppose a software organization takes twenty-three days, on average, to move a completed change into production. The team studies the process, notices several unnecessary handoffs, removes them, and gets the number down to nineteen days; another improvement brings it to fourteen.

This is measurement doing exactly what we hoped it would do. Without the number, everyone might have a vague sense that releases are getting faster, but “velocity: feels faster” is not especially useful as an operating model. With measurement, we can change something, observe what happens, and learn whether our intervention actually helped.

That feedback loop is one of the great strengths of measurement. Athletes use it to improve performance, engineers use it to make systems faster and more reliable, businesses use it to remove waste, and scientists use it to find out whether an idea survives contact with reality. The fact that measurement changes behavior is not, by itself, a problem; often changing behavior is the whole point.

The more interesting question is which behavior changes.

Consider code review. Software engineers frequently ask another engineer to look over a change before it becomes part of the product, and a good review can catch a bug, expose a risky assumption, reveal a security problem, or simply force the author to explain the design clearly enough that a weakness becomes obvious. None of this is mystical. Thoughtful review really can improve software.

Suppose an organization notices that teams doing regular code review tend to produce fewer defects. Management encourages the practice, more changes receive review, and defects decline. Wonderful. We have observed something useful, encouraged a behavior that seems connected to an outcome we care about, and watched the outcome improve.

Then someone asks for a dashboard.

### The Correlation Was Real

The request isn't unreasonable. If code review appears to be helping, leadership will naturally want to know whether it is happening consistently. Maybe some teams have adopted it while others haven't, or perhaps review rates fall whenever schedules get tight and everyone starts feeling hurried.

So we begin measuring code-review compliance. At first perhaps only 40 percent of changes receive a formal review, then 65 percent, then 80, then 90. Quality improves along the way, and every step seems to reinforce the idea that the organization has found a useful lever.

Eventually the number becomes important enough that someone establishes a target: code-review compliance should remain above 95 percent.

Nothing dramatic has happened. No executive has walked into a room and announced that meaningful engineering practice will henceforth be replaced by ceremonial clicking. Every decision in the sequence has been defensible, and that is precisely why this kind of problem is so easy to create.

Originally, the question was whether an important change received a meaningful second set of eyes. The system is now increasingly asking whether the change received a review that counts.

Those questions overlap, but they are not identical.

A careful thirty-minute conversation between two engineers may be enormously valuable and still fail whatever mechanism records an official review. Meanwhile, a two-second approval in the correct tool may satisfy the metric perfectly. The dashboard can tell us that a review occurred; depending on how it was designed, it may know very little about whether anyone actually thought.

The original correlation was not imaginary. That's important, because bad metrics are often discussed as though they must have begun as stupid ideas. In practice, many of them begin as useful observations.

Code review really did help quality. The organization encouraged more of it, and quality improved. The metric appeared to confirm that the intervention was working, so naturally everyone became more confident in the metric.

And then, just as naturally, they pushed harder.

At first, that may work beautifully. Moving review coverage from 40 percent to 70 percent could capture a great deal of obvious value as changes that once received no scrutiny begin getting meaningful review. Moving from 70 to 85 percent may continue helping.

But the easy gains do not necessarily continue forever. Eventually most of the obvious valuable behavior is already happening while the target keeps moving, and the organization faces a choice it may not realize it is making. It can continue the difficult work of improving the underlying practice, or it can discover cheaper ways of improving the measurable representation of the practice.

Human beings are quite good at finding the cheaper route.

This doesn't require dishonesty. It requires optimization.

Project estimation provides another version of the same story. Organizations want predictable delivery for perfectly sensible reasons: customers make plans, other teams depend on dates, budgets have to be allocated, and managers need some idea when things will arrive.

So teams estimate their work, and the organization begins comparing those estimates with actual delivery. Missed commitments turn red, red numbers attract attention, and teams that consistently hit their dates begin to look well managed.

Again, none of this is absurd.

But eventually teams learn something about the environment. If estimating six weeks and taking seven produces an unpleasant conversation, while estimating eight weeks and finishing in seven produces a pleasant one, people will begin to prefer estimates with a little more room in them. They may call that responsible risk management, management may call it sandbagging, and both descriptions may contain some truth.

The organization asked for predictability, measured predictability, and rewarded predictability.

People became more predictable.

The number improved, but the meaning of the improvement became less obvious.

This is the central difficulty of optimization. A proxy can be genuinely related to something we care about and still become less useful as we push harder on it, because our ability to optimize the proxy may eventually outrun the proxy's ability to represent the outcome. The relationship that justified the measure in the first place can remain real even as the measure becomes easier to improve independently.

At that point, the shortest path to a better number no longer necessarily passes through the thing we originally wanted.

And organizations are very good at discovering short paths.

### Local Optimization, Global Absurdity

The problem becomes more interesting when different parts of the organization are optimizing different things.

Imagine a software company in which development wants to deliver features quickly, quality wants fewer defects, security wants less risk, operations wants stability, finance wants lower cost, and management wants predictability. None of these goals is foolish; in fact, I would be worried about a company that did not care about them.

Each group therefore creates processes and measurements around the responsibility it has been given.

Security notices that some releases contain vulnerabilities, so the company adds a security review before release. Security risk declines, which is good, but releases now take longer.

Development adapts. Instead of sending many small changes through the review process, teams begin bundling work together so they can pay the approval cost less often. Development's local efficiency improves, but the larger releases are harder to test and riskier to deploy.

Operations notices the increased deployment risk and adds more pre-release testing. Stability improves, but release time grows again.

Management then notices that delivery has become less predictable, so it introduces additional checkpoints, milestone reviews, and status updates. Predictability improves somewhat, but engineers now spend more time in meetings and reporting systems.

Eventually someone schedules a meeting to discuss why productivity is declining.

Attendance is mandatory.

The joke works because nobody had to behave irrationally to get there. Security did its job. Operations did its job. Development responded to friction. Management responded to declining predictability, and each group may even be able to point to a dashboard showing improvement in the thing it was responsible for.

Security incidents are down. Operational failures are down. Planning compliance is up.

And the overall system may still be miserable.

Software engineers encounter this kind of problem all the time in technical systems. You can make one component faster while slowing the application as a whole, or reduce memory consumption in one subsystem by forcing another part of the system to do more work. The local metric improves while the larger experience does not.

Organizations behave the same way, except their components have budgets, managers, reporting structures, and vice presidents.

The trouble is not merely that different departments optimize different things. Pressure also changes shape as it travels through the hierarchy.

Suppose a senior executive concludes that the company needs to improve delivery speed. That priority moves to division leaders, who ask directors what they are doing to improve speed; directors ask managers; managers begin examining the numbers their teams can influence.

Eventually an engineer is asked why one bug took nine days to close.

Nobody in that chain has to be malicious. The executive may be worried about competitiveness, the director may be responding to a legitimate organizational priority, the manager may be trying to understand team performance, and the engineer may simply be working through a difficult problem.

But the message changes as it moves downward. “Improve delivery speed” becomes “reduce cycle time,” which eventually becomes “why is this number red?”

The engineer learns something from that interaction. Closing bugs quickly is safe.

The next time two bugs are waiting—one easy and one complicated—the easy bug has acquired an advantage that has nothing to do with how important it is to the product. The employee is not the only person adapting to the metric; managers are adapting to the numbers above them, departments are adapting to organizational commitments, and the organization gradually becomes a network of locally reasonable decisions.

Then we look at the aggregate behavior and wonder why everyone is acting so strangely.

This is where one of the questions from the previous chapter becomes useful again: **What behavior have we made rational?**

I find that more productive than immediately asking why people are gaming the system. If an entire sales organization begins delaying deals until the next quarter, something about the incentive structure may deserve attention. If engineers systematically avoid difficult bugs, perhaps the way we talk about closure time matters. If managers consistently pad schedules, perhaps we have made missing a date more dangerous than producing an honest estimate.

Behavior is information too.

That does not mean individuals are absolved of responsibility whenever incentives are involved. People can be lazy, evasive, dishonest, or simply wrong, and no amount of systems thinking should require us to pretend otherwise. But when hundreds of otherwise capable people begin displaying the same strange behavior, it is worth considering whether they have learned something about the environment we created.

The system may be teaching people how to survive inside it.

And sometimes the most revealing moment comes when a person does exactly what we want them to do and the metric punishes them for it.

### Sometimes the Metric Needs to Lose

Return to the customer-service example from the previous chapter. The organization wants calls resolved efficiently, and it has established an eight-minute target because long calls often signal poor training, clumsy tools, unnecessary process, or customers who are being passed around without getting help.

Then one day a representative receives a genuinely difficult call.

The customer's situation touches several systems. There is an error that needs to be investigated, corrected, and explained, and the representative has to make sure the customer understands what to do next. Thirty-seven minutes later, the problem is solved.

The customer is happy.

The call-duration metric is not.

What should happen next is not especially mysterious. Someone should probably ask why the call took thirty-seven minutes.

That is what the metric is for.

Perhaps the representative needs more training, or the internal tools are terrible, or twenty minutes were wasted because information had to be re-entered in three systems. If any of those things are true, the ugly number has helped expose a real problem.

But it is also possible that this particular customer simply needed thirty-seven minutes of help.

If so, we have reached an important moment in the life of a metric. The number is bad, and the decision was still right.

A useful test of any measurement system is whether it can survive that possibility. Can an employee make the metric worse and still have exercised excellent judgment? Can a team miss a target and have done exactly what we would want them to do in the same circumstances? Can a manager look at a red box, understand the story behind it, and say, “Yes, that was the right call”?

If the answer is always no, then the metric is no longer helping judgment. It has begun replacing it.

This does not mean targets become optional whenever reality is inconvenient. That would be an extremely comfortable interpretation of this book.

“I know every number is terrible, but Michael says context matters.”

Nice try.

Sometimes the number looks bad because something actually went badly. A thirty-seven-minute call may have taken far too long, a project may have slipped because the team planned poorly, and a bug may have remained open because nobody treated it with enough urgency.

Accountability still matters. The difference is where the conversation begins.

A metric used as information tells us that something differed from what we expected and invites us to find out why. A metric used as a verdict assumes the deviation has already explained itself.

That distinction matters because healthy systems sometimes produce ugly numbers.

A hospital trying to reduce wait times may have a terrible afternoon because a critically ill patient arrives and requires enormous attention. The wait-time metric gets worse, but it would be strange to conclude that the hospital therefore made a bad decision.

That doesn't mean waiting suddenly became good. It means the purpose of the target was always larger than the target itself.

We wanted shorter waits because we wanted better care. The moment shortening the wait conflicts with caring properly for a patient, the larger purpose has to matter more.

This is where judgment re-enters the picture, and judgment is inconvenient because it does not scale as neatly as a rule. “Eight minutes” is wonderfully easy to communicate. “Aim for eight minutes because it is usually a useful indicator, except when the circumstances justify something else, and then explain why” is harder to put in a dashboard.

Unfortunately, it is also much closer to reality.

And we should notice that judgment was present before the employee ever received the thirty-seven-minute call. Someone chose call duration as the metric, someone decided eight minutes was desirable, someone decided how that number would be reported, and someone decided what consequences would follow when it was missed.

Those were judgments too.

Once embedded in a dashboard, however, those earlier decisions can start looking like properties of nature. Eight minutes becomes less like a management choice and more like the boiling point of water.

Allowing the metric to lose is not abandoning rigor. It is remembering that the number has always been part of a larger act of human judgment.

### Optimize for the Point

Suppose a software team has committed to releasing a product on Friday. On Wednesday, the team discovers a serious architectural problem.

Now they have a decision to make. They can ship on Friday and accept the risk, or delay the release and fix the problem properly.

The schedule can tell us that Friday was the commitment. It can tell us how much work remains, what other projects depend on that date, and perhaps what the delay will cost.

All of that is valuable.

What the schedule cannot tell us is whether the architectural problem is serious enough to justify missing the commitment, whether the team is being appropriately cautious or unnecessarily perfectionistic, or what a failure would mean for customers.

Judgment has to hold those things together.

When a metric begins producing strange behavior, it is often useful to walk backward from the number toward the reason we started measuring it.

Take test coverage.

Why do we care about test coverage? Presumably because we want engineers to test the software. Why do we care about testing? Because testing helps us find problems before customers do. Why does that matter? Because we want reliable software, and people depend on the systems we build.

Now we have arrived somewhere much closer to the point.

From there we can walk forward again. Does writing this test make the software more reliable? If yes, excellent. If we are writing a meaningless test solely to move coverage from 94.8 percent to 95, then perhaps we have allowed the proxy to become detached from the reason it existed.

The purpose should remain relatively stable while the proxy remains negotiable.

Optimization tends to reverse that order. We begin with something that matters, find a measurable behavior associated with improvement, and push the number upward. The underlying outcome gets better, which increases our confidence in the metric, so we push harder still.

Eventually the number can become an objective in its own right while the original purpose recedes into the background.

That is how a company can sincerely value quality while rewarding people for shipping too quickly, or sincerely value customers while pressuring employees to end calls, or sincerely value thoughtful engineering while making difficult bugs dangerous to pick up. Nobody has to stop caring about the larger purpose. They only have to become more accountable for the proxy than for the thing the proxy represents.

The way back is not to stop optimizing. Optimization is useful.

The way back is to keep the optimization attached to the purpose.

If the shortest path to a better number still passes through the outcome we care about, the metric is probably helping. When those paths diverge, something has to give: perhaps the target needs to change, perhaps the incentive needs to change, perhaps more context needs to be added, or perhaps someone simply needs permission to exercise judgment and let the number look bad.

That requires something from both sides of the dashboard.

Managers need to create enough room for a person to say, “I know this number looks bad, but here's what happened,” without making the sentence feel like the opening statement in a criminal trial. Employees, for their part, need to explain the number honestly and accept that context is not magic; sometimes the explanation will not be good enough.

Context is not an escape from accountability, and measurement is not an escape from judgment.

They have to remain in conversation.

When the metric and lived experience disagree, we do not need to decide in advance which one deserves to win. We can ask what the number sees that the person does not, what the person sees that the number cannot, and then make a judgment about the thing we actually care about.

That was the whole point of the metric in the first place.

A company does not ultimately need faster bug closure. It needs better software. A hospital does not ultimately need shorter wait times. It needs to care for patients well. A customer-service organization does not ultimately need shorter calls. It needs to help customers effectively.

The measurements matter because those larger things matter first.

That means we can still track the numbers seriously. We can set targets, use data to challenge comfortable stories, and hold people accountable when accountability is warranted. None of that requires pretending the proxy and the purpose are the same thing.

Every once in a while, the two will come apart.

When they do, the number should still have a seat at the table. It may be telling us something important, and we should listen carefully.

But it does not get the final word.

Reality does.
