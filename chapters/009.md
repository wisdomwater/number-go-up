# 9 — Nickels and Noses

I have spent a lot of time playing music in church. Sometimes I play drums, sometimes piano, and like any musician I want to play well. You practice, learn the arrangement, pay attention to tempo, listen to the other musicians, and try not to introduce an unexpected percussion solo during the quietest moment of the service. Competence matters because a room full of people can tell surprisingly quickly when the band has not done its homework.

There is quite a lot about music that can be measured. Tempo has a number, pitch has a frequency, volume has a level, and a drummer who consistently speeds up during the chorus can be shown fairly convincing evidence of the problem. Rehearsal works for many of the same reasons mastery works elsewhere: we isolate weak spots, repeat difficult transitions, listen to recordings, make adjustments, and gradually become better at what we are trying to do. Measurement is not an intrusion into music; used well, it can help us become better musicians.

But every once in a while, something happens that has very little to do with whether I played everything correctly. A song settles into the room differently, the musicians begin listening to one another in a way that cannot quite be rehearsed, and the congregation seems unusually present. Maybe I experience it strongly and someone else does not, so I have learned to be cautious about declaring what happened on behalf of everyone else. Still, there are moments when music seems to become more than the successful execution of music.

I can prepare for those moments without being able to produce them. I can practice the piano, learn the chords, improve my timing, and show up ready to play; the sound engineer can balance the room, the musicians can rehearse, and the service can be planned with considerable care. All of those things matter, and doing them poorly can certainly get in the way. What we cannot do is write “10:43 a.m. — congregation experiences transcendence” into the service plan and expect the schedule to make it happen.

That distinction has become increasingly important to me because church occupies an unusual place in a book about measurement. A church is an organization, and organizations need information. It has budgets, buildings, employees, volunteers, schedules, programs, equipment, and enough ordinary logistical problems to keep anyone from becoming excessively ethereal. Yet the organization exists in service of realities that are much harder to represent on a dashboard.

Church needs the numbers.

It just needs to remember what the numbers are for.

## We Were Just Playing Music

One of the pleasures of becoming more competent as a musician is that technique gradually stops consuming all of your attention. When I am struggling simply to play the notes, most of my awareness is occupied by my own hands. As the music becomes more familiar, I can begin hearing the bass player more clearly, noticing where the singer is going, and responding to whether the room seems to need more sound or less. Skill, at its best, creates freedom to pay attention.

This is why I don't find a simple opposition between mastery and spiritual experience very convincing. The inexperienced musician may be wonderfully spontaneous while making everyone else on stage wish for a metronome. Years of discipline can make a musician more capable of responding to what is happening because less attention is required merely to remain competent. Mastery does not necessarily close us to experience; sometimes it creates the conditions in which we can become more available to it.

What mastery cannot do is own what happens next. I can play the same song on two different Sundays with roughly the same arrangement, tempo, musicians, and level of preparation, and the experiences can feel entirely different. Sometimes the song simply goes well, everyone does their job, and we move on to the next part of the service, which is a perfectly respectable outcome. Sometimes something happens that none of us can quite claim credit for.

Religious language can become dangerous here because it is easy to attach divine approval to whatever produces the strongest emotion. A swelling chord progression, dim lighting, or a talented singer can move people quite reliably, and emotional intensity is not the same thing as spiritual depth. Churches can learn the mechanics of creating an experience just as competently as any theater or concert venue. The fact that something feels powerful does not automatically tell us what happened.

But skepticism can become its own kind of reduction. Explaining the acoustics, psychology, musicianship, and lighting does not exhaust what a person may have experienced any more than knowing how a piano works exhausts the experience of hearing music. Someone may remember a person they lost, feel a burden they have carried begin to loosen, become aware of gratitude, or simply experience a few minutes of stillness in a week that contained very little of it. I do not need to decide exactly what happened in order to respect that something may have happened which the technical description does not contain.

Music has taught me to hold both realities together. Preparation matters. Technique matters. The microphones should work, the musicians should know the songs, and starting approximately together remains a worthwhile aspiration. Yet all that competence finally exists in service of something it cannot guarantee.

That is not a failure of mastery.

It is a boundary.

## Then the Megachurch Moved In

At one point, a large church moved into our area. It would make for a cleaner story if I described it as a soulless religious corporation descending upon the neighborhood with smoke machines and a marketing department, but that would not be fair. They had excellent musicians, strong programs, resources for families, and plenty of people sincerely trying to serve their community. Some children enjoyed the place so much that their parents probably needed a strategic extraction plan to get them home.

Still, the arrival of a much larger church changes the environment around smaller churches. You become aware of what they offer, how many people attend, what their music sounds like, what they can provide for families, and what becomes possible with resources you do not have. None of that requires jealousy or bad motives. Comparison can enter a room occupied entirely by decent people.

And once comparison enters, measurement is rarely far behind.

Churches have always counted things because churches have always had practical responsibilities. Buildings cost money, employees need salaries, programs need volunteers, and rooms have capacities that fire marshals remain stubbornly unwilling to calculate in units of spiritual enthusiasm. If two hundred people regularly attend and suddenly only one hundred do, leadership should probably notice. You cannot pay the electric bill with transcendence.

There is an old church phrase for the measurements that tend to dominate these conversations: **nickels and noses**. How much money came in, and how many people showed up? The phrase is usually spoken with a little embarrassment because everyone recognizes that church is supposed to be about something larger. Then the budget meeting begins, and the nickels and noses return.

I don't think they should leave.

If attendance is increasing, leaders need to know because they may need more chairs, more children's workers, another service, or a larger room. If giving is falling, someone needs to know whether the church can continue its commitments. If a program consistently attracts nobody, it is reasonable to ask whether the program should continue consuming volunteers and resources simply because someone once thought it was a good idea.

Numbers can help a church steward what it has been given. The problem begins when stewardship quietly becomes purpose.

## When the Question Changes

Imagine that a church has been asking a simple question: *How can we help people worship, grow, love one another, and become more whole?* Attendance can provide useful information related to that question. If nobody comes, whatever good we hoped to offer is not reaching very many people; if people keep leaving, perhaps there is something leadership needs to understand. The number deserves to speak.

Then imagine the question shifting almost imperceptibly.

*How do we get attendance up?*

Those questions are close enough that we can easily mistake them for each other. Often the same action might serve both: improve children's programs, make newcomers feel welcome, communicate more clearly, or offer music people find meaningful. Growth may be evidence that something healthy is happening.

But the questions are not identical.

A church can grow because something beautiful is happening. It can also grow because it has excellent marketing, a charismatic leader, convenient programs, talented musicians, a desirable location, or enough resources to offer experiences people enjoy. A church can shrink because something is deeply wrong, but it can also shrink because a neighborhood changed, a major employer left town, or another congregation opened nearby.

Attendance knows how many people came.

It does not know why they came.

More importantly, it does not know what happened to them while they were there.

Suppose six hundred people attend this weekend instead of five hundred. That may be wonderful news, and there is no spiritual virtue in pretending otherwise. But the question that keeps the number in its proper place is not *Does attendance matter?* It is *What were we hoping would happen to those six hundred people?*

Perhaps we hoped someone lonely would discover community. Perhaps a person carrying shame would encounter grace, someone angry would begin the long work of forgiveness, a family under strain would find support, or people would become more capable of loving their neighbors. Those are not decorative aspirations added after the real work of growing attendance has been completed. If the church believes its own language, those are closer to the point.

The number can tell us whether six hundred people entered the building.

It cannot tell us whether the building entered them.

## How Do You Measure Forgiveness?

At this point, the obvious response is to improve the metrics. If attendance and giving are inadequate proxies for the things that really matter, perhaps we should measure the things that really matter. We have spent most of this book becoming sophisticated enough about measurement that surely we can build a better spiritual dashboard.

Perhaps the congregation could complete a quarterly forgiveness assessment. Baseline forgiveness is 6.4, the year-end target is 7.3, and anyone falling below the stretch objective will be invited to the Forgiveness Center of Excellence. Managers—sorry, pastors—could review progress monthly, identify forgiveness outliers, and develop corrective action plans for anyone whose sanctification appears to be behind schedule.

Within six months, someone would figure out how to game salvation.

The joke works because forgiveness is real. People genuinely carry resentment, release it, reconcile when reconciliation is possible, establish boundaries when it is necessary, soften toward people they once hated, and sometimes discover that a wound no longer occupies the place in their lives it once did. Something has changed, and that change leaves evidence.

But the evidence does not necessarily behave like a score.

A pastor who has known someone for years may recognize that they have become less defensive, more patient, or more capable of acknowledging harm without immediately protecting themselves. A friend may notice that someone can finally speak about an old injury without being consumed by it. The person themselves may realize one day that they have gone several weeks without rehearsing an argument that once occupied their thoughts every morning.

None of these observations is meaningless because it lacks a standardized unit.

This is an important distinction because the alternative to measurement is not ignorance. Human beings knew things about one another long before dashboards arrived. We recognize trustworthiness, courage, tenderness, resentment, generosity, fear, and change through relationship, memory, attention, and judgment.

Those ways of knowing can certainly be wrong. A number has the great advantage of making some kinds of self-deception harder, which is one reason I have defended measurement throughout this book. But numbers can also create their own illusion of certainty, especially when we assign precision to something that does not naturally possess it.

Forgiveness may be real without being 7.3.

## What the Offering Plate Cannot Know

Giving provides an especially interesting example because here the spiritual reality and the measurable reality occupy exactly the same event. Someone gives money. The church deposits it, the bank records it, and the accounting system can tell us exactly how many dollars were received.

That number matters. If the church spends more than it receives for long enough, no amount of theological nuance will keep the lights on. The finance team should count every dollar accurately, report it honestly, create a responsible budget, and become concerned if the numbers no longer work.

But suppose we ask a different question: *Was the person generous?*

Now the amount becomes less decisive. A wealthy person can write a large check without noticing the expense, while someone with very little may give twenty dollars at genuine personal cost. The accounting system records the larger gift because dollars are exactly what dollars are designed to count.

It would be absurd to criticize the accounting system for this. We asked it how much money was given, and it answered correctly. The mistake would be asking the number to settle a question about generosity that requires knowing something about the person.

This is where I think the relationship between measurement and meaning becomes clearest. The measurable fact does not become less true when we place it beside a deeper reality. Five thousand dollars is still more money than twenty dollars, and if the roof needs replacing, the difference matters enormously. Yet the larger amount is not therefore proof of greater generosity.

The numbers are allowed to speak.

They simply do not get the last word.

## Productivity: Zero

Contemplative prayer presents an even more irritating problem for the optimizer. I can sit quietly for twenty minutes, return my attention when it wanders, and finish with no new information, no solved problem, no completed task, and no visible improvement in the world. My calorie expenditure is unimpressive, and from a conventional productivity perspective the return on investment is difficult to defend.

There are ways to defend it, of course. Silence may make me less reactive, more attentive, or more capable of noticing what is happening inside me before I act on it. Research may identify benefits associated with contemplative practices, and I am perfectly happy if twenty minutes of prayer leaves me calmer during the next staff meeting.

But something changes if that becomes the reason I pray.

Prayer becomes another input in the personal performance system from Chapter 5. I meditate so I can become more productive, sleep so I can perform better tomorrow, exercise so I can improve the health dashboard, and rest so I can return to work with greater efficiency. Eventually even the parts of life that were supposed to give us relief from optimization have been recruited into the optimization program.

This is where the question from Chapter 8 returns in a different form. *What is this for?* If the answer to every activity is another activity further down the chain, life begins to resemble an organizational chart in which nobody can identify the customer. Everything serves something else.

Perhaps some things are allowed to matter without proving their usefulness elsewhere.

I can sit in silence because silence has a place in my life. I can play piano because playing piano is worth doing, spend time with someone because I love them, or participate in worship without requiring the experience to produce a measurable improvement by Tuesday. These things may have consequences, sometimes wonderful ones, but the consequences do not have to become their justification.

This is difficult for the part of me that learned adulthood through usefulness. Work needs to get done, bills need to be paid, people depend on us, and responsible adults cannot spend every afternoon staring thoughtfully at clouds. Instrumental thinking is not a corruption of maturity; it is one of the ways we care for the world.

But perhaps usefulness does not exhaust value.

That possibility is small enough to miss. It does not ask me to reject planning, measurement, competence, or responsibility. It asks whether there are parts of life I can enter without immediately demanding that they produce something else.

## What the Numbers Are For

A church has to live with this tension every week. Someone has to count the offering, schedule the volunteers, know how many children are in the classrooms, maintain the building, pay the staff, and decide whether there is enough money for the things the community hopes to do. Spiritual purpose does not remove organizational reality.

But organizational reality does not define spiritual purpose either.

The church counts people because people matter, not because counting is the point. It counts money because money makes ministry possible, not because a larger balance is evidence of greater faithfulness. It rehearses music because preparation can serve worship, while knowing that a flawless performance and a meaningful encounter are not the same thing.

This is not a tidy division between the measurable and the immeasurable. The two are constantly intertwined. A meal offered to someone who is hungry has a cost, takes time to prepare, serves a countable number of people, and may also communicate something about belonging that none of those numbers can express.

Perhaps that is why church makes such a useful bridge in this story. It refuses to let me solve the tension by choosing a side. If I dismiss measurement, I can become irresponsible about resources entrusted to me; if I elevate measurement, I can become extremely efficient at operating an institution whose purpose I have gradually forgotten.

Wisdom seems to require holding both.

Count the people.

Balance the budget.

Rehearse the music.

Then remember why you are doing any of it.

For most of my adult life, mastery taught me to act upon the world: to plan, build, measure, correct, improve, and make things happen. I still believe in those verbs, and church has given me no reason to abandon them. If anything, caring about something deeply should make us want to serve it competently.

But sitting behind a piano or a drum kit has also introduced me to another kind of attention. I prepare as well as I can, begin to play, listen to the people around me, and discover that I am participating in something I do not entirely control. The preparation matters, but eventually preparation gives way to presence.

Perhaps that is true beyond music.

Perhaps there are things we can know by measuring them, and other things we begin to know only by entering them.

That is where we will go next.
