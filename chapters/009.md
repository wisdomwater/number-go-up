# 9 — Nickels and Noses

I have spent a lot of time playing music in church. Sometimes I play drums, sometimes piano, and like any musician I want to play well. You practice, learn the arrangement, pay attention to tempo, listen to the other musicians, and try not to introduce an unexpected percussion solo during the quietest moment of the service. Competence matters because a room full of people can tell surprisingly quickly when the band has not done its homework.

But every once in a while, something happens that has very little to do with whether I played everything correctly. The song settles into the room differently, the musicians begin listening to one another in a way that cannot quite be rehearsed, and the congregation seems to become unusually present. Maybe I feel it strongly and someone else does not, so I have learned to be cautious about declaring what happened on behalf of everyone. Still, there are moments when music seems to become more than the successful execution of music.

This is where mastery begins encountering something it cannot manufacture. I can practice the piano, learn the chords, improve my timing, and prepare myself to play well. A sound engineer can balance the room, the musicians can rehearse, and the service can be planned with considerable care. We can create favorable conditions for an experience without being able to command the experience itself.

No worship leader can write “10:43 a.m. — congregation experiences transcendence” into the service plan and expect the schedule to make it happen. We can place a song at 10:43, dim the lights, play the right progression, and perhaps even produce an emotional response with some reliability. But whether a particular person encounters beauty, comfort, conviction, God, memory, grief, or nothing unusual at all remains beyond our control. Other people are not variables, and neither, apparently, is wonder.

For someone who has spent a career learning how to make things happen, this is an unusual kind of work. Preparation matters enormously, yet preparation cannot guarantee the thing we most hope will happen. The musician has to practice and then, at some point, stop practicing and play. Mastery brings us to the moment, but it cannot determine what the moment will become.

## We Were Just Playing Music

There is an enormous amount about music that can be measured. Tempo has a number, pitch has a frequency, volume has a level, and a recording can be analyzed in more ways than most musicians would ever care to know. A drummer who consistently speeds up during the chorus can be shown fairly convincing evidence of the problem. Measurement can make musicians better.

Rehearsal works for the same reason so much of mastery works. We repeat difficult transitions until they become natural, isolate weak parts, listen to recordings, make adjustments, and develop enough technical ability that we no longer have to think consciously about every movement. The goal is not to become mechanical. The goal is to become competent enough that technique stops demanding all of our attention.

Then something interesting happens. The better we become at playing, the more capable we are of listening. I can stop thinking quite so hard about what my hands are doing and notice what the bass player is doing, where the singer is going, and whether the room seems to need more sound or less. Technique, at its best, creates freedom.

This is one of the reasons I do not find a simple opposition between mastery and wonder very convincing. The inexperienced musician may be wonderfully spontaneous while making the rest of the band wish for a metronome. Discipline can deepen an experience rather than diminish it. The person who has spent years learning an instrument may encounter possibilities inside a piece of music that a beginner cannot yet hear.

But skill does not give the musician ownership of the experience. I can play the same song two Sundays in a row with roughly the same notes, tempo, arrangement, and musicians, and the two experiences can feel entirely different. Sometimes the song simply works as planned, which is perfectly fine. Sometimes something arrives that nobody knows how to put on the set list.

I like that music makes this distinction difficult to avoid. We can prepare without pretending that preparation produces everything that matters. We can care deeply about excellence while leaving room for something we did not engineer. The work is partly making something happen and partly becoming available to what happens.

## Then the Megachurch Moved In

At one point, a large church moved into our area. It would make for a cleaner story if I described it as a soulless religious corporation descending upon the neighborhood with smoke machines and a marketing department, but that would not be fair. They had excellent musicians, strong programs, resources for families, and plenty of people sincerely trying to serve their community. Some children enjoyed the place so much their parents probably needed a strategic extraction plan to get them home.

Still, the presence of a much larger church changes the environment around smaller churches. You become aware of what they offer, how many people attend, what their music sounds like, and what experiences they can provide with resources you do not have. Comparison enters the room. Once comparison enters, measurement is rarely far behind.

Churches have always counted things because churches have always had practical responsibilities. Buildings cost money, employees need salaries, programs need volunteers, and rooms have capacities that fire marshals remain stubbornly unwilling to calculate in units of spiritual enthusiasm. If two hundred people regularly attend and suddenly only one hundred do, leadership should probably notice. You cannot pay the electric bill with transcendence.

There is an old church phrase for the measurements that tend to dominate these conversations: **nickels and noses**. How much money came in, and how many people showed up? The phrase is usually spoken with a little embarrassment because almost everyone recognizes that church is supposed to be about something larger. Then the budget meeting begins, and the nickels and noses return.

Again, the numbers are not the problem. If attendance is increasing, leaders need to know because they may need more chairs, more children's workers, or another service. If giving is falling, someone needs to understand whether the church can continue its commitments. Responsible stewardship requires counting things.

The more interesting shift happens when the practical question becomes the purpose. Instead of asking, *What helps people worship, grow, love one another, and become more whole?* we begin asking, *What will get more people through the doors?* Those questions may overlap, and often they do. But just as with the customer-service call in Chapter 3, the trouble appears where the proxy and the purpose separate.

A church can grow because something beautiful is happening. It can also grow because it has excellent marketing, a charismatic leader, convenient programs, good music, or the resources to provide experiences people enjoy. A church can shrink because something is deeply wrong, or because the neighborhood changed, a large employer left town, or another congregation opened nearby. Attendance tells us how many people came; it does not tell us why they came or what happened to them while they were there.

This is where the question from our corporate chapters returns in a different setting. Suppose six hundred people attend this weekend instead of five hundred. The increase may be worth celebrating, but what were we hoping would happen to those six hundred people? Once we ask that question, the dashboard becomes much harder to build.

## How Do You Measure Forgiveness?

Imagine a church leadership team deciding that attendance and giving are too shallow, so they resolve to measure the things that really matter. Perhaps the congregation could complete a quarterly forgiveness assessment. Baseline forgiveness is 6.4, the year-end target is 7.3, and anyone falling below the stretch objective will be invited to the Forgiveness Center of Excellence. Within six months, someone will discover how to game salvation.

The joke works because forgiveness is real. People genuinely carry resentment, release it, reconcile, establish boundaries, soften, change, and sometimes become capable of loving someone they once hated. These changes leave evidence in a life. We can recognize transformation without being able to represent it adequately with a score.

The same is true of generosity. A church can measure how much someone gives, but the amount alone does not tell us what the gift means. A wealthy person can write a large check without noticing the expense, while someone with very little can give twenty dollars at real personal cost. The financial system records the larger gift because dollars are exactly what dollars are designed to count.

Love presents the problem even more clearly. I could gather evidence that I love my wife: time spent together, things I have done for her, conversations, sacrifices, messages, anniversaries remembered, and perhaps a few forgotten that are best omitted from the longitudinal study. All of that might tell you something true about our relationship. But if I concluded that the marriage currently rates an 8.7, I would have transformed a living relationship into something oddly smaller than itself.

This does not mean love, forgiveness, or generosity are invisible. Quite the opposite. They become visible constantly in behavior, relationships, choices, expressions, and the kinds of people we are becoming. The mistake is assuming that because something leaves evidence, the evidence can fully contain the thing.

We already know how to live with this distinction in ordinary life. I do not need a friendship score to know that a friend sat with me when life was difficult. I do not need a compassion index to recognize the difference between someone performing concern and someone who is actually present. Human beings have forms of knowing that involve judgment, memory, relationship, and attention as well as measurement.

This can make organizations uncomfortable because these forms of knowledge are harder to standardize. A number can be placed on a slide and compared with last quarter, while a story about someone becoming less afraid does not fit as neatly into a dashboard. Yet if becoming less afraid was part of what we hoped would happen, the awkwardness of representing it does not make it less important. It may simply reveal the boundary of the instrument.

## Productivity: Zero

Contemplative prayer presents an even more irritating problem for the optimizer. I can sit quietly for twenty minutes, return my attention when it wanders, and finish with no new information, no solved problem, no completed task, and no visible improvement in the world. My calorie expenditure is unimpressive. From a conventional productivity perspective, the return on investment is difficult to defend.

There are ways to defend it, of course. Research may show benefits associated with contemplative practices, and I may notice that silence makes me more patient, less reactive, or better able to concentrate. These are worthwhile effects, and I am happy when they occur. But something is lost if I decide the purpose of twenty minutes of prayer is improving my performance during the next staff meeting.

We do this with rest too. Sleep becomes valuable because it makes us productive tomorrow, vacations become useful because they prevent burnout, and hobbies become important because creative activity improves cognitive flexibility. Even friendship can be defended by citing research about longevity and social connection. Apparently nothing is safe until we can prove that it improves a KPI.

There is another possibility: some things may be valuable without needing to justify themselves through something else. I can play piano because playing piano is worth doing, spend time with a friend because I love the friend, and sit quietly because silence itself has a place in my life. A walk can be worthwhile even if I forget to record it. Not everything valuable needs to become an input into a future output.

This is what philosophers sometimes call intrinsic value, but the experience is more ordinary than the terminology suggests. Children understand it instinctively when they spend an hour doing something that produces absolutely nothing except the hour they just spent. They build, draw, wander, sing, collect rocks, tell ridiculous stories, and ask questions whose practical applications are extremely limited. The activity does not have to lead somewhere else to deserve their attention.

Adults have good reasons for becoming more instrumental. Food has to be prepared, bills paid, work completed, children cared for, and futures planned. We learn to ask what something is for because consequences matter. The ability to sacrifice immediate pleasure for a larger purpose is one of the achievements of maturity.

But perhaps maturity has another movement after that one. We learn that an activity can serve a purpose, then eventually rediscover that purpose is not the only reason to do something. We can bring all our adult knowledge and responsibility back into an experience that does not need to become useful. The child encounters the world before mastery; the mature adult may learn to encounter it again after mastery.

That second encounter is different from the first. I cannot unknow what I know about exercise when I go for a walk, or forget everything I have learned about music when I sit at the piano. I do not need to become innocent again. I need only stop requiring every experience to report to the future.

## From Making to Receiving

The language of mastery is filled with active verbs. We measure, plan, build, improve, optimize, manage, correct, execute, and achieve. These verbs have carried much of civilization forward, and they have carried many of us through successful adult lives. There is no reason to become embarrassed by them now.

But there is another family of verbs that behaves differently. We notice, listen, receive, attend, wait, encounter, and participate. These do not describe passivity so much as a different relationship with what is happening. They assume that reality may contain something worth discovering that I did not put there.

A musician needs both sets. I practice, prepare, rehearse, and learn the song, then I listen to what is happening when we play it. A host prepares dinner, sets the table, and invites people over, but cannot schedule the moment when a casual conversation unexpectedly becomes important. A person can walk into the woods deliberately without being able to arrange the exact moment when the light through the trees stops them.

This is where surprise begins to look different. In engineering, surprise often indicates that something escaped our model. The system behaved in a way we did not predict, which usually means there is more work to do. A great deal of engineering exists precisely to remove unpleasant surprises from systems that should behave reliably.

There are many places in life where I am grateful for this. I do not want the brakes on my car to surprise me, the airplane wing to explore new forms of structural creativity, or payroll to embrace mystery on payday. Predictability is a genuine human achievement. Some parts of the world are better precisely because competent people worked very hard to make them boring.

The trouble begins when the same expectation expands into every part of life. We start wanting relationships, careers, bodies, children, spiritual lives, and even leisure to produce predictable returns from properly executed inputs. Surprise becomes evidence that something escaped the plan. A sufficiently optimized life begins to resemble a system from which all unexpected behavior has been successfully removed.

I am no longer sure that would be a good life.

Some of the experiences I value most were not things I could have ordered in advance. A conversation went somewhere unexpected, music affected me differently than it had before, a person entered my life through circumstances I could not have planned, or an ordinary moment acquired significance I understood only much later. I participated in those experiences, sometimes actively, without being their sole author.

This does not ask us to choose between action and reception. We still practice the instrument, prepare the meal, make the phone call, take the walk, show up for work, and care for the people entrusted to us. Mastery creates conditions in which many good things become more likely. Then we discover that creating the conditions is not the same as creating everything that happens within them.

For most of this book, we have been learning how much can be accomplished by acting deliberately upon the world. We measure reality, change it, coordinate it, simplify it, manage ourselves within it, and take responsibility for the choices that are ours. These abilities are real, and nothing in what follows requires us to leave them behind. We are simply approaching the place where their authority ends.

Maybe mastery brought us to the door.

What happens on the other side may require learning how to receive.
