## Chapter 9 — Nickels and Noses

## We Were Just Playing Music

I play music at my church.

I've done it for years.

Sometimes drums. Sometimes piano. Whatever is needed.

There is something wonderfully simple about playing music in a church. At its best, you're not trying to accomplish very much in the usual sense of the word.

You're trying to help people worship.

You're trying to create space.

You're trying to choose songs that say something true, that meet people where they are, that help them pray when they don't quite have words of their own.

At least, that's the idea.

Of course, churches are still organizations run by human beings, so nothing remains quite that simple.

At some point, a megachurch moved in down the street.

This wasn't their fault.

They didn't arrive as an invading army.

They weren't standing in our parking lot handing out flyers or stealing congregants as they walked toward the sanctuary.

They were simply there.

And they were big.

They had resources.

They had programs.

They had excellent musicians.

They could do things at a scale that a smaller church simply couldn't.

Suddenly, whether we intended it or not, there was a comparison.

And comparison changes questions.

The question had once been something like:

**What music will help people worship?**

Then other questions began appearing alongside it.

**What kind of music are people looking for?**

**What songs will younger people connect with?**

**What are successful churches playing?**

**What will make someone want to come back?**

These aren't terrible questions.

That's important.

If you're leading music for other people, what those people connect with actually matters.

Nobody benefits from a worship leader stubbornly playing songs nobody else wants to sing while insisting that everyone else simply lacks spiritual depth.

A church should pay attention to the people it serves.

But I could feel something shifting.

The question was no longer only:

**What will speak to the heart?**

It was also:

**What will draw people?**

And the second question had one enormous advantage over the first.

We could see the answer.

People either came or they didn't.

The room looked fuller or emptier.

Attendance went up or down.

People returned the following week or disappeared.

The feedback was immediate.

The heart is considerably less cooperative.

I cannot look across a room on Sunday morning and determine that seventeen people experienced a 12 percent increase in compassion.

I don't know whether someone forgave their father because of something they encountered there.

I don't know whether a person who arrived carrying enormous grief felt, for twenty minutes, that they weren't alone.

I don't know whether someone became a little less afraid.

I don't know whether a lyric found its way into a person's mind three days later when they needed it.

Those things are mostly invisible.

Attendance isn't.

So naturally, we noticed attendance.

I don't think we were shallow.

I don't think anyone stopped caring about people's hearts.

I certainly don't remember a meeting where someone announced that spiritual transformation was no longer a strategic priority and would henceforth be replaced by market share.

We were doing something much more ordinary.

We were following the pattern of the culture around us.

Successful things grow.

Healthy businesses grow.

Popular products grow.

Audiences grow.

Platforms grow.

Careers grow.

Investments grow.

If something is working, there should eventually be more of it.

So if a church is healthy, shouldn't more people come?

It's a perfectly reasonable thought.

And once the megachurch was down the street, the comparison made the question harder to ignore.

They were growing.

Were we?

Number go up.

Something good must be happening.

Number go down.

Something must be wrong.

There it was again.

The same instinct we've encountered everywhere else in this book had found its way into a place where I hadn't expected it.

We had taken something complicated, intimate, and difficult to see and found something nearby that we could count.

Then, very slowly, the count began acquiring meaning.

The strange thing is that nobody has to consciously choose this.

Measurable things have gravity.

They pull attention toward themselves because they give us something intangible things cannot.

Clarity.

A room with three hundred people in it is fuller than a room with two hundred.

Nobody needs to discern that.

Nobody needs to pray about it.

Nobody needs to sit quietly and ask what it means.

Three hundred is more than two hundred.

Finally, something we can work with.

The trouble begins with the next sentence.

**Therefore...**

Therefore we're healthier.

Therefore we're reaching people.

Therefore the music is working.

Therefore we're doing something right.

Maybe.

Those things could be true.

But the number itself doesn't know.

All it knows is how many people came through the door.

And this is where the old temptation appears again.

We start with something that matters but is difficult to see.

We find something nearby that is easier to measure.

Then we slowly allow the visible thing to stand in for the invisible one.

Nobody has to be cynical.

Nobody has to be manipulative.

Nobody even has to notice.

We were just playing music.

We wanted it to matter.

And like everyone else, we wanted some way to know whether it did.

## Bigger Is Better

There is something deeply persuasive about growth.

We trust it.

A business with increasing revenue appears healthier than one with declining revenue.

A product gaining customers appears more successful than one losing them.

An audience getting larger suggests that something is connecting.

A retirement account going up is generally preferable to the alternative.

Growth is not an arbitrary thing to value.

In many parts of life, growth really is good.

So we develop an instinct.

**Bigger is better.**

Not always.

But often enough that the assumption becomes almost invisible.

A restaurant with a line out the door must be good.

A book selling millions of copies must have something going for it.

A company hiring thousands of employees must be succeeding.

A social media account with a million followers must be influential.

A church with thousands of people attending must be doing something right.

Maybe.

There is that word again.

Maybe.

The difficulty isn't that size tells us nothing.

Size tells us something important.

If people are coming, they are responding to something.

If more people keep coming, something about the experience is attracting them or giving them a reason to return.

That deserves attention.

A church should not look at steadily declining attendance and dismiss it with:

**We're probably just becoming more spiritual.**

That would be a wonderfully convenient theology.

Sometimes people leave because something isn't working.

Sometimes a community has become disconnected from the people around it.

Sometimes the music really isn't connecting.

Sometimes the programs aren't meeting people's needs.

Sometimes leadership should pay attention.

Measurement gives reality a way to disagree with us here too.

The problem begins when we move from:

**More people are coming.**

To:

**Therefore, we are succeeding.**

Succeeding at what?

That's the question.

If the goal is filling the room, attendance is an excellent metric.

Count the people.

Problem solved.

But churches generally claim to be doing something more ambitious than filling rooms.

They talk about transformation.

Community.

Discipleship.

Love.

Service.

Healing.

Faith.

Becoming more like Christ.

Those are considerably harder to count.

And once again, the measurable thing has an advantage.

Attendance gives us a number every Sunday.

Transformation does not.

You can put attendance on a chart.

You can compare this month with last month.

You can compare Easter with last Easter.

You can compare one service with another.

You can calculate growth rates.

You can identify trends.

You can make the line go up and to the right.

Try doing that with wisdom.

**Wisdom increased 6.3 percent year over year.**

Excellent quarter.

The absurdity tells us something.

Not that wisdom is unreal.

Not that growth in wisdom is impossible.

We know people can become wiser.

We know people can become kinder.

We know people can become more generous, more patient, more courageous, more forgiving.

We may even recognize those changes when we see them.

But they resist the clean numerical language we've become accustomed to using for growth.

This creates an imbalance.

The things easiest to measure become the easiest to manage.

The things easiest to manage become the easiest to discuss.

The things easiest to discuss become the things that show up in meetings.

And the things that show up in meetings begin to feel like the things that matter.

**Measurability creates cultural gravity.**

We've seen this before.

Churches aren't exempt because their mission is spiritual.

They're still human organizations.

They have budgets.

Staff.

Buildings.

Schedules.

Programs.

Committees.

Strategic plans.

Probably a spreadsheet somewhere that nobody understands but everyone is afraid to delete.

They face the same pressures every organization faces.

They need feedback.

They need to make decisions.

They need to know whether something is working.

And when the central purpose of the organization is difficult to measure, proxies become almost irresistible.

Attendance is one.

Money is another.

Programs.

Volunteers.

Small groups.

Baptisms.

Membership.

All of these numbers can tell us something.

Some may tell us something quite important.

But the question remains:

**What exactly grew?**

If attendance increased 20 percent, we know attendance increased 20 percent.

That's real.

That's useful.

That's worth understanding.

Maybe community grew too.

Maybe generosity grew.

Maybe faith deepened.

Maybe people became more loving.

Maybe lives were transformed.

But those are additional claims.

The attendance number doesn't contain them.

This distinction becomes especially difficult because growth feels so good.

I've watched a room fill up.

There is energy in it.

More voices singing.

More conversations.

More people meeting one another.

More possibility.

It is difficult not to feel that something is going right.

And perhaps something is.

We don't need to become suspicious of joy every time the room gets fuller.

But we should remember the lesson we've been learning throughout this book.

**The number knows what the number knows. Nothing more.**

Attendance knows how many people attended.

It doesn't know why they came.

It doesn't know what happened while they were there.

It doesn't know what happens when they leave.

It doesn't know whether a crowded room contains transformation or simply a crowd.

And a small room presents the opposite problem.

Fewer people does not automatically mean less meaning.

Some of the most important things that happen between human beings happen in very small rooms.

A conversation.

An apology.

A prayer.

Someone telling the truth for the first time.

Someone sitting beside another person in grief.

None of these scale particularly well.

That doesn't make them failures.

This is where **bigger is better** begins to run into trouble.

Bigger can be better.

More people can mean more lives touched.

More resources can mean more good done.

Growth can create possibilities that small organizations simply don't have.

There is no virtue in being small merely for the sake of being small.

But size is a dimension.

It is not a verdict.

Once again, the question has to come first.

**What's the point?**

Only then can we ask whether getting bigger is helping us get there.

Otherwise, growth becomes self-justifying.

We grow because growth means success.

And we know growth means success because we're growing.

Number go up.

Everybody nods.

This is good.

But if we're going to talk seriously about spiritual life, eventually we have to ask a more difficult question.

What if the things we're actually trying to grow are things we cannot easily count?

## Nickels and Noses

There is an old phrase for the two numbers churches have historically found easiest to count:

**Nickels and noses.**

How much money came in?

How many people showed up?

The phrase is usually meant critically.

It suggests a church has reduced its mission to attendance and offerings.

Fill the seats.

Fill the plate.

Success.

But by now, hopefully, we've learned not to dismiss a metric simply because it is incomplete.

Nickels and noses matter.

Churches need money.

Buildings cost money.

Electricity costs money.

Staff need to be paid.

Programs need resources.

People need help.

Mission work costs money.

And Sunday morning coffee must apparently be provided in quantities sufficient to sustain the kingdom of God.

Someone needs to know whether the organization can pay its bills.

Giving also tells us something about participation.

People tend to financially support things they care about.

If giving collapses, leadership should probably be curious about why.

The same is true of attendance.

A church is, among other things, a gathering of people.

If nobody gathers, there may be a problem.

If attendance has been falling for five years, it would be strange for leadership to refuse to look at the number because spirituality is too mysterious for spreadsheets.

Maybe the community around the church has changed.

Maybe people's needs have changed.

Maybe trust has been damaged.

Maybe the church is no longer communicating effectively.

Maybe everyone moved to Florida.

The number cannot tell us why.

But it can tell us something happened.

That's useful.

This is the recurring problem with bad metrics.

They are rarely completely useless.

If they were, we wouldn't keep them.

The danger is that they tell us **something**, and we slowly promote that something into everything.

Attendance becomes more than attendance.

It becomes reach.

Then impact.

Then health.

Then success.

Giving becomes more than giving.

It becomes commitment.

Then generosity.

Then spiritual maturity.

Again:

Maybe.

There may be relationships between these things.

A growing, healthy community may attract more people.

A generous community may give more money.

If a church is genuinely helping people, perhaps people will tell their friends.

The proxies are not random.

That's exactly why they are persuasive.

But correlation does not erase the distance between the things.

A person can attend every Sunday and remain completely unchanged.

A person can give a large amount of money without being particularly generous.

Someone else can give very little and be giving at enormous personal cost.

The offering report sees dollars.

It does not see sacrifice.

Attendance sees presence in the room.

It does not see presence of heart.

And the problem gets even more interesting when the metric becomes a target.

Suppose attendance is declining.

Leadership understandably wants to reverse it.

So we ask what will bring people in.

Better music?

Different preaching?

More programs?

Improved children's ministry?

Advertising?

A better website?

Different service times?

These may all be excellent ideas.

Nothing about trying to attract people is inherently corrupt.

If you believe what you're doing matters, you probably want people to experience it.

But once attendance becomes the target, Goodhart follows us into the sanctuary.

We begin optimizing for what gets people through the door.

And what gets people through the door may or may not be the same thing that helps them become the people we hope they become.

Those goals can align.

Often they do.

But they are not identical.

The same is true of giving.

A church needs financial stability.

So perhaps we measure giving.

Set targets.

Track trends.

Run campaigns.

Teach about generosity.

Again, all potentially legitimate.

But the moment dollars become the primary evidence of generosity, something important disappears.

The person writing the largest check may barely notice the money is gone.

The person giving twenty dollars may have thought about it all week.

The spreadsheet ranks the first person higher.

The spiritual reality may be considerably more complicated.

This is where measurement encounters a category it handles poorly:

**interior life.**

Metrics are very good at counting visible events.

A person attended.

A person gave.

A person volunteered.

A person joined a group.

A person completed a class.

Those things happened.

We can count them.

But spiritual traditions are usually interested in what those visible practices are doing inside a person.

Is someone becoming more loving?

More patient?

Less afraid?

More honest?

More generous?

More able to forgive?

Less controlled by resentment?

More capable of seeing another person as a human being rather than an obstacle?

Those are much closer to the point.

They are also much harder to put in a quarterly report.

So the temptation is obvious.

Count what can be counted.

Use it as evidence for what cannot.

And eventually forget that we made the substitution.

This is the same move we've been following from the beginning.

We care about something complicated.

We find a measurable proxy nearby.

The proxy gives us visibility.

Visibility gives us confidence.

Confidence gives the proxy authority.

Then one day we find ourselves talking as though the proxy and the reality were the same thing.

The irony is that spirituality should make us particularly cautious about this.

Spirituality deals almost entirely in realities that resist easy quantification.

Faith.

Hope.

Love.

Wisdom.

Humility.

Forgiveness.

Compassion.

Presence.

Transformation.

We recognize these things.

We experience them.

We can often see their effects.

But ask for the unit of measurement and the whole system begins to wobble.

How many units of forgiveness did you produce this week?

What was your average daily compassion?

Is humility up quarter over quarter?

How does your current love-of-enemy performance compare with the industry benchmark?

The questions sound ridiculous.

But they expose something serious.

If these are the things we ultimately care about, how do we know whether they're growing?

That's a much harder question than counting nickels and noses.

And it may require a different way of seeing.

## What Exactly Are We Trying to Grow?

Let's say attendance goes up 20 percent.

That's good news.

Probably.

There is that word again.

The room is fuller.

More people are participating.

More people are hearing the message.

More people are meeting one another.

More people have an opportunity to become part of the community.

Those are meaningful possibilities.

But if we want to know whether the church is actually succeeding, eventually we have to ask the question we've been asking throughout this book:

**What's the point?**

Why do we want more people in the room?

The answer probably isn't:

**Because there were 412 last month and 495 would look better on the annual report.**

The number points somewhere.

Presumably, we want people to come because we believe something can happen to them.

Or in them.

Or among them.

We hope people encounter God.

We hope they find community.

We hope the lonely find connection.

We hope the hurting find healing.

We hope people become wiser.

Kinder.

More generous.

More courageous.

More loving.

More capable of forgiving.

Less controlled by fear.

Less consumed by themselves.

Whatever language a particular tradition uses, the point is usually some form of transformation.

The church isn't merely trying to gather people.

It is trying to help people become something.

And now measurement gets difficult.

Because transformation doesn't happen in clean units.

Suppose attendance rises 20 percent while the people attending become more angry, more tribal, more afraid, and less capable of loving anyone outside their group.

Number go up.

Did we succeed?

Suppose attendance falls 20 percent, but the remaining community becomes more generous, more compassionate, more deeply connected, and more willing to serve the people around them.

Number go down.

Did we fail?

I don't know.

That's the uncomfortable answer.

I would want to know more.

Maybe declining attendance is still a serious problem.

Maybe the church is becoming healthier in one dimension and unhealthy in another.

Maybe something valuable is deepening while something else genuinely needs attention.

Reality has an annoying habit of refusing to fit inside a single score.

That's why **What's the point?** matters so much.

The question takes us upstream from the metric.

Before attendance.

Before giving.

Before programs.

Before growth targets.

What are we actually trying to produce?

This is where organizations of every kind can get into trouble.

We often begin with purpose and then create measurements to help us pursue it.

Over time, the measurements become easier to discuss than the purpose.

Purpose is messy.

Purpose requires judgment.

Purpose invites disagreement.

Numbers are much more cooperative.

Imagine a leadership meeting.

Attendance is down 8 percent.

Everyone knows what that means numerically.

Now ask:

**Are people becoming more compassionate?**

Long pause.

Someone clears their throat.

There probably isn't a spreadsheet.

The difficulty of answering the second question can make the first one feel more important than it actually is.

At least we know something.

And we do.

That's the point I keep returning to.

Attendance is not meaningless because compassion is hard to measure.

Attendance tells us about attendance.

Giving tells us about giving.

Volunteering tells us about volunteering.

The mistake is expecting those measurements to carry the entire weight of the mission.

They can't.

The mission is too large.

This is where the hierarchy from earlier in the book becomes especially important.

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment.**

Meaning comes first.

What are we trying to do?

Then measurement.

What information might help us understand whether it is happening?

Then judgment.

Given the information we have—and the information we don't—what should we do?

Reverse that order and strange things happen.

Measurement tells us what matters.

Attendance is measurable, therefore attendance must be the goal.

Then judgment serves the metric.

How do we make attendance go up?

Now we are optimizing before we've finished asking what we're optimizing for.

This doesn't require bad motives.

That's what makes it so easy.

Everyone may genuinely care about people.

Everyone may sincerely want transformation.

Everyone may believe deeply in the mission.

But the measurable part of the mission gradually receives more attention because it produces better feedback.

The invisible part has no dashboard to defend itself.

That line may be worth sitting with.

**The invisible part has no dashboard to defend itself.**

Nobody receives an automatic notification saying:

**Warning: Compassion has declined 14 percent over the last six months.**

There is no red indicator when people become slightly more judgmental.

No quarterly chart tracks whether resentment is loosening its grip.

No alert appears when a person finally forgives someone after carrying anger for twenty years.

The things that matter may be happening everywhere, and our systems may barely notice them.

Or they may not be happening at all, while every organizational metric looks fantastic.

That's the danger.

A church can become extraordinarily successful at church metrics.

Attendance up.

Giving up.

Programs expanding.

More volunteers.

More services.

More buildings.

More activity.

And still leave unanswered the deepest question:

**What is all of this producing in people?**

Businesses face versions of this question too.

So do schools.

So do governments.

So do families.

So do individuals.

But spirituality makes the tension unusually visible because the desired outcome is so profoundly interior.

We are trying to talk about changes in the human heart.

And the human heart has proven remarkably resistant to quarterly reporting.

Maybe that's not a defect.

Maybe it is telling us something about the kind of reality we're dealing with.

We can count the people in the room.

We can count the dollars in the offering.

We can count volunteers and programs and hours of service.

Keep the numbers.

They may help.

But if the point is transformation, eventually we need to look beyond the things that can be counted.

We need to ask harder questions.

Questions like:

**How do you measure forgiveness?**

## How Do You Measure Forgiveness?

So let's try.

How do you measure forgiveness?

We could make a survey.

**On a scale of one to ten, how completely have you forgiven the person who hurt you?**

Seven.

Excellent.

Up from six last quarter.

At this rate, we should achieve full forgiveness sometime in Q3.

The joke works because we immediately recognize that something has gone wrong.

Forgiveness is real.

It happens.

A person can spend years carrying anger toward someone and eventually release it.

A relationship can be restored.

A wound can lose its power.

Someone can remember what happened without being consumed by it.

Something changes.

We know that.

But what exactly would we count?

Days without thinking angry thoughts?

Number of hostile fantasies?

Heart rate when the person's name comes up?

Minutes required to stop rehearsing the argument in the shower?

We might be able to measure some of these things.

Researchers can study psychological and physiological effects associated with forgiveness. We can ask people questions. We can observe behaviors. We can look for patterns.

The problem isn't that forgiveness leaves no evidence.

The problem is that none of the evidence is forgiveness itself.

The reality is larger than any particular indicator we might choose.

The same is true of compassion.

Suppose I want to know whether I'm becoming more compassionate.

I could count charitable donations.

Hours volunteered.

People helped.

Kind messages sent.

Those might all tell me something.

But I can perform compassionate actions for remarkably uncompassionate reasons.

I can volunteer because I want to be seen volunteering.

I can give because I feel guilty.

I can help someone while quietly resenting every minute of it.

Conversely, I can become more compassionate without producing an obvious increase in measurable activity.

Maybe I become more patient with the person who annoys me.

Maybe I listen instead of immediately offering advice.

Maybe I become less certain that everyone who disagrees with me is an idiot.

That last one could substantially reduce activity on the internet.

Real change.

Difficult dashboard.

What about humility?

This one presents an especially entertaining measurement problem.

We could simply ask people how humble they've become.

Unfortunately, anyone reporting exceptional performance should probably lose points.

**Humility score: 9.7.**

**Top one percent globally.**

**Extremely proud of my progress.**

Some realities become distorted simply by the attempt to score them.

Love works this way too.

How much do I love my wife?

I could count date nights.

Texts.

Gifts.

Hours spent together.

Times I said, “I love you.”

Maybe those things matter.

If all of them suddenly disappeared, that might tell us something worth noticing.

But imagine telling my wife:

**Good news. Affection is up 11 percent year over year.**

I suspect she would have follow-up questions.

Because love isn't merely the sum of loving behaviors.

It includes them, but it also includes attention, loyalty, vulnerability, patience, desire, sacrifice, memory, commitment, and a thousand tiny decisions that would be absurd to record.

Some of the most loving things we do barely register as events.

Sitting beside someone.

Not saying the thing we could have said.

Getting up when the baby cries.

Listening to the same fear for the fifth time.

Staying.

There may be no number.

But there is reality.

This is an important distinction because our culture can subtly train us to treat measurability as evidence of seriousness.

If we can measure something, it feels concrete.

Objective.

Real.

If we cannot measure it, it begins to feel soft.

Subjective.

Maybe even sentimental.

But the limitations of an instrument do not establish the limitations of reality.

A bathroom scale cannot measure blood pressure.

That doesn't make blood pressure subjective.

A thermometer cannot tell me the distance to Chicago.

That doesn't mean Chicago is a matter of opinion.

Every instrument has a domain.

Measurement itself has a horizon.

This doesn't mean we should abandon evidence when talking about spiritual or interior life.

Quite the opposite.

If I claim I have become extraordinarily compassionate while everyone around me experiences me as cruel, perhaps my inner spiritual dashboard needs recalibration.

Our actions matter.

Our relationships matter.

Other people's experiences of us matter.

Reality still gets to disagree with the stories we tell about ourselves.

But evidence and reduction are not the same thing.

We can look for evidence of love without believing love can be contained in a score.

We can recognize wisdom without pretending to have a wisdom unit.

We can see generosity expressed through giving without assuming dollars are generosity.

We can notice the effects of forgiveness without believing we have captured forgiveness itself.

This requires something less tidy than measurement.

It requires judgment.

Attention.

Relationship.

Time.

Perhaps even discernment.

Those words make engineers nervous.

I understand.

I am one.

Give me a clean dataset and I will happily avoid the mysteries of the human heart for another afternoon.

But sometimes the ambiguity belongs to the thing itself.

The answer isn't always a better sensor.

Sometimes reality is asking us to pay attention differently.

This may be especially true in spiritual life.

A person can pray for years without being able to demonstrate a statistically significant increase in holiness.

Someone can sit silently every morning and have nothing to show for it.

No output.

No artifact.

No completed task.

No line going up and to the right.

From the perspective of the productivity dashboard, the result is remarkably clear.

**Productivity: zero.**

And yet it may be among the most important things that person does all day.

## Productivity: Zero

I practice contemplative prayer.

Sometimes that means sitting quietly for twenty minutes and doing almost nothing.

There is no agenda.

No list of requests.

No problem I'm trying to solve.

No insight I'm attempting to manufacture.

The practice is mostly an exercise in presence.

Sit.

Be still.

Notice the thoughts that appear.

Let them go.

Return to silence.

From the perspective of modern productivity culture, this is an astonishing waste of twenty minutes.

I could answer email.

Exercise.

Read.

Write.

Clean something.

Learn a language.

Listen to a podcast at twice the normal speed so I can become wise in half the time.

Instead, I sit in a chair.

**Productivity: zero.**

That's part of what I love about it.

For twenty minutes, I am not trying to make anything go up.

I'm not trying to become more efficient.

I'm not tracking output.

I'm not competing with anyone.

There is no personal best.

I cannot finish prayer 12 percent ahead of schedule and reinvest the savings elsewhere.

Nothing is being produced that I can point to afterward and say:

**There. That's what those twenty minutes were for.**

And yet I don't think nothing is happening.

Perhaps I become a little more aware of the machinery constantly running inside me.

The anxiety.

The rehearsed conversations.

The desire to control.

The judgments.

The endless commentary.

The strange internal committee that apparently believes every thought requires an immediate meeting.

Sitting quietly doesn't make all of that disappear.

But sometimes it creates a little space around it.

And that space may matter later.

Maybe someone says something that irritates me, and I don't react quite as quickly.

Maybe I notice that I'm becoming defensive before the defense comes out of my mouth.

Maybe I'm more present when someone is talking.

Maybe I become slightly less convinced that every uncomfortable feeling requires immediate action.

Maybe nothing dramatic happens at all.

This creates a terrible attribution problem.

Suppose I sit in silence on Monday morning and respond more patiently to someone on Thursday afternoon.

Was the prayer responsible?

I have no idea.

Maybe I slept better Wednesday night.

Maybe I happened to be in a good mood.

Maybe I've matured.

Maybe the other person was simply less annoying than usual.

Human beings are complicated systems.

We don't get clean control groups.

But this is true of many of the things that shape us most deeply.

A conversation changes us.

A friendship changes us.

A book changes us.

Grief changes us.

Music changes us.

Years of caring for another person change us.

Often we cannot identify the moment the change occurred.

There is no notification.

**Congratulations. Your capacity for empathy has increased.**

We notice it later.

Or someone else notices it.

We encounter a situation that once would have produced one response, and something different comes out of us.

The change reveals itself in the living.

This is difficult for a measurement culture because we like causation to be visible.

Do X.

Measure Y.

Observe improvement.

Repeat.

That pattern is enormously powerful.

It has given us medicine, engineering, science, manufacturing, and much of the modern world.

But not everything important in a human life develops through clean, attributable interventions.

Some things accumulate quietly.

They work beneath the level of measurement.

Their effects appear indirectly.

Sometimes years later.

Sometimes in another person.

Maybe I become more patient, and my patience changes the way someone experiences me.

Maybe that changes the way they respond.

Maybe that changes a relationship.

Where exactly would we put the sensor?

This doesn't mean contemplation is magically effective simply because it's difficult to measure.

That's another trap.

**Unmeasurable** does not mean **profound** any more than measurable means important.

I can sit silently for twenty minutes and accomplish nothing beyond discovering that my left foot has fallen asleep.

Mystery is not evidence.

The point is more modest.

Some practices may be valuable even when their value cannot be neatly separated, attributed, and counted.

We may have to evaluate them differently.

Over time.

Through experience.

Through relationships.

Through the kind of person we are becoming.

This is where spirituality introduces a different timescale.

Dashboards like frequent updates.

Daily active users.

Weekly trends.

Monthly targets.

Quarterly results.

The interior life can be annoyingly slow.

Patience may take years.

Forgiveness may take decades.

Grief keeps its own calendar.

Wisdom appears to have no respect whatsoever for quarterly planning.

Sometimes the wisest response to a spiritual practice is simply to keep showing up.

Not because the number is improving.

Because there is no number.

This is deeply countercultural.

We are accustomed to asking whether something is working.

That's a good question.

But **working** can quietly mean **producing a visible result quickly enough for me to confirm that my effort was worthwhile.**

Some of the most formative things in life refuse those terms.

Raising a child.

Building a marriage.

Becoming trustworthy.

Learning to forgive.

Learning to listen.

Learning to be still.

Their value emerges across time, through thousands of interactions too small to count.

Spiritual traditions have understood this for a very long time.

They often use organic language rather than mechanical language.

Roots.

Seeds.

Vines.

Trees.

Fruit.

Things that grow slowly.

Things that cannot be pulled upward to make them grow faster.

Things whose development is partly hidden underground.

That language feels very different from a dashboard.

A dashboard asks:

**What did we produce?**

A tree asks us to wait long enough to see what kind of fruit appears.

Maybe that gives us another way to think about spiritual growth.

Not as something we can perfectly measure.

But as something we can learn to recognize.
