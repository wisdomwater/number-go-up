# 2 - When the Measure Becomes the Target

### The Observer Enters the System

In the last chapter, we treated measurement as a way of looking at reality. We have something we care about, we find something measurable that tells us something about it, and we accept that the resulting number will necessarily leave part of the reality behind. The measurement is incomplete, but useful; it gives us a map.

There is just one complication when the territory happens to contain human beings: sometimes the territory notices that we're holding the map.

This is one of the fundamental differences between measuring a physical object and measuring a human system. If I put a ruler beside a board and discover that it's forty-seven inches long, the board doesn't become self-conscious about the result. It doesn't wonder whether forty-seven is good, whether management was hoping for forty-eight, or whether this unfortunate measurement is going to come up during its next performance review.

People are different. We notice what is being measured, who is doing the measuring, and whether the resulting number seems to matter. Once we notice, the measurement itself becomes part of the environment we're responding to.

There is a loose analogy here to what scientists call the observer effect. We don't need to wander very far into physics to make the point, and I have no intention of dragging quantum mechanics into a book about management dashboards if I can possibly avoid it. The useful idea is simply that obtaining information about a system sometimes requires interacting with that system, and the interaction itself can affect what we're trying to observe.

Human systems have their own version of this problem.

Suppose a company becomes concerned about how much time its engineers spend in meetings. This is not an unreasonable concern, particularly since engineers have been complaining about meetings since shortly after the invention of the meeting. Managers complain about them too, although managers occasionally express this concern by scheduling a meeting to discuss why everyone is attending so many meetings.

Eventually someone decides we need data.

So we start tracking meeting hours. Immediately, the measurement has a cost: someone has to collect the data, perhaps by analyzing calendars automatically, asking employees to categorize meetings, or having managers review reports. If the process becomes elaborate enough, we can eventually consume several hours of engineering work producing a number that tells us how many hours of engineering work we're consuming, which has a certain organizational elegance to it.

But the direct cost of producing the number is usually the less interesting part. Something else happens as soon as engineers learn that meeting hours are being tracked.

*Oh. They're watching meeting hours.*

Now people begin making slightly different decisions. Engineers may decline meetings they would otherwise have attended, managers may shorten recurring meetings, teams may move routine conversations into chat, and people may become more thoughtful about who actually needs to be in the room. If excessive meetings really were a problem, this could be exactly the result we hoped for.

But notice what happened. We began with what looked like an observational exercise—let's find out how much time people spend in meetings—and ended up changing the thing we were observing. The measurement directed attention toward meeting time, and that attention became one of the causes of what happened to meeting time next.

This isn't necessarily a flaw. If a hospital starts carefully tracking preventable infections, we hope people become more attentive to infection prevention. If a factory begins tracking injuries, increased attention to safety may be precisely the point, just as a software organization that measures customer-reported defects may genuinely want its engineers to care more about reducing them.

Measurement directs attention, and attention changes behavior. Once we recognize that, every metric introduced into a human system presents us with two questions rather than one:

**What will this measurement tell us?**

And perhaps less obviously:

**What will measuring this cause?**

Consider AI adoption. A software organization believes AI tools may help engineers learn faster, automate repetitive work, explore unfamiliar code, or solve certain problems more effectively, so leadership reasonably wants to know whether people are actually using them. Perhaps the organization begins tracking usage of approved AI tools and discovers that adoption is lower than expected.

Before that dashboard exists, an engineer encountering a problem might ask, *Would AI help me solve this?* Once AI usage becomes something the organization regularly reports and discusses, another question can quietly enter the room: *Where can I use AI?*

Those questions are close enough that we might not notice the difference, but their center of gravity isn't quite the same. The first begins with the work and considers whether AI is a useful tool; the second begins with the tool and goes looking for work that might justify using it.

That shift might even be desirable for a while. If leadership believes people are avoiding useful tools simply because they're unfamiliar with them, deliberately encouraging experimentation could help employees discover capabilities they would otherwise miss. But we should be clear about what we're doing: the organization is no longer simply observing AI adoption.

It is influencing it.

And once people know which number we're watching, that number begins acquiring a new kind of power.

### And Then We Attach Consequences

Attention alone can change behavior, but organizations rarely measure important things simply because they enjoy looking at charts. Eventually someone asks what the number ought to be. If AI adoption is 47 percent, perhaps it should be 60; if customer response time is eleven minutes, perhaps we could get it below ten.

Again, there is nothing unreasonable about this. We usually measure something because we care about the reality behind it, and if that reality can be improved, we would naturally like to improve it. The transition from measurement to target is often a perfectly sensible one.

Then consequences begin to appear.

Sometimes they're explicit: hit a sales quota and receive a bonus, miss a performance target and lose one, or reach the quarterly goal and receive additional funding. More often the consequences are softer. A team gets praised at an executive meeting, an employee is described as a high performer, a manager is asked to explain why a particular number is red, or a suspiciously large number of meetings suddenly appear on everyone's calendars because the dashboard has gone in an undesirable direction.

Anyone who has worked in a large organization for long enough understands the motivational power of not wanting to explain a red box on a PowerPoint slide.

None of this requires employees to be cynical. Human beings care about how we're perceived, whether our work is valued, whether our manager thinks we're competent, and whether we're considered cooperative or difficult. Employment also has the inconvenient property of being connected to things like food, housing, and health insurance, which gives workplace signals a little more emotional weight than the average spreadsheet might suggest.

Imagine, then, a customer-service organization trying to improve efficiency. Management notices that very long calls often correlate with confused customers, poor training, cumbersome software, or representatives who are having difficulty resolving problems. Average call duration seems like useful information, so the organization starts tracking it.

There is nothing absurd about the metric. If one representative routinely needs forty minutes to resolve problems everyone else handles in ten, that's worth understanding, and if average call duration suddenly doubles across an entire organization, a manager would be negligent not to wonder why.

Then we set a target: eight minutes.

For straightforward calls, everything works beautifully. A customer calls with a familiar problem, the representative resolves it in six minutes, and everyone wins. The customer is happy, the employee's number looks good, and somewhere a dashboard becomes slightly greener.

Eventually, however, a customer calls with a complicated problem. Maybe several things have gone wrong at once, or the customer is confused, or they're elderly and need a little more time walking through the solution. Eight minutes arrives and the problem isn't solved.

At that moment, the employee isn't making only one decision anymore. They are deciding how best to help the customer while simultaneously thinking about the system that is recording how long the call takes.

If the eight-minute target is merely informational, perhaps that doesn't matter very much. But suppose call duration appears on the employee's performance dashboard, their manager asks about it every week, bonuses depend partly on it, or people with longer calls tend to receive worse performance ratings. Continuing to help the customer now carries a measurable cost.

This is where the perspective of the measurer and the measuree can begin to diverge.

The manager may sincerely think of call duration as one data point among many. They may care deeply about customer satisfaction, quality of resolution, employee judgment, and the context surrounding an unusual call. If asked, they might say, quite truthfully, “We're not saying every call has to be under eight minutes. We're just trying to understand the trend.”

The employee sees something slightly different. They see their name next to a red number, know that their manager can see it, and suspect that their manager's manager can probably see it too. They also know that performance decisions will eventually be made, while having much less certainty about how much context will survive when those decisions happen.

For the measurer, perhaps it really is one data point. For the measuree, it may be the data point with their name next to it.

This doesn't mean the manager is lying or the employee is paranoid. They simply occupy different positions in the system, and those positions give the same number a different kind of weight.

Organizations are full of messages whose meaning changes as they travel. An executive sees a dashboard and asks a vice president why a number moved; the vice president asks a director, who asks a manager, who eventually asks the employee closest to the work. Nobody in this chain has to be unreasonable, and every person may genuinely be trying to understand what happened.

Still, by the time the question reaches the person whose work produced the number, *We're curious about this trend* can acquire an emotional texture surprisingly close to *Make the number go up.*

Or down, depending on the dashboard.

This is one reason power matters whenever we measure people. The person designing or reviewing the metric generally has more freedom to regard it as information, while the person being measured has to consider the possibility of consequences. A metric can therefore carry considerably more authority at the bottom of an organization than the people at the top realize they placed on it.

The measurer sees information. The measuree experiences expectation.

Once we add a target, a bonus, a rating, a promotion, a public ranking, or simply the knowledge that someone important is watching, expectation becomes incentive.

And incentives have a way of making people creative.

### Goodhart's Law

Economist Charles Goodhart is associated with an observation that has become almost unavoidable whenever people talk about metrics: **when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.** Like most wonderfully concise sayings, it needs a little qualification, but it describes something important about what happens when we attach consequences to proxies.

A metric doesn't become worthless the instant we try to improve it. If my doctor tells me my blood pressure is too high and I change my habits until it comes down, nobody needs to conclude that blood-pressure cuffs have been ruined by Goodhart's Law. The interesting problem appears when I can improve the number without improving the reality the number was supposed to represent.

Return to our customer-service example. Average call duration originally gave us information about efficiency, and if calls become shorter because employees receive better training, the software improves, or common problems become easier to solve, then the falling number reflects exactly the kind of improvement we wanted.

But those aren't the only ways to make calls shorter.

A representative can rush customers through explanations, transfer difficult calls, discourage questions, or find a reason to end the interaction before everything has been resolved. In every case, the average call duration may improve, even though the customer experience hasn't improved with it.

The organization wanted efficient customer service. It asked for shorter calls because shorter calls appeared to be evidence of efficient customer service. Those things are related, but they aren't identical, and the difference between them gives people room to improve the proxy independently of the purpose.

This suggests one of my favorite questions to ask about any proposed metric:

**How could someone make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing I care about?**

I don't mean the question cynically. Most employees are not sitting at their desks plotting elaborate schemes to corrupt the quarterly dashboard, and if we approach measurement with the assumption that everyone is trying to cheat, we will create a workplace considerably worse than the one we're trying to improve.

The question is really about understanding the distance between the proxy and the purpose. If the number can improve only when the underlying reality improves, we have found an unusually strong metric. More often there are several paths by which the number can move, some of which take us toward what we care about and some of which merely take us toward a better number.

Software engineering provides almost unlimited opportunities to observe this.

I once worked in an environment where speed mattered a great deal. We needed engineers to move quickly, solve problems, keep projects advancing, and respond when things went wrong. There was nothing unreasonable about wanting urgency in a software organization.

One engineer was particularly good at producing visible work. Code appeared quickly, bugs were fixed quickly, and there was always evidence that something was happening. Unfortunately, some of the code produced quickly also created bugs, which then produced more visible work when those bugs were discovered and assigned.

The engineer fixed those quickly too.

From the perspective of a measurement system built around activity, this could look magnificent. Code written, bug discovered, bug assigned, bug fixed, another bug discovered, another bug fixed. The machine had achieved perpetual motion.

Meanwhile, another engineer might spend more time thinking before making the change. They might inspect the architecture, test an assumption, ask an annoying question in a design review, or notice that the obvious solution would create a much larger problem three months later. Eventually they write the code, it works, and then almost nothing happens.

Which is precisely the problem.

There is no dramatic bug report, heroic debugging session, emergency patch, or collection of visible artifacts demonstrating how quickly the engineer responded. The value of the work exists partly in events that never occurred.

Prevention has terrible marketing.

This doesn't mean the fast engineer was careless or the careful engineer was wise. Software is difficult, excellent engineers create bugs, speed genuinely matters, and excessive caution can become its own form of dysfunction. Nobody deliberately designed a system intended to reward defective software.

The organization wanted urgency, responsiveness, and visible progress, and the measurements were attempts to see those things. The problem was that the proxy made some forms of contribution easier to observe than others.

Activity leaves evidence. Prevention often leaves absence.

The careful engineer's contribution may be a bug that was never filed, a customer escalation that never happened, or a redesign that never became necessary. Those are real forms of value, but they are awkward things to enter into a quarterly tracking system.

*How many disasters did you prevent this quarter? Please enter a whole number.*

This is one of the recurring difficulties of measurement. We tend to reward things that leave artifacts because artifacts are countable, and that can slowly make visible activity look more valuable than invisible prevention. The artifacts aren't meaningless; we simply have to be careful about confusing visibility with value.

### Rational Adaptation

When people respond to incentives in ways we don't like, we often say they're gaming the system. Sometimes that's exactly what is happening: people falsify data, hide failures, manipulate reports, or deliberately exploit loopholes they know violate the purpose of the measurement. That's dishonesty, and calling it rational adaptation would be an unnecessarily polite way of describing it.

But much of what gets labeled gaming is more ordinary.

People adapt to the environment we give them.

Suppose I'm an engineer with five bugs assigned to me. One is easy and relatively low impact; I understand it and can probably close it before the end of the day. Another is difficult and potentially important, and it may take several days merely to understand what's happening before I can even estimate how long the fix will take.

Now suppose my organization pays close attention to average bug-closure time. Which bug should I work on?

There may be excellent reasons to choose the difficult one. It could affect an important customer, reveal a deeper architectural problem, or prevent future failures. But the easy bug has one interesting property the difficult bug does not: I know exactly how it will look on the dashboard.

If bug-closure time is one of the ways my performance is evaluated, that knowledge matters. I don't need to be dishonest, lazy, or indifferent to the product; I may care deeply about doing good work while also understanding the environment in which that work will be judged.

We created the game and published the rules. We shouldn't be surprised when people learn how to play it.

This is why employee behavior can sometimes be diagnostic of the measurement system itself. If a team starts choosing easier bugs, avoiding risky projects, or breaking work into smaller units because smaller units score better, we may indeed have a problem with employee behavior. But before correcting the employees, another question is worth asking:

**What behavior have we made rational?**

Imagine an engineer with a free Friday afternoon. She could ship a small feature that will appear immediately in the tracking system, refactor a fragile part of the codebase that has been causing subtle problems for months, or spend the afternoon helping a newer engineer understand a difficult part of the system.

All three might create value. Only one may produce a satisfying green box by Monday morning.

The feature creates visible evidence quickly, while the refactoring may prevent problems nobody will ever know were prevented and the mentoring may produce most of its value months later in someone else's work. The measurement system hasn't ordered the engineer to choose the feature, but it has changed the economics of the decision.

This matters because work is always competing for attention. Most organizations have far more potentially useful work than time to do it, so people constantly make choices about what deserves the next hour, day, or week. If some kinds of work reliably produce recognized evidence while other kinds depend on someone eventually noticing their long-term effect, visible work acquires a structural advantage.

Over time, invisible work can begin to disappear without anyone ever deciding that it was unimportant. The system simply made something else easier to justify.

Power complicates this further. A senior engineer with years of credibility may be comfortable saying, “I know this won't help my metric, but it's the right thing to do.” A newer employee, someone who recently received a poor performance rating, or someone worried about layoffs may quite reasonably be less willing to make a choice that requires explaining a bad number later.

People don't optimize only for rewards. They optimize for safety.

This is how a manager can sincerely tell employees to “use your judgment” while maintaining a measurement system that quietly makes certain kinds of judgment risky. Both messages may be genuine, but the employee still has to decide which one is more likely to carry consequences.

None of this means accountability should disappear. Context can become an extraordinarily flexible explanation if we allow it to excuse every poor result, and sometimes the straightforward interpretation really is the right one. A bug took too long because somebody handled it badly, a project slipped because decisions were poor, or an employee is genuinely underperforming.

Numbers help us see those things, which is one reason we need them.

The point is that behavior around the metric becomes evidence too. If capable people repeatedly make choices that seem irrational from the standpoint of the larger goal but perfectly rational from the standpoint of the measurement system, we should at least consider the possibility that the system is telling us something about itself.

### The Metric Eats Its Own Signal

There is one more complication, and in some ways it is the strangest one. Once people begin optimizing a metric, the metric itself becomes harder to interpret.

Return again to average call duration. Suppose it falls from eleven minutes to eight. We know the measurement is accurate; calls really are shorter.

What we don't yet know is why.

Perhaps the organization improved its training, gave representatives better tools, or removed unnecessary steps from the process. Perhaps employees became more skilled and customers are receiving better help in less time. If so, the number is telling a very encouraging story.

Or perhaps employees learned that long calls hurt their numbers and began ending them sooner.

The same dashboard can display an eight-minute average in both worlds.

Before the target existed, call duration may have been a relatively natural observation of how long calls happened to take. After employees know that eight minutes is the desired result, the measurement contains something new: it contains the work itself, but it also contains people's response to being measured.

Once people begin optimizing a metric, we have to interpret the metric in light of the optimization.

This is easy to forget because our instincts often lead us in the opposite direction. A metric becomes important, so we watch it more closely; because we watch it more closely, people pay more attention to it; because people pay more attention, they begin optimizing it; and then, precisely because everyone is paying attention to the number, we become tempted to give it even more authority.

But optimization may have made the signal noisier at exactly the moment we became more confident in it.

Suppose AI usage doubles over six months. That might mean engineers discovered valuable workflows, training improved, or employees who were initially hesitant became comfortable with the tools. It might also mean people noticed that leadership was watching AI usage and began using AI in situations where they previously wouldn't have bothered.

Most likely, reality contains some mixture of all of those things.

The usage number still tells us something. It simply doesn't tell us exactly what it told us before increasing AI usage became an organizational goal.

This is why a healthy measurement system needs room for two sentences that initially sound uncomfortable:

**The number looks bad, but...**

and

**The number looks great, but...**

If every red number must indicate failure, people will learn to avoid red numbers, and if every green number must indicate success, people will learn to produce green ones. Human beings are remarkably good at adapting to whatever definition of success we make consequential enough.

Reality is less cooperative. Sometimes a metric gets worse because a team chose to tackle a difficult and important problem, while another metric improves because people discovered a shortcut that quietly damages the thing we actually care about. And, of course, sometimes a bad number really does mean things are going badly, just as a good number can reflect genuine improvement.

The point isn't to distrust the number. It's to remain curious about what produced it.

That brings us back to the questions we began with. **What will this measurement tell us? What will measuring this cause?** Once we turn the measurement into a target, we can add a third: **How could someone make this number look fantastic without improving the thing I care about?**

Those questions won't solve every problem with metrics, but they make something possible that a dashboard alone cannot provide: conversation. A manager can notice that an engineer's bug-closure time is unusually long and ask what happened rather than beginning with an accusation, while the engineer can explain that a difficult bug exposed an architectural problem without pretending that every delay is automatically justified.

The number gives us a reason to look. The conversation helps us understand what we find, and judgment still has work to do.

That's the important difference between measuring a board and measuring a person. The board doesn't know the target, alter its behavior, protect itself from consequences, or discover that forty-eight inches would be more career-enhancing than forty-seven. People notice the measurement, interpret what it means, and adapt to the environment it creates.

When we introduce a metric into a human system, then, we aren't merely installing a thermometer. We're changing the temperature.

That doesn't mean we stop measuring. It means we measure with our eyes open, aware that the dashboard is no longer simply a picture of the system but has become one of the forces acting inside it.

And that leaves us with another problem. Dashboards are useful precisely because they compress all this complexity into something a manager can see.

What happens when the compressed view begins to feel more real than the work itself?
