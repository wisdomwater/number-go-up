# Chapter 2: When the Measure Becomes the Target

## The Observer Enters the System

There is an idea from physics that has escaped the laboratory and become part of popular culture: the observer effect.

It is often explained badly, especially when quantum mechanics gets involved. You'll sometimes hear that particles somehow know we're watching them, as though electrons are shy little creatures that behave differently when scientists enter the room.

That's not really what's happening.

The important idea is much simpler: **you cannot always measure something without interacting with it.**

To measure a system, you have to get information out of it somehow. At very small scales, that interaction can itself affect the thing being measured. The measuring instrument is not necessarily standing outside reality, silently taking notes. It has entered the system.

Human systems have their own version of this problem.

Suppose I want to know how much time engineers spend in meetings.

At first this sounds like a straightforward measurement problem. Track meetings. Count hours. Add them up. Produce a graph.

But how am I going to collect the data?

Maybe I ask every engineer to categorize their calendar. Now hundreds of engineers have to spend time maintaining accurate calendars.

Maybe I create a weekly survey. Now they have to fill out the survey.

Maybe I build software that automatically analyzes calendars. Now someone has to design, build, validate, maintain, and explain the system. Someone else will probably have to create a dashboard.

The dashboard is inevitable.

None of these costs necessarily make the measurement a bad idea. The information might be valuable enough to justify them.

But measurement is not free.

This is easy to forget because the finished number looks effortless. A chart appears at an executive meeting showing that engineers spend 31 percent of their time in meetings. What the chart doesn't show is how many hours of engineering work were consumed producing the number telling us how many hours of engineering work were being consumed.

There is a certain elegance to that.

Every measurement has a cost.

Sometimes the cost is trivial. A computer records a timestamp automatically and nobody needs to think about it.

Sometimes the cost is enormous. Organizations can build entire reporting structures around collecting, cleaning, validating, summarizing, presenting, and explaining metrics. People create reports for people who create reports for people who present reports to people who request additional reports.

Eventually we may need a metric to determine whether all the metrics are helping.

But the direct cost of measurement is only the beginning.

The more interesting cost appears when people become aware that the measurement exists.

Imagine management announces that it wants to understand how much time engineers are spending in meetings. Engineers are told not to worry. Nobody is being evaluated. Leadership is simply trying to understand where time goes.

Fair enough.

But now I know someone is looking.

I receive a meeting invitation and think about the metric.

Does this meeting count?

Should I decline it?

Should I mark it differently on my calendar?

Does a design review count as a meeting or engineering work?

What about a one-on-one with my manager?

What about the thirty-minute conversation I had with another engineer that solved a problem we'd been stuck on for two days?

Before the measurement existed, these were simply activities. Now they are categories.

And categories invite interpretation.

This is where measuring a human system becomes fundamentally different from measuring a board.

The board does not care. Put a ruler beside it and nothing happens. The board doesn't notice that it is forty-seven inches long and begin wondering whether management prefers forty-eight.

Human beings notice.

We notice what is being counted. We notice who is asking. We notice how often they ask. We notice which numbers appear on dashboards and which numbers disappear into databases never to be mentioned again.

And then we begin trying to understand what the measurement means.

Why are they tracking this?

Is thirty-one percent too high?

What number do they want?

Is this going to affect staffing?

Is this going to affect performance reviews?

Should I be doing something differently?

These questions can arise before anyone attaches a formal consequence to the metric. Nobody needs to announce a target. Nobody needs to threaten anyone's job.

The act of paying attention already communicates something.

**This matters.**

That's an effect on the system.

Measurement doesn't merely extract information. In a human system, it can introduce information as well.

It tells the people being measured something about the people doing the measuring.

What they're curious about.

What they're worried about.

What they're trying to improve.

What they might eventually reward.

What they might eventually punish.

This doesn't mean people always react badly to being measured. Sometimes measurement improves behavior in exactly the way we hope.

If I start tracking how many steps I take each day, I may walk more.

If a hospital tracks hand-washing compliance, staff may wash their hands more consistently.

If a software team begins measuring build times, engineers may notice inefficiencies they had previously ignored and make the system faster.

Measurement can focus attention. That's one of its strengths.

But we should recognize what just happened. We didn't merely observe the system.

We changed it.

The hospital isn't simply discovering how often people wash their hands. The act of measuring hand washing may be increasing hand washing.

My fitness tracker isn't simply observing how much I walk. Knowing that I'm at 8,732 steps may cause me to take a completely unnecessary lap around the kitchen before bed because apparently I am now the kind of person who cannot sleep with an unfinished circle on his watch.

The measurement has become part of the causal system.

This is why metrics involving human behavior require a different kind of thinking.

We have to ask not only:

**What will this measurement tell us?**

We also have to ask:

**What will measuring this cause?**

Those are separate questions.

A metric can provide useful information while simultaneously changing behavior. It can improve one behavior while unintentionally discouraging another. It can direct attention toward something important while pulling attention away from something equally important.

And once consequences are attached, these effects become much stronger.

We'll get there soon.

For now, it's enough to recognize that the observer has entered the system.

The moment people know which number you're watching, the measurement is no longer merely describing their world.

It has become part of their world.

## Oh, They’re Watching This

There is a moment that happens whenever a new metric appears at work.

You hear that management has started tracking something.

Maybe it's bugs closed.

Maybe it's meeting hours.

Maybe it's customer response time.

Maybe it's how often employees use AI tools.

And somewhere in your brain, a tiny light turns on.

**Oh. They're watching this.**

Nobody has to tell you what to do next.

You start paying attention too.

This is one of the simplest ways measurement changes human behavior. What gets measured gets attention, and attention is limited.

Imagine an organization introduces a dashboard tracking AI adoption. Leadership explains that the goal is simply to understand how employees are using new tools. Nobody is required to hit a quota. There are no performance consequences. It's just information.

Then the dashboard appears in the monthly meeting.

AI adoption is 42 percent.

Someone asks why it isn't higher.

The following month, it's 47 percent.

Better.

A few teams are highlighted for particularly strong adoption. Their managers explain some of the things they've been doing. Leadership is pleased.

Still no quota.

Still no performance consequence.

But does anyone really believe the measurement is neutral anymore?

The organization has communicated something very clearly without putting it in a policy document:

**We care about this.**

That message changes how people allocate attention.

An engineer who might previously have asked, “Would AI help me solve this problem?” may begin asking a slightly different question:

“Where can I use AI?”

Those questions sound similar, but they aren't.

The first begins with the problem.

The second begins with the metric.

Maybe the answer is the same. Perhaps AI genuinely is the best tool for the job. But the center of gravity has shifted.

This happens everywhere.

A teacher learns that standardized test scores are receiving increased attention. She thinks about test scores more.

A doctor learns that patient appointment times are being tracked. He thinks about appointment times more.

A customer service representative learns that average call duration is on the dashboard. She notices how long calls are taking.

A software engineer learns that bug closure rates are being reviewed. He becomes much more aware of how many bugs he closes.

None of these people are cheating.

They're paying attention.

And attention is one of our scarcest resources.

Every hour spent improving one thing is an hour not spent improving something else. Every concern that moves toward the front of our minds pushes something else slightly toward the back.

This means measurement creates priorities even when nobody explicitly announces them.

Suppose a manager tells her team:

“Quality is our highest priority.”

Then every Monday she reviews delivery speed.

On Tuesday she asks why the team missed its schedule.

On Wednesday she sends an email comparing delivery rates across teams.

On Friday she congratulates the group that shipped the most features.

Once a quarter, she reminds everyone that quality is the highest priority.

What does the team believe?

People are remarkably good at distinguishing stated values from operational values.

We learn what actually matters by watching where attention goes.

This is why the absence of a formal target doesn't make a metric harmless or ineffective. Repeated attention is itself a signal.

People notice what gets discussed.

They notice what gets graphed.

They notice which numbers require explanations when they go down.

They notice which numbers cause leaders to smile when they go up.

Eventually, they begin anticipating the questions.

This is especially powerful when the people doing the measuring have authority over the people being measured.

A senior executive may look at a dashboard and think:

*Interesting. AI usage increased six percent.*

An employee several levels below may look at the same dashboard and think:

*I should probably be using more AI.*

Same number.

Different experience.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences expectation.

That expectation may never have been intended. Leadership may sincerely want people to use judgment. They may genuinely mean it when they say, “Use AI where it makes sense.”

But humans don't interpret communication only through words.

We interpret emphasis.

If you tell me to use my judgment but repeatedly ask me about one particular number, I will eventually incorporate that number into my judgment.

That's not irrational.

I'm learning the environment.

And sometimes that's exactly what we want measurement to do.

If a hospital starts prominently tracking preventable infections, I hope people pay more attention to preventable infections. If a factory starts tracking serious safety incidents, increased attention to safety may be enormously beneficial.

Measurement can focus a system on something it has neglected.

That's power.

But power needs to be handled deliberately.

Because every time we turn the organizational spotlight toward one thing, other things remain outside the beam.

The question isn't simply whether the thing we're measuring matters.

The question is what happens when everyone realizes that it matters **to the people watching the numbers**.

At first, perhaps they simply pay more attention.

Then someone sets a target.

And things get considerably more interesting.
