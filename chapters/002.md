# 2 — Number Go Up {#chapter-2-number-go-up}

There is a particular kind of satisfaction in watching something move in the direction you intended. You change something, wait for reality to respond, and there it is: faster, stronger, larger, smaller, depending on what you were trying to accomplish. The number gives the change a satisfying precision, but the pleasure isn't really in the number itself. The pleasure is in discovering that the world responded to something you did.

Software gives me this feeling with almost embarrassing reliability. A piece of code takes eight hundred milliseconds to perform an operation, I change the implementation, rerun the benchmark, and now it takes four hundred. Assuming I haven't broken something else in the process—and software engineers learn to add that qualification fairly early—there isn't much ambiguity about what happened. I wanted the number to go down, I did something, and it went down. That feels good.

Chapter 1 was about discovering this relationship between measurement and mastery. Once we know that reality will give us feedback, however, another possibility appears. We don't have to wait passively for improvement. We can pursue it.

That small change in posture has enormous consequences. Instead of merely noticing that something became better, we begin asking what made it better and whether we can do more of that. We establish a baseline, change something, measure again, and keep the changes that work. Improvement becomes deliberate.

Eventually we give this process a name: optimization.

### The Pleasure of Making Something Move

Optimization can sound sterile when written on a whiteboard, but the experience itself is deeply human. Anyone who has spent an afternoon fixing something knows the pleasure of finally finding the adjustment that makes it work. The engine stops making the noise, the query finishes in half the time, the budget finally balances, or the process that used to require twelve steps now requires four. Something that resisted us has become more cooperative.

There is a reason engineers can spend unreasonable amounts of time shaving milliseconds from operations that were already pretty fast. Part of the motivation may be practical, especially when those milliseconds occur millions of times. But there is also the simple pleasure of seeing whether we can do it. A problem has presented us with a boundary, and optimization asks whether the boundary can be moved.

Numbers make that game possible because they give improvement a direction. “Make it better” is difficult to act upon, while “reduce the time this takes” gives us somewhere to look. We profile the system, find the expensive operation, change it, and see what happens. If the number improves, we have evidence that our theory was useful.

Then something else happens: success invites another question. Four hundred milliseconds is much better than eight hundred, but could it be three hundred? Once we reach three hundred, perhaps two hundred is possible. The improved number becomes the new baseline from which improvement begins again.

This is one of the subtle characteristics of optimization that will matter later. Optimization is not naturally oriented toward arrival; it is oriented toward direction. Faster can usually become faster still, cheaper can become cheaper, and more can become more. The process does not contain its own stopping rule.

That isn't necessarily a problem. If a computer becomes faster, more efficient, and more reliable every few years, I am quite happy for the people designing it to continue their suspicious inability to leave well enough alone. Much of technological progress comes from people asking whether something already good could be made slightly better. Repeated often enough, slightly better becomes a different world.

### From Improvement to a System

The power of optimization becomes clearer when we stop treating it as an isolated event and turn it into a system. Instead of waiting until something obviously goes wrong, we decide what good performance looks like, establish ways to observe it, and create regular opportunities to adjust. Feedback stops being occasional. It becomes part of how the work operates.

This is familiar to anyone who has worked in a large organization. Projects have milestones, budgets have forecasts, products have quality measures, teams have objectives, and leaders have dashboards intended to show whether things are moving in the desired direction. None of these tools guarantees success. They make it possible to notice sooner when reality has begun disagreeing with the plan.

That matters because large projects rarely fail all at once. A dependency slips, an assumption turns out to be wrong, defects accumulate, costs rise, or work that seemed straightforward becomes unexpectedly complicated. Without feedback, each problem can remain local until enough of them accumulate into a surprise large enough to reach leadership. Measurement gives the organization a chance to see trouble while there is still time to respond.

At its best, this creates a healthy rhythm. Set a direction, observe what is happening, learn from the difference, and adjust. The plan provides intention without pretending to know the future perfectly. Measurement provides feedback without requiring every deviation to become a crisis.

Anyone who has been through enough project reviews has also seen the opposite rhythm. A milestone slips, the status turns yellow, and the first response is an energetic discussion about how soon the status can become green again. Sometimes the correct answer is to work the problem. Other times the correct answer is to admit that the original plan was wrong.

That distinction matters because optimization is only as good as the objective guiding it. Making a number move is not difficult if we are willing to change the wrong things. The harder question is whether movement in the number still corresponds to improvement in the thing we actually care about. For now, however, the important point is simpler: organizations learned to build feedback directly into the way work gets done.

### How We Built the Corporate World

A company cannot operate on good intentions alone. It cannot announce that it intends to make excellent products, serve customers, treat employees well, deliver on time, and remain financially healthy, then assume all of those things are happening because everyone seems busy. People need ways to know whether the organization is actually doing what it intends to do. Measurement becomes part of the infrastructure.

Revenue and expenses tell one story, schedules and milestones another, while defects, customer behavior, inventory, capacity, and hundreds of other signals reveal different parts of the organization. No single measure contains the company, and nobody expects it to. Together they provide enough feedback for thousands of people to make coordinated decisions. That is an extraordinary achievement.

Consider what it takes to build something like a modern processor. Thousands of people work on different pieces of a system whose complete complexity exceeds what any one person could hold in their head. Teams make decisions whose consequences affect teams they may never meet, and work begun years earlier has to arrive at roughly the right place at roughly the right time. Somehow, after enough planning, testing, coordination, revision, argument, and caffeine, a physical object eventually appears.

This kind of work is impossible without abstraction. A person responsible for one component may understand it in exquisite detail, while someone coordinating hundreds of components needs a different view. Information has to move through the organization in forms that allow people to decide where attention is required. We will return to what gets lost in that process later, because the loss matters.

But first it is worth appreciating what the process makes possible. Large organizations can coordinate knowledge and effort across boundaries no individual could cross alone. A hospital, an airplane, a semiconductor, an operating system, or a global supply chain represents the accumulated work of people who each understand only part of the whole. Measurement and planning allow those parts to meet.

This is why I don't find dashboards inherently depressing. A dashboard is often simply someone trying to understand a system too large to experience directly. The person three levels above me may be looking at thirty projects and deciding which five need help. The detail that makes my project understandable to me might make their view unusable.

Of course, corporate life occasionally produces reports whose primary purpose appears to be proving that reports were produced. Anyone who has spent enough time in a large organization has filled out a spreadsheet while wondering whether another human being will ever look at it. Bureaucracy is what happens when systems designed to create visibility develop survival instincts of their own. But the absurdities should not obscure the underlying achievement.

We learned how to coordinate intention at scale.

### Becoming One of the Adults in the Room

There is a parallel process happening to the people inside these organizations. Early in a career, someone gives us a relatively well-defined problem and checks whether we solved it correctly. Later, we're trusted with problems whose solutions aren't obvious. Eventually we may be expected to decide which problems need solving in the first place.

There is a strange moment somewhere in a long career when you realize that you have become one of the adults in the room. People ask what you think, younger employees assume you know things, and decisions that once would have occurred several levels above you now arrive in your inbox with the expectation that you will figure them out. You may not feel dramatically wiser than you did twenty years ago. Apparently nobody consulted your feelings before updating your responsibilities.

That progression can bring genuine satisfaction. Competence feels good, being trusted feels good, and a promotion can mean more than the additional money attached to it. It can mean that years of ordinary work have accumulated into something other people recognize. There is no need to reduce ambition to ego in order to examine its limits later.

Much of mastery is surprisingly boring while it is happening. We show up, solve today's problem, learn something, make a mistake, correct it, and come back tomorrow. Nobody makes a movie about the engineer who quietly became excellent by doing competent work for twenty-five years. Yet this is how a great deal of expertise is actually created.

The same pattern helps explain why experienced people often make difficult work look easier than it is. They have seen versions of the problem before, know which details deserve attention, and have accumulated enough failed approaches to avoid some of them without consciously replaying the history. What looks like instinct is often compressed experience. Years of feedback have become judgment.

Organizations depend upon this accumulated mastery. Processes and dashboards matter, but so do people who have learned how to interpret what the system is telling them. A red indicator may represent a crisis, a temporary anomaly, a bad assumption, or simply a number that needs more context. Experience helps determine which one we are looking at.

This is the corporate version of number go up. We become more capable, the systems around us become more sophisticated, and increasingly complicated work becomes possible. The reward for learning how to handle complexity is usually being given more complexity. Somehow this is considered a promotion.

### When Everyone Knows the Score

Up to this point, the feedback loop has behaved reasonably well. We decide what we want, observe what is happening, and adjust when reality disagrees. The system may be imperfect, but the underlying logic is sound. It works particularly well when the thing being optimized has no opinion about the optimization.

Software does not know it is being measured. A processor does not become anxious because its benchmark results are being reviewed by senior leadership. A database does not notice that another database is outperforming it and begin choosing easier queries to protect its annual rating. Machines can be complicated, but they are wonderfully indifferent to office politics.

People are not.

The moment a measure becomes visible to the people whose behavior affects it, the feedback loop changes. A team learns which numbers leadership watches, which questions appear in reviews, and which outcomes require explanations. Nobody has to issue a formal instruction for people to begin noticing what the organization rewards with attention. We are very good at learning the rules of environments we inhabit.

This does not immediately make the measurement bad. If a project is late, quality is deteriorating, or customers are unhappy, people should know. Visibility can expose problems that would otherwise remain hidden. But the number has acquired a second function.

It is still information about the work, but it is now also information about what the organization appears to value.

That distinction is easy to miss because the dashboard looks exactly the same. The manager may see a signal that deserves investigation, while the employee may see a standard against which their performance will eventually be judged. Both can be reasonable interpretations. The difference lies in where each person is standing.

Once that happens, optimization becomes more interesting. We are no longer observing a system that remains indifferent to observation. We are measuring people who can see the measurement, interpret what it means, and change their behavior in response. The feedback loop now contains participants who understand that they are inside it.

That is where our beautifully simple story begins to become complicated.

The number hasn't stopped describing the game.

But it has become part of how the game is played.
