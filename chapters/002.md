# When the Territory Sees the Map

> A machine does not care that you are measuring it. A person does.

## When the System Knows You Are Watching

There is something wonderfully uncomplicated about measuring a machine. If I time a piece of software and discover that it takes ten seconds to run, the software does not feel embarrassed. It does not notice that another program finished in eight seconds, wonder how that comparison will affect its annual review, or decide that next time it should arrange its work so the benchmark looks a little better. It simply does whatever the system causes it to do.

That was largely the world of the previous chapter. Measure something, observe the result, make an adjustment, and measure again. The feedback changes the person doing the measuring because we learn from what we see, but the memory controller does not become anxious when someone opens its log file. The cache does not develop ambitions, and the test tool does not spend Sunday evening worrying about what the dashboard will look like Monday morning.

Human beings make the loop more interesting because we can look back. We notice what is being measured, form theories about why it matters, remember what happened the last time someone took a risk, and adjust accordingly. Once a metric is tied to praise, scrutiny, compensation, promotion, reputation, or merely the prospect of an unpleasant meeting, it becomes part of the environment in which choices are made.

There is a kind of observer effect here, although the analogy only goes so far. Observation does not mysteriously change us simply because someone is watching. We change because we understand what the observer cares about and can anticipate some of the consequences. Rational creatures do rational things, even when the resulting system looks slightly ridiculous from a distance.

That adds another layer to the feedback loop. Management measures behavior to understand what is happening, employees see what management is measuring and respond, management observes the changed behavior, and everyone learns a little more about the game. The measurement has stopped sitting quietly outside the system. It has entered the system itself.

## The Score Behind the Score

For years, we worked with quarterly goals called MBOs—Management by Objectives. The idea was reasonable enough: set clear goals, make them ambitious, and push the organization to move faster than it otherwise might. We were encouraged to pull schedules in, make stretch commitments, and take on more risk in the name of speed. On paper, the message was that boldness mattered.

At the same time, another phrase appeared constantly in our business update meetings: *flawless execution*. Missing a milestone meant explanations, recovery plans, and sometimes a mark that followed you into the yearly focal review. The language around risk-taking remained, but the consequences around missing were much easier to feel. It did not take an advanced degree in incentive theory to figure out which message carried more weight.

People adjusted their commitments accordingly. If you accepted an aggressive schedule and missed it, you had created a problem for yourself and usually for several people above you. If you built in more room, hit the milestone, or delivered a little early, you were far more likely to be congratulated for meeting your objectives. One message said, *stretch*. The system whispered, a bit more convincingly, *whatever you do, don't miss*.

Nobody needed to issue a memo instructing people to pad schedules. The lesson was taught through repetition. Engineers and managers watched what happened to other people, remembered what had happened to themselves, and developed a sense for how much risk the organization actually wanted rather than how much it said it wanted. This was not necessarily cynicism; most of the time it was simply pattern recognition applied to corporate life.

Organizations communicate their values in at least two languages. One is spoken in meetings, written on slides, and printed in leadership principles. The other is expressed through what gets rewarded, what gets punished, and what creates an escalation at four o'clock on a Friday afternoon. When the two disagree, people tend to become fluent in the second.

## What the System Teaches

This is where metrics begin doing more than reporting performance. Every review, milestone discussion, recognition program, and uncomfortable conversation sends information back into the organization. People learn which outcomes matter enough to attract attention and which values become negotiable once a deadline is in danger.

A company can sincerely say that it wants experimentation and boldness. But if the cost of a failed experiment is much greater than the reward for taking one, caution begins to look less like timidity and more like good judgment. What appears from above as resistance to risk may simply be a rational response to the incentives visible from below.

The same principle applies to almost any number tied to reputation or reward. Once people know how a measure will be interpreted, that interpretation becomes part of the decision. The metric has not become false, but it has stopped being passive.

That is the important shift. The organization thinks it is measuring behavior, while the measurement is also helping produce the behavior it will later observe. Once people can see the score, the score begins shaping the game.

## When Presence Became a Badge Swipe

We saw another version of this after COVID, when, like many companies, we moved heavily toward working from home. For several years, the arrangement seemed to work reasonably well. People recovered hours that had once been spent commuting, had more flexibility around their schedules, and learned how to collaborate without everyone occupying the same physical space. Projects still shipped, meetings multiplied with remarkable enthusiasm, and milestones continued to be hit.

Eventually management began to miss something that was harder to represent on a dashboard. There is an energy that comes from people being physically together: hallway conversations, a quick question across a desk, the accidental exchange of ideas that never earns a calendar invitation. Those things are real, even if they are annoyingly resistant to measurement, so the company introduced a return-to-office policy requiring four days in the office and allowing one day at home.

Then came the practical question: how do you know whether thousands of people are actually coming in? Conveniently, every employee already had to swipe a badge to enter the building. Badge data therefore became the measurable representation of office presence, and from the perspective of a large organization it was probably the most obvious place to start.

Of course, employees could see the measure too.

Some people discovered that they could drive to the office, badge in, get a cup of coffee, and then head home to finish the day there. According to the dashboard, they had been in the office. According to the original purpose of the policy—having people spend meaningful time together—they had perhaps participated mostly in the coffee portion.

Management eventually noticed, which led to the next version of the system. Badge-in was no longer enough; badge-out was added, along with a requirement that employees spend at least six hours in the office. The measure had become more precise because the people being measured had taught management what the first version failed to capture.

I do not know what the next adaptation will be, but I have enough faith in human ingenuity to assume there will eventually be one. Each refinement closes one interpretation of the rule while making the rule itself a little clearer. What began as a fairly human desire to restore colocated interaction had become an increasingly detailed protocol for proving physical presence.

## The Loop Learns Too

The badge story is useful because nobody is standing outside the system. Management is not merely observing employees, and employees are not merely obeying management. Each side watches the other, learns, and adjusts. The feedback loop has become recursive.

Management chooses a proxy for presence. Employees adapt to the proxy. Management observes the adaptation and adds another measure. The new measure becomes part of the next round of decisions, and the cycle continues until everyone has accumulated enough rules that eventually somebody will need a dashboard to explain the dashboard.

This is why greater precision does not necessarily settle the problem. Sometimes it simply moves the boundary. Badge-in becomes badge-in plus badge-out; hours appear; exceptions appear; eventually the definition of “being at work” can become much more exact while the original question—whether meaningful collaboration is actually happening—remains stubbornly human.

The lesson is not that employees are devious or managers foolish. Both sides are responding to what they can observe and control. The difficulty is that once the rule becomes visible, rational people incorporate it into their behavior, and the measurement begins changing the thing it was introduced to represent.

The territory has seen the map.

## What the System Learns

A few principles are worth carrying forward. People notice what is measured, and they notice even more quickly what carries consequences. Once a score becomes visible, it becomes one of the facts people use when deciding what to do next.

Stated values matter, but repeated outcomes tend to matter more. If an organization says *take risks* while consistently rewarding *do not miss*, people will eventually learn which message is safer to believe. If presence is represented by a badge event, some portion of human creativity will inevitably turn toward understanding exactly what counts as an event.

Measurement in human systems is therefore never entirely passive. The metric influences behavior, the changed behavior alters what management sees, and the organization responds again.

At first, people simply learn the score. Eventually, they learn how to play it.
