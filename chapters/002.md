# Number Go Up

## The Joy of a Tight Loop

At one point, I was working on code that translated our GPU hardware specification into source code. We had a custom tool that exposed the specification through a query interface, which was useful in theory and painfully slow in practice. A single query could take ten minutes to return.

That kind of delay changes the way you work. Software development is often a rhythm of making a small change, running the code, seeing what happened, and adjusting from there. When every experiment costs ten minutes, that rhythm falls apart. You hesitate before trying things, batch changes together, and spend more time waiting than learning.

Eventually I got tired of it and added a cache. The script would make the expensive query once, save the result to temporary files, and then load those files on subsequent runs. What had taken ten minutes now took roughly two seconds.

The difference was dramatic. I could make a small change, run the script, inspect the result, and try again almost immediately. Instead of thinking several steps ahead and hoping I had anticipated everything correctly, I could let the system answer me as I worked.

Code a little. Test a little. Learn a little. Improve a little.

The program had not become smarter. The feedback had simply become faster. But that one change transformed the entire experience of working on it.

## Feedback Wants Cadence

That experience taught me that feedback is not only valuable because it exists. Its timing matters. A result that arrives immediately can shape the next decision, while the same result arriving ten minutes later may interrupt the very process it was supposed to guide.

A tight feedback loop keeps cause and effect close together. Change something, observe what happened, and adjust while the original decision is still fresh in your mind. The shorter that loop becomes, the easier it is to experiment, and the more experiments you can fit into the same amount of time.

That is one reason improvement can feel so satisfying. The system responds. You make a change and reality answers back, sometimes confirming your intuition and sometimes correcting it. Either way, you learn.

Over time, those small cycles accumulate. What begins as deliberate trial and error gradually becomes familiarity, then competence, and sometimes something that looks almost like instinct. The number, the test result, or the visible outcome is not the mastery itself. It is the signal that helps mastery form.

## When Repetition Becomes Intuition

On another team, I worked on a testing tool for memory controllers. We had test cards that could be programmed with random read and write vectors, then set loose against the hardware while we tracked the final memory values. It was a good way to expose functional bugs, race conditions, and the kinds of subtle failures that only appeared under the right timing.

We also had a principal engineer who seemed to operate at a different level from the rest of us. Someone could bring him a log file that looked like a wall of noise, and within moments he would point to a few lines and say what was probably wrong. To me, it looked almost like magic.

At first, I approached those failures mechanically. I would trace signals, compare expected and actual values, and work through the protocol step by step. The process was slow because every clue had to be interpreted consciously.

But after enough exposure, things started to change. Certain failure patterns began to look familiar. A particular sequence in the log would remind me of a bug I had seen months earlier, or a timing relationship would suggest a likely class of problem before I had fully worked through it.

I never reached the principal engineer’s level, and I left that team after about five years. But I was much farther along than when I started. What had once looked like an indecipherable stream of events was beginning to resolve into patterns.

That was one of the first times I really understood that expertise is not just knowing more facts. It is seeing structure where a beginner still sees noise.

## When Feedback Becomes Instinct

We sometimes talk about intuition as though it stands in opposition to analysis, but much of what we call intuition is analysis that has been repeated often enough to become compressed. The principal engineer was not guessing. He had seen enough failures, protocols, timing relationships, and strange corner cases that certain patterns had become familiar before he consciously worked through every step.

Repeated feedback builds that familiarity. Each bug teaches something about the system. Each wrong hypothesis narrows the possibilities. Each successful diagnosis adds another pattern to the mental library.

Over time, the loop speeds up. What once required careful tracing can eventually become recognition. The expert still may need to verify the answer, but the path toward it becomes shorter because experience has already done much of the sorting.

This is another reason feedback is so powerful. It does more than tell us whether we were right or wrong in the moment. Given enough repetition, it changes what we are capable of seeing.

## When the System Starts Watching Back

So far, the feedback loops had been mostly benign. The query tool did not care that I was measuring how long it took. The memory controller did not change its behavior because I was studying the logs. In both cases, measurement helped me understand the system more clearly.

Organizations are different because the system contains people.

Once a number is attached to performance, schedules, goals, reviews, or rewards, the people being measured can see it too. They learn what gets noticed, what gets praised, and what creates uncomfortable conversations. The metric stops being something outside the system and becomes one of the forces acting inside it.

That does not require anyone to be cynical or dishonest. Most people simply respond to the environment they are given. If one behavior is rewarded and another creates risk, the organization teaches people which behavior is safer.

At that point, feedback is no longer only helping us learn about the system. It is also teaching the system how to behave.

## The Score Behind the Score

For years, we worked with quarterly goals called MBOs—Management by Objectives. We were encouraged to be aggressive, pull schedules in, and commit to ambitious milestones. The language was about stretching, moving faster, and taking on more risk.

At the same time, one phrase came up again and again in our business update meetings: *flawless execution*. Missing a milestone meant explaining what happened, why it happened, and what would be done differently next time. Those misses could also follow you into the yearly focal review.

It did not take long to understand which message carried more weight.

If you committed to an aggressive schedule and missed it, there was pain attached to the miss. If you padded the schedule, hit the milestone, or delivered early, you were much more likely to be praised for meeting your objectives. One value said, *take risks*. The system said, *do not miss*.

People adapted.

That does not mean everyone became dishonest or stopped caring about ambitious work. It means we learned to distinguish between the value that was spoken and the value that was enforced. When the consequences consistently favor one behavior, that behavior becomes the safer strategy no matter what the slide deck says.

Organizations often reveal their real priorities this way. The stated objective matters, but the reward structure matters more. People learn the score by watching what happens when someone wins, misses, stretches, or fails.

The metric system does not merely record culture. It helps create it.

## Visible Activity, Invisible Quality

I once worked with an engineer in system validation who could write code much faster than the rest of us. He was constantly delivering ahead of schedule and regularly received service awards for getting things done quickly. From a distance, the numbers looked great.

The problem was that speed sometimes came at the expense of care. His code accumulated bugs faster than anyone else’s, often because testing had been rushed or corners had been cut. Then, because those bugs were his to fix, he also became one of the most prolific bug fixers on the team.

That created an odd measurement problem. His commit rate was high, which looked productive. His bug-fix rate was also high, which looked responsive. Both numbers could be interpreted as evidence of strong performance.

What we did not have was a metric that connected the two.

No dashboard showed that some of the visible bug-fixing activity was downstream of the same speed that had produced the defects in the first place. Meanwhile, engineers who moved more carefully and prevented those bugs from existing generated less activity for anyone to count.

That is one of the hardest things about quality: absence does not make much noise. A bug that never gets written produces no ticket, no fix, no heroic save, and no line on a status report. Prevention is often invisible precisely because it worked.

Visible activity is easy to measure. Invisible quality is much harder.

## What the Dashboard Cannot See

Metrics are especially good at counting things that happen. Commits, bugs closed, tickets resolved, milestones reached, features shipped—activity leaves evidence behind. Prevention usually does not.

That creates a bias toward the visible. The engineer who fixes ten bugs generates ten countable events. The engineer who designs the system carefully enough that those bugs never appear may generate nothing comparable. One produces a trail of measurable activity; the other produces an absence.

Organizations can easily mistake that asymmetry for a difference in value. We reward what we can see because what we can see is easier to compare, summarize, and put on a slide. The danger is not that the visible work is unimportant, but that visibility starts standing in for contribution.

This is where metrics begin to distort judgment. They can tell us what happened without telling us why it happened, what caused it, or what never happened because someone prevented it. A count can be accurate and still conceal the relationship that matters most.

The more we rely on numbers to evaluate people, the more important it becomes to ask what the number leaves out.

## When the System Starts Playing the Metric

There is an important difference between measuring a system and measuring people inside a system. A cache does not care how fast I say it is. A memory controller does not become self-conscious when I inspect its logs. The measurement can change my understanding of the system without changing the system’s motives.

People are different. We can see the dashboard too.

Once we know which numbers matter, we begin adjusting ourselves around them. We learn that an aggressive commitment may be praised in principle while a missed commitment carries consequences. We learn that visible activity is easier to demonstrate than invisible prevention. We notice what earns recognition, what survives a performance review, and what requires an uncomfortable explanation.

None of this requires manipulation or bad intent. It is simply another feedback loop. The organization signals what it rewards, people respond, leadership observes the resulting numbers, and those numbers reinforce the behaviors that produced them.

The metric has now crossed an important boundary. It is no longer merely describing the system from the outside. It has entered the system and become one of the forces shaping what happens inside it.

A map is useful because it simplifies the territory. It tells us what to notice and helps us navigate something too complicated to hold in our heads all at once. But once we begin rebuilding the landscape so that it looks better on the map, something has changed.

The representation has started to become the reality.

And that creates an entirely different kind of problem.
