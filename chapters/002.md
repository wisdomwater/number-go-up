# Chapter 2: When the Measure Becomes the Target

## The Observer Enters the System

There is an idea from physics that has escaped the laboratory and become part of popular culture: the observer effect.

It is often explained badly, especially when quantum mechanics gets involved. You'll sometimes hear that particles somehow know we're watching them, as though electrons are shy little creatures that behave differently when scientists enter the room.

That's not really what's happening.

The important idea is much simpler: **you cannot always measure something without interacting with it.**

To measure a system, you have to get information out of it somehow. At very small scales, that interaction can itself affect the thing being measured. The measuring instrument is not necessarily standing outside reality, silently taking notes. It has entered the system.

Human systems have their own version of this problem.

Suppose I want to know how much time engineers spend in meetings.

At first this sounds like a straightforward measurement problem. Track meetings. Count hours. Add them up. Produce a graph.

But how am I going to collect the data?

Maybe I ask every engineer to categorize their calendar. Now hundreds of engineers have to spend time maintaining accurate calendars.

Maybe I create a weekly survey. Now they have to fill out the survey.

Maybe I build software that automatically analyzes calendars. Now someone has to design, build, validate, maintain, and explain the system. Someone else will probably have to create a dashboard.

The dashboard is inevitable.

None of these costs necessarily make the measurement a bad idea. The information might be valuable enough to justify them.

But measurement is not free.

This is easy to forget because the finished number looks effortless. A chart appears at an executive meeting showing that engineers spend 31 percent of their time in meetings. What the chart doesn't show is how many hours of engineering work were consumed producing the number telling us how many hours of engineering work were being consumed.

There is a certain elegance to that.

Every measurement has a cost.

Sometimes the cost is trivial. A computer records a timestamp automatically and nobody needs to think about it.

Sometimes the cost is enormous. Organizations can build entire reporting structures around collecting, cleaning, validating, summarizing, presenting, and explaining metrics. People create reports for people who create reports for people who present reports to people who request additional reports.

Eventually we may need a metric to determine whether all the metrics are helping.

But the direct cost of measurement is only the beginning.

The more interesting cost appears when people become aware that the measurement exists.

Imagine management announces that it wants to understand how much time engineers are spending in meetings. Engineers are told not to worry. Nobody is being evaluated. Leadership is simply trying to understand where time goes.

Fair enough.

But now I know someone is looking.

I receive a meeting invitation and think about the metric.

Does this meeting count?

Should I decline it?

Should I mark it differently on my calendar?

Does a design review count as a meeting or engineering work?

What about a one-on-one with my manager?

What about the thirty-minute conversation I had with another engineer that solved a problem we'd been stuck on for two days?

Before the measurement existed, these were simply activities. Now they are categories.

And categories invite interpretation.

This is where measuring a human system becomes fundamentally different from measuring a board.

The board does not care. Put a ruler beside it and nothing happens. The board doesn't notice that it is forty-seven inches long and begin wondering whether management prefers forty-eight.

Human beings notice.

We notice what is being counted. We notice who is asking. We notice how often they ask. We notice which numbers appear on dashboards and which numbers disappear into databases never to be mentioned again.

And then we begin trying to understand what the measurement means.

Why are they tracking this?

Is thirty-one percent too high?

What number do they want?

Is this going to affect staffing?

Is this going to affect performance reviews?

Should I be doing something differently?

These questions can arise before anyone attaches a formal consequence to the metric. Nobody needs to announce a target. Nobody needs to threaten anyone's job.

The act of paying attention already communicates something.

**This matters.**

That's an effect on the system.

Measurement doesn't merely extract information. In a human system, it can introduce information as well.

It tells the people being measured something about the people doing the measuring.

What they're curious about.

What they're worried about.

What they're trying to improve.

What they might eventually reward.

What they might eventually punish.

This doesn't mean people always react badly to being measured. Sometimes measurement improves behavior in exactly the way we hope.

If I start tracking how many steps I take each day, I may walk more.

If a hospital tracks hand-washing compliance, staff may wash their hands more consistently.

If a software team begins measuring build times, engineers may notice inefficiencies they had previously ignored and make the system faster.

Measurement can focus attention. That's one of its strengths.

But we should recognize what just happened. We didn't merely observe the system.

We changed it.

The hospital isn't simply discovering how often people wash their hands. The act of measuring hand washing may be increasing hand washing.

My fitness tracker isn't simply observing how much I walk. Knowing that I'm at 8,732 steps may cause me to take a completely unnecessary lap around the kitchen before bed because apparently I am now the kind of person who cannot sleep with an unfinished circle on his watch.

The measurement has become part of the causal system.

This is why metrics involving human behavior require a different kind of thinking.

We have to ask not only:

**What will this measurement tell us?**

We also have to ask:

**What will measuring this cause?**

Those are separate questions.

A metric can provide useful information while simultaneously changing behavior. It can improve one behavior while unintentionally discouraging another. It can direct attention toward something important while pulling attention away from something equally important.

And once consequences are attached, these effects become much stronger.

We'll get there soon.

For now, it's enough to recognize that the observer has entered the system.

The moment people know which number you're watching, the measurement is no longer merely describing their world.

It has become part of their world.

## Oh, They’re Watching This

There is a moment that happens whenever a new metric appears at work.

You hear that management has started tracking something.

Maybe it's bugs closed.

Maybe it's meeting hours.

Maybe it's customer response time.

Maybe it's how often employees use AI tools.

And somewhere in your brain, a tiny light turns on.

**Oh. They're watching this.**

Nobody has to tell you what to do next.

You start paying attention too.

This is one of the simplest ways measurement changes human behavior. What gets measured gets attention, and attention is limited.

Imagine an organization introduces a dashboard tracking AI adoption. Leadership explains that the goal is simply to understand how employees are using new tools. Nobody is required to hit a quota. There are no performance consequences. It's just information.

Then the dashboard appears in the monthly meeting.

AI adoption is 42 percent.

Someone asks why it isn't higher.

The following month, it's 47 percent.

Better.

A few teams are highlighted for particularly strong adoption. Their managers explain some of the things they've been doing. Leadership is pleased.

Still no quota.

Still no performance consequence.

But does anyone really believe the measurement is neutral anymore?

The organization has communicated something very clearly without putting it in a policy document:

**We care about this.**

That message changes how people allocate attention.

An engineer who might previously have asked, “Would AI help me solve this problem?” may begin asking a slightly different question:

“Where can I use AI?”

Those questions sound similar, but they aren't.

The first begins with the problem.

The second begins with the metric.

Maybe the answer is the same. Perhaps AI genuinely is the best tool for the job. But the center of gravity has shifted.

This happens everywhere.

A teacher learns that standardized test scores are receiving increased attention. She thinks about test scores more.

A doctor learns that patient appointment times are being tracked. He thinks about appointment times more.

A customer service representative learns that average call duration is on the dashboard. She notices how long calls are taking.

A software engineer learns that bug closure rates are being reviewed. He becomes much more aware of how many bugs he closes.

None of these people are cheating.

They're paying attention.

And attention is one of our scarcest resources.

Every hour spent improving one thing is an hour not spent improving something else. Every concern that moves toward the front of our minds pushes something else slightly toward the back.

This means measurement creates priorities even when nobody explicitly announces them.

Suppose a manager tells her team:

“Quality is our highest priority.”

Then every Monday she reviews delivery speed.

On Tuesday she asks why the team missed its schedule.

On Wednesday she sends an email comparing delivery rates across teams.

On Friday she congratulates the group that shipped the most features.

Once a quarter, she reminds everyone that quality is the highest priority.

What does the team believe?

People are remarkably good at distinguishing stated values from operational values.

We learn what actually matters by watching where attention goes.

This is why the absence of a formal target doesn't make a metric harmless or ineffective. Repeated attention is itself a signal.

People notice what gets discussed.

They notice what gets graphed.

They notice which numbers require explanations when they go down.

They notice which numbers cause leaders to smile when they go up.

Eventually, they begin anticipating the questions.

This is especially powerful when the people doing the measuring have authority over the people being measured.

A senior executive may look at a dashboard and think:

*Interesting. AI usage increased six percent.*

An employee several levels below may look at the same dashboard and think:

*I should probably be using more AI.*

Same number.

Different experience.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences expectation.

That expectation may never have been intended. Leadership may sincerely want people to use judgment. They may genuinely mean it when they say, “Use AI where it makes sense.”

But humans don't interpret communication only through words.

We interpret emphasis.

If you tell me to use my judgment but repeatedly ask me about one particular number, I will eventually incorporate that number into my judgment.

That's not irrational.

I'm learning the environment.

And sometimes that's exactly what we want measurement to do.

If a hospital starts prominently tracking preventable infections, I hope people pay more attention to preventable infections. If a factory starts tracking serious safety incidents, increased attention to safety may be enormously beneficial.

Measurement can focus a system on something it has neglected.

That's power.

But power needs to be handled deliberately.

Because every time we turn the organizational spotlight toward one thing, other things remain outside the beam.

The question isn't simply whether the thing we're measuring matters.

The question is what happens when everyone realizes that it matters **to the people watching the numbers**.

At first, perhaps they simply pay more attention.

Then someone sets a target.

And things get considerably more interesting.

## And Then We Attach Consequences

Attention changes behavior.

Consequences change it faster.

Sooner or later, someone looks at the metric we've been carefully observing and asks the obvious question:

**What should the number be?**

AI adoption is 47 percent. Should it be 60?

Customer satisfaction is 8.2. Could we get it to 8.5?

The average response time is eleven minutes. Could we get it below ten?

Engineering productivity increased 7 percent last year. What should the target be this year?

This isn't unreasonable. Organizations measure things because they want to understand them, but understanding is rarely the final goal. Usually we want to make something better.

So we establish a target.

And once we establish a target, we begin attaching consequences to whether people reach it.

Sometimes the consequences are obvious.

Hit your sales quota and you get a bonus.

Miss your performance target and you don't.

Score high enough on the test and you get into the program.

Hit the quarterly numbers and your division receives additional funding.

These are explicit incentives. Everyone understands the arrangement. But many consequences are much softer.

Praise is a consequence.

Recognition is a consequence.

Being described as a high performer is a consequence.

Having your project highlighted at an executive meeting is a consequence.

So is having to explain why your number is red.

Anyone who has spent enough time in a large organization understands the motivational power of not wanting to explain a red box on a PowerPoint slide.

Sometimes there isn't even a formal reward or punishment. You simply learn which numbers make your manager happy and which ones cause additional meetings to appear on your calendar.

That's enough.

Humans are social creatures. We care how we're perceived. We care whether the people with authority over us think we're doing a good job. We care whether we're seen as cooperative, competent, innovative, productive, or difficult.

And, for most of us, employment has the additional inconvenience of being connected to food and housing.

So when an organization attaches consequences to a number, employees pay attention.

Again, this is not necessarily bad.

If a salesperson receives a commission for making sales, we expect that person to try harder to make sales. That's the point.

If a hospital rewards departments for reducing preventable infections, we hope people will focus more carefully on practices that prevent infections.

If an engineering team establishes a target for reducing build times, engineers may prioritize improvements they've been putting off for years.

Incentives work.

The problem is that the incentive attaches to the thing we can measure, while the thing we actually care about is often larger.

Suppose I manage a customer support organization.

What do I want?

I want customers to receive excellent help.

That's difficult to measure directly.

So perhaps I measure average call duration. Long calls are expensive. Customers probably don't enjoy sitting on the phone for forty-five minutes. Shorter calls might indicate that representatives are resolving problems efficiently.

That's a reasonable proxy.

Then I set a target.

Calls should average less than eight minutes.

Then I attach consequences.

Teams that hit the target receive recognition. Managers whose teams consistently miss it are asked to explain why. Individual representatives can see their numbers. Performance reviews may include them.

What problem is the customer service representative solving now?

Originally, the problem was:

**Help the customer.**

Now there is another problem:

**Keep the call under eight minutes.**

Usually those goals align.

A skilled representative may solve the customer's problem quickly and efficiently. Customer happy. Metric happy. Manager happy. Everybody goes home.

But eventually a customer calls with a problem that requires twenty minutes.

Now the goals conflict.

Helping the customer well makes the metric worse.

Improving the metric may require helping the customer less well.

This is the moment where the proxy begins exerting power over the thing it was supposed to represent.

From the manager's perspective, average call duration may still be one data point among many.

From the representative's perspective, that number may feel very different.

The manager thinks:

**I'm gathering information about efficiency.**

The employee thinks:

**I'm being judged on whether this call ends before eight minutes.**

Both can be telling the truth.

This difference in perspective matters enormously.

People higher in an organization often have more freedom to interpret metrics contextually. They can look at several dashboards, listen to explanations, consider broader business conditions, and exercise judgment.

The person being measured may not know how much context will ultimately matter.

They know the number is recorded.

They know their manager can see it.

They know someone will notice if it gets too far from the target. That uncertainty changes the safest strategy.

If I believe good judgment will be rewarded even when the metric suffers, I can exercise good judgment.

If I'm not sure, the rational choice may be to protect the metric.

This is why a phrase like “It's only one data point” can sound very different depending on which side of the dashboard you're standing on.

For the measurer, perhaps it really is one data point.

For the measuree, it may be the data point with their name next to it.

This becomes especially important when power is unequal. An executive may genuinely intend a metric to be directional rather than prescriptive. But intentions weaken as they travel through an organization.

The executive asks a vice president why the number is low.

The vice president asks a director.

The director asks a manager.

The manager asks the team.

By the time the question reaches the person doing the work, “We're curious about this trend” can acquire a surprisingly different emotional texture.

**Make the number go up.**

Nobody necessarily said those words.

Nobody had to.

This is how metrics acquire power.

First we measure something because we want to understand the system.

Then we pay attention to it.

Then we establish a target.

Then we attach consequences.

And eventually the people inside the system begin organizing some of their behavior around producing the desired number.

At that point, something fundamental has changed. The metric is no longer merely measuring behavior.

It is causing behavior.

And now we have to ask a question that sits at the center of nearly every dysfunctional metric system:

**What happens when people become really good at giving us the number we asked for?**

## Goodhart’s Law

There is a wonderfully concise description of what happens next.

It is called **Goodhart’s Law**.

The idea is usually expressed like this:

**When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**

The wording is almost suspiciously perfect for this book.

The idea comes from British economist Charles Goodhart, who was thinking about monetary policy rather than software engineers, customer service representatives, or fitness trackers. His original observation was more technical, but the version above captures something that shows up almost everywhere human beings are measured.

The strange thing about Goodhart’s Law is that the metric doesn't have to be bad.

In fact, the problem is more interesting when the metric starts out good.

Imagine that we discover a strong relationship between customer satisfaction and call duration. Customers whose problems are resolved efficiently tend to be happier. Calls that drag on for forty minutes often indicate confusion, poor training, or inefficient systems.

So we start measuring call duration.

At first, the number gives us useful information.

Then we establish a target.

Keep calls under eight minutes.

Now the employees answering the phones know exactly what the organization wants.

And because human beings are remarkably creative creatures, they begin finding ways to produce it.

Some become genuinely more efficient.

Good.

Some learn which problems can be handled faster.

Also good.

Some rush customers through explanations.

Less good.

Some transfer difficult calls to another department.

Definitely less good.

Some discover that certain kinds of calls are particularly dangerous to their averages and begin finding creative ways to get those calls off their screens.

The number improves.

Management looks at the dashboard.

Average call duration: down 14 percent.

Success.

Maybe.

This is the central problem Goodhart’s Law describes.

Before the target existed, call duration was useful partly because nobody was trying very hard to manipulate call duration. The number emerged naturally from the work.

Once people were rewarded for producing a particular number, the number became something they could optimize independently of the underlying goal.

The relationship changed.

Originally:

**Better customer service → shorter calls.**

But once shorter calls become the target, another path becomes available:

**Shorter calls → shorter calls.**

We can improve the number without improving the thing the number was supposed to represent.

That sounds almost comically obvious when written down.

Of course people will do this. And yet organizations repeatedly behave as though they won't.

We create a proxy because it correlates with something valuable. Then we attach rewards to the proxy and assume the correlation will remain unchanged. But the incentive itself changes the relationship.

Consider education.

Suppose students who understand mathematics tend to score well on a standardized mathematics test.

That makes the test useful.

We administer it across thousands of students and discover patterns. Some schools perform better than others. Some students are struggling. Certain concepts seem poorly understood.

The test is giving us information about learning.

Then test scores become targets.

Schools are ranked by them.

Funding may depend on them.

Teachers may be evaluated using them.

Parents use them to compare schools.

Suddenly everyone has a powerful reason to increase test scores.

One way to do that is to improve mathematical understanding.

Excellent.

But that's not the only way.

Schools can spend more time teaching the specific material likely to appear on the test. Teachers can devote class time to test-taking strategies. Subjects that aren't heavily tested can receive less attention. Students can become very good at producing the kind of answers the assessment rewards.

Again, nobody necessarily has to cheat.

The system can drift toward the metric entirely through people behaving rationally.

Test scores rise.

Did learning improve?

Possibly.

That's the frustrating answer.

The number may still contain useful information. Goodhart’s Law doesn't mean that the moment a target is established the metric becomes worthless.

It means we can no longer interpret the number as though the target didn't exist.

The measurement now contains the effects of the incentive.

This is where our observer effect returns.

Before the metric mattered, we were trying to observe the system. Then we made the observation important. Now the system is responding to our observation.

The people inside it are learning. They're adapting. They're finding shortcuts. They're discovering what counts.

They're figuring out which activities improve the score and which activities consume time without appearing on the dashboard.

And some of those adaptations may be exactly what we wanted.

That's worth emphasizing.

The goal of an incentive is to change behavior.

If we measure workplace injuries and reward safer practices, we hope behavior changes. If we establish fuel-efficiency standards, we want engineers designing more efficient vehicles. If I set myself a goal of walking ten thousand steps, I am deliberately using a number to influence my own behavior.

Behavior changing in response to measurement is not a defect.

The difficult question is whether the behavior moves toward the **point** or merely toward the **number**.

That's the distinction.

When the two remain aligned, the metric can be enormously useful.

Number gets better.

Reality gets better.

Wonderful.

But whenever the number and the point can be separated, the incentive creates pressure to separate them.

And the stronger the consequences attached to the number, the stronger that pressure becomes.

This gives us a useful way to think about any target.

Imagine that the people being measured are intelligent, creative, and highly motivated to hit the number.

Because they probably are.

Then ask:

**How could they make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing I care about?**

That question may feel cynical.

It isn't.

It is respectful of human adaptability.

If your metric determines bonuses, promotions, funding, reputation, grades, rankings, or job security, you should assume people will become extraordinarily interested in understanding how it works.

They will learn the rules.

And then they will play the game.

The danger isn't that people might get good at hitting our targets. The danger is that they might get very good at hitting exactly the targets we gave them.

## Congratulations, You Got What You Asked For

I've watched Goodhart's Law play out in software engineering.

Years ago, I worked with an engineer who could produce code incredibly quickly.

And management loved it.

That isn't hard to understand. Software organizations exist, among other things, to produce software. When an engineer can take a requirement and turn it into working code quickly, that looks a lot like productivity.

And sometimes it is.

The problem was that the code wasn't always particularly careful.

Bugs followed.

Sometimes a lot of them.

Now we had a new problem. Bugs are bad. Customers don't like them. Test teams don't like them. Managers definitely don't like them, especially when the bugs are blocking a release and suddenly begin appearing on PowerPoint slides.

Fortunately, we had someone who was very good at fixing bugs.

The same engineer.

He could fix them incredibly quickly. And management loved that too.

From the perspective of visible activity, this engineer was a machine.

Features delivered.

Code written.

Bugs fixed.

Problems solved.

There was always something happening.

Meanwhile, imagine another engineer working more slowly.

She spends more time thinking about the design before she writes the code. She asks annoying questions.

What happens if this input is invalid?

What happens when two things occur at the same time?

What happens if this dependency fails?

Are we sure this belongs here?

Could we simplify this before we build it?

Her first version takes longer.

But it has fewer bugs.

Maybe considerably fewer.

Which engineer looks more productive?

That depends entirely on what you're measuring.

If we're counting code written, features completed, bugs closed, or visible acts of problem solving, the first engineer may look fantastic.

The second engineer has a visibility problem.

There is no bug ticket for the bug she never created.

There is no heroic debugging session for the outage that never happened.

There is no urgent email thanking her for solving the crisis she quietly prevented three months earlier.

Prevention has terrible marketing.

A firefighter running into a burning building is visibly heroic.

The person who checked the wiring six months earlier and prevented the fire gets to enjoy the considerably less exciting reward of nothing happening.

Software engineering has a lot of work like that.

Good architecture can look like nothing happening.

Careful design can look like nothing happening.

Mentoring another engineer before a mistake gets committed can look like nothing happening.

Deleting unnecessary code can actually make certain productivity metrics go backward.

The better engineer can sometimes leave behind less visible evidence of having done anything at all. This creates a strange asymmetry.

Problems generate artifacts.

They generate bug reports, tickets, meetings, emails, patches, commits, status updates, investigations, root-cause analyses, and eventually satisfying little checkmarks showing that the work has been completed.

Prevented problems generate silence.

And silence is difficult to put on a dashboard.

So the engineer I worked with found himself in an almost perfect incentive loop.

Write code quickly.

Receive recognition for producing code quickly.

Introduce bugs.

Fix bugs quickly.

Receive recognition for fixing bugs quickly.

Return to writing code quickly.

The machine had achieved perpetual motion. We were rewarding both the creation of the problem and the solution to it.

To be clear, I don't think my coworker sat down one morning and devised this strategy. I don't imagine him rubbing his hands together and thinking, *Today I shall introduce seventeen bugs and become Employee of the Month.*

That would actually make the story less interesting.

He was doing what the system encouraged him to do.

Move fast.

Produce visible output.

Respond urgently when something breaks.

Solve problems.

Management wasn't trying to reward buggy software either.

They wanted good software delivered quickly. Of course they did.

But the behaviors easiest to observe were speed, output, and responsiveness. Carefulness was harder to see. Prevention was harder to see.

The value of a design decision whose payoff might not become apparent for two years was much harder to see.

So visible activity accumulated evidence.

Quiet effectiveness often didn't.

This is how an organization can end up rewarding **thrash in the name of urgency**.

Everyone is busy.

Tickets are moving.

Code is being committed.

Bugs are being closed.

People are jumping onto emergency calls.

Managers are sending thank-you emails.

Dashboards glow with activity.

The organization is vibrating with productivity.

And somewhere underneath all that motion is a question nobody particularly wants to ask:

**Would we need quite so much heroic work if we'd done the work differently in the first place?**

This isn't an argument for moving slowly.

Speed matters.

Software companies that spend twelve years thoughtfully considering every design decision tend to encounter another important business metric called revenue.

Nor is it an argument that bugs mean someone did bad work. Software is complicated. Good engineers introduce bugs. Excellent engineers introduce bugs. Sometimes the only way to discover a problem is to build something, run it, and see what breaks.

The issue is what the system consistently rewards.

If we reward visible activity without accounting for the conditions that produced the activity, we can accidentally make rework look like productivity. And once that happens, the number can become deeply misleading.

Imagine two teams.

Team A completes one hundred work items and later completes forty additional work items fixing problems introduced by the first hundred.

Team B completes eighty work items and needs ten fixes.

Which team accomplished more?

If the dashboard counts completed work, Team A wins 140 to 90.

Congratulations.

Number go up.

But if the point was to deliver reliable software that solves customer problems, we may have just congratulated the wrong team.

This is why the question underneath Goodhart's Law matters so much.

People do not have to cheat a metric for the metric to distort behavior. They simply have to live inside the incentive system we created.

We decide what to count.

We decide what to praise.

We decide which accomplishments become visible.

We decide which numbers appear in performance reviews and which forms of contribution remain mostly invisible.

Then people adapt.

And when they give us exactly what our system encouraged them to give us, we shouldn't be surprised.

Congratulations.

You got what you asked for.

## Gaming the System Isn’t Always Cheating

When a metric starts producing strange behavior, there is a convenient explanation.

People are gaming the system.

Sometimes they are.

People lie on reports. They manipulate data. They hide bad results. They discover loopholes and exploit them in ways that everyone involved understands are dishonest.

Human beings contain multitudes.

But I think we reach for the language of gaming too quickly.

Suppose a company tells its salespeople that their bonuses depend on quarterly sales.

What do we expect them to do?

Sell things.

They might call more customers. They might follow up more aggressively. They might offer discounts to close deals before the quarter ends. They might prioritize customers who seem ready to buy and spend less time developing relationships that could produce larger sales six months from now.

Is that gaming the system?

Maybe.

But it's also responding to the incentive.

The company said quarterly sales matter. The employees believed them.

This distinction becomes particularly important when the person being measured has less power than the person doing the measuring.

Imagine an employee is told that a particular metric is only one factor in their performance evaluation.

Their manager says not to overthink it.

“Use your judgment.”

This sounds reasonable.

But the employee also knows the metric appears on a dashboard.

They know their number is compared with their coworkers' numbers.

They know the manager asks about it when it falls.

They know people with high numbers receive recognition.

They know layoffs happened two years ago.

What exactly does “use your judgment” mean under those conditions?

It may mean:

**Use your judgment, but understand that we are recording this.**

People are very good at reading systems. We listen to what leaders say, but we also watch what leaders do. We notice who gets promoted. We notice who gets praised.

We notice which projects receive funding. We notice what gets discussed in performance reviews. We notice which mistakes are forgiven and which ones follow someone around for years.

Then we adjust.

This isn't necessarily cynical. It's adaptive.

Imagine a teacher whose school is evaluated heavily on standardized test performance.

She loves teaching.

She wants her students to understand mathematics deeply. She wants them to become curious, confident thinkers who can reason through unfamiliar problems.

She also knows her school's scores matter.

Her principal knows.

The district knows.

Parents know.

Maybe funding depends on them. Maybe her own evaluation includes them. So as the test approaches, she spends more time preparing students for the test.

She teaches them strategies for recognizing common question formats. She reviews material likely to appear. She spends less time on an interesting project because it won't help much with the assessment.

Has she betrayed education?

Or has she correctly understood the environment in which she works?

That's harder.

The same thing happens inside companies.

If I know that completed tickets matter, I may prefer smaller tasks that I can finish quickly.

If I know that bugs closed matter, I may grab easier bugs.

If I know that utilization matters, I may make sure every hour is assigned somewhere.

If I know that AI adoption matters, I may find reasons to use AI in places where I otherwise wouldn't bother.

If I know that response time matters, I may respond quickly even when waiting would allow me to give a better answer.

At some point, someone looking at the system from above may become frustrated.

“People are optimizing for the metric!”

Yes.

We taught them to.

This is one of the stranger habits of organizations. We create an incentive, communicate the incentive repeatedly, attach consequences to it, and then become suspicious when people respond to the incentive.

We created the game.

We published the rules.

We put the score on a dashboard.

Then we acted surprised when people started playing.

The phrase **gaming the system** can obscure this because it places the moral responsibility entirely on the player.

Sometimes that responsibility belongs there. If someone falsifies a report, that's dishonesty. If a salesperson creates fake accounts to hit a target, that's fraud. If a manager deliberately hides defects to protect a quality score, we don't need an elaborate theory of incentives to explain what's wrong.

But there is a large territory between fraud and completely unaffected behavior.

That's where most people live.

They prioritize.

They interpret.

They anticipate.

They choose one legitimate task over another legitimate task.

They spend more time on work that will be recognized and less time on work that won't.

They learn which kinds of accomplishments travel well through the organization.

And they become skilled at producing evidence that they are doing a good job.

That last part matters.

In complex work, **doing good work and producing evidence of good work are not always the same activity.**

Sometimes the best work is highly visible.

A major feature ships. A difficult bug gets fixed. A large customer signs a contract.

Everyone can see it.

Other contributions are much harder to demonstrate.

A conversation prevents a bad architectural decision.

A senior employee spends an afternoon helping a junior employee understand something.

Someone notices a project is headed in the wrong direction and convinces the team not to build it.

A manager quietly resolves tension between two people before it becomes a larger conflict.

Someone asks the question that prevents six months of unnecessary work.

What ticket do we close for that?

How many points is it worth?

People eventually learn the answer.

Usually, not many.

So the incentive system doesn't have to tell people to stop doing invisible work. It merely has to make visible work more valuable.

Over time, rational choices accumulate. And eventually the organization may discover that some of the things it cares about most are happening less often.

Not because people stopped caring.

Not because everyone became selfish.

Not because employees discovered some clever way to cheat.

But because every system teaches people how to survive inside it.

If we don't like what people learn, blaming the students is probably not enough. We should take another look at the curriculum.
