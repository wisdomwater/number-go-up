# Conclusion — Enough

Eventually, I am going to finish this book.

That is a strange thing to write after spending eleven chapters thinking about our relationship with measurement, because finishing a book produces an impressive collection of new things to measure. There will be copies sold, reviews received, ratings averaged, pages read, audiobook minutes streamed, and probably several statistics I do not yet know exist. Somewhere, a dashboard is waiting for me, and I already know what I am going to want it to say.

Number go up.

I would like to imagine that writing this book has made me spiritually mature enough not to care how many people read it, but that seems unlikely. I have spent too many years building things and hoping they succeed to become suddenly indifferent when one of them happens to have my name on the cover. If a thousand people read the book, I will be delighted, and after some appropriate period of gratitude, ten thousand will begin to seem even better. Should ten thousand people read it, I have every confidence that my imagination will prove capable of producing a larger number.

There is nothing particularly wrong with that. I wrote the book because I wanted people to read it, and selling more copies would mean, among other things, that more people had the opportunity to encounter these ideas. The sales number would also tell me something about whether the book found an audience, just as the measurements throughout this book have told us real things about software, organizations, health, money, and ourselves. The trouble would begin only if I quietly asked that number to tell me whether writing the book had been worthwhile. I have spent enough time with numbers by now to know how that conversation goes.

### The Number Has No Enough

Numbers are very good at telling us whether there is more or less. They can tell us whether we are approaching a target, moving away from it, or remaining stubbornly flat despite what we considered an excellent week. With enough context, they can help us understand whether something is working and whether a change we made had the effect we hoped it would have. What they cannot do on their own is tell us when we have enough.

Suppose this book sells ten thousand copies. Whether that is enough depends entirely on what I hoped the book would do. Enough to earn back its production costs is one question, enough to reach the people I imagined while writing it is another, and enough to make me permanently secure in my identity as an author may turn out not to be a publishing question at all.

This is why *enough* is such an interesting word. It sounds quantitative, but hidden inside it is a question of purpose. A measuring cup can tell me when I have enough flour because the recipe has already established what the flour is for. Without some idea of what we are trying to make, more and less are simply directions.

The same is true of the larger numbers in our lives. How much money is enough depends partly on what we hope the money will make possible. How much professional success is enough depends on what we hoped the career would give us or allow us to give to others. Measurement can help enormously once those questions have been answered, but it cannot answer them for us. Enough has to come from somewhere else.

### What Were the Numbers For?

Looking back over the territory of this book, I don't find myself wanting to apologize for measurement. Quite the opposite. Measurement has given us extraordinary ways of understanding the world, correcting ourselves, coordinating our efforts, and doing things that intuition alone could never accomplish.

The same is true in an ordinary life. We track money because security matters, monitor our health because bodies matter, evaluate work because we want what we do to be useful, and plan for the future because tomorrow will eventually become today. Even ambition, which can look slightly embarrassing after several chapters of philosophical reflection, often begins with something quite reasonable: we want to become good at something and make a contribution that matters.

Those desires helped us build real things. A career can represent decades of learning how to solve problems other people trust us to solve. Savings can represent thousands of ordinary decisions to leave something for the future rather than spend everything in the present. A home, a family, a community, or a body of work may contain years of choices whose significance was difficult to see while we were making them.

Of course, not everything worked. We made mistakes, pursued things that mattered less than we thought they did, neglected things we later realized mattered more, and sometimes allowed a number to become far more important than whatever it was originally supposed to serve. Some of those choices deserve honest reflection, and some may call for repair. But I don't want reflection on the first half of life to become one final performance review.

We were learning how to live while we were living. There is some mercy in recognizing that. The younger versions of ourselves made decisions with the information, maturity, responsibilities, fears, and hopes they had at the time. They built imperfectly, as everyone does, but they also built much of the life we now have the luxury of examining. I am grateful to them.

### The Life We Were Preparing For

For much of adulthood, preparing for later is exactly what responsibility requires. We work, save, practice, plan, maintain our health, and accept obligations whose rewards may not arrive for years. A great deal of what is good in my life exists because some earlier version of me was willing to think beyond whatever he wanted on a particular Tuesday.

I do not want to tell that person he should have worried less about the future and spent more time contemplating sunsets. He was raising a family, building a career, paying a mortgage, and trying to make choices that would give the people he loved a stable life. There were seasons when preparation needed a great deal of his attention because there really was a great deal to prepare.

The strange thing is that eventually some of the future we prepared for begins to arrive. The career becomes established, the children grow, the mortgage gets smaller, the retirement account becomes something more than an optimistic line on a spreadsheet, and the person who always seemed to exist somewhere out ahead of us begins looking suspiciously like the person in the mirror. Later does not arrive all at once, so it can be surprisingly easy to miss.

This is where the habits of mastery can continue long after they have completed some of their original work. We know how to prepare, so we keep preparing; we know how to improve, so we keep improving. There is always another level of security we could reach before relaxing, another professional milestone before feeling established, or another improvement to ourselves before we feel ready to inhabit the life we already have.

The answer is not to stop caring about tomorrow. Tomorrow still deserves something from me, and there are responsibilities I would be foolish to neglect in the name of presence. But perhaps tomorrow does not need to justify today.

That leaves room for a different relationship with the life all that preparation helped create. I can care about my health while recognizing that health exists so I can live, rather than living so I can maintain excellent health metrics. I can manage money responsibly while remembering that security was meant to create freedom rather than an endless obligation to accumulate more of it. I can continue doing meaningful work without requiring the next achievement to prove that the previous twenty years mattered. The numbers remain useful in each case. What changes is their place in the story.

### Still Learning

I expect I will continue measuring things for the rest of my life. I like goals, plans, systems, and feedback far too much to emerge from writing this book as someone who wanders through meadows guided entirely by intuition. There will still be software that needs to work, money that needs managing, health that deserves attention, and commitments that require follow-through. The engineer is not going anywhere, nor do I want him to.

What I hope changes is my ability to notice when a useful number begins asking for more authority than it deserves. Sometimes it will reveal something I need to know, and I want enough humility to listen. Sometimes it will show that an approach is not working, and I want to change rather than protecting a comforting story from inconvenient evidence.

And sometimes the number will simply have finished speaking. I suspect I will miss that moment fairly often. I will turn a measurement into a verdict, optimize something that did not need optimizing, or continue staring at a dashboard after it has already answered the question that brought me there. There is a good chance I will check the sales figures for a book about our complicated relationship with numbers and feel disappointed that the number is not going up quickly enough.

That no longer strikes me as a contradiction that needs solving. It seems more like the ordinary work of being human. Wisdom may not be a condition in which I permanently transcend these habits, but a growing ability to recognize them, remember what I was trying to do in the first place, and return. Then, eventually, forget again. And return again.

### Enough for What?

Maybe that is why the question I want to carry forward is not simply *Do I have enough?* It is *Enough for what?* The second question gives the first one somewhere to stand because it returns the measurement to whatever purpose gave it meaning. Sometimes the answer will tell us to keep going. There are real needs we have not met, responsibilities we have not completed, skills we still need to develop, and worthwhile things that remain to be built. Growth is not the enemy of wisdom, and wanting something to improve does not mean we have failed to understand the argument of this book.

Other times, though, the answer may reveal that something we have been preparing for is already here. The money was meant to create some freedom, the work was meant to contribute something useful, the health was meant to give us a body capable of participating in life, and all those years of competence were meant to help us care for things that mattered. Perhaps we do not always need another milestone before allowing ourselves to notice what those efforts have made possible.

That does not require a dramatic change of life. Most of us will wake up tomorrow to roughly the same responsibilities we had today. There will be work to do, appointments to keep, people who need us, and numbers worth paying attention to. But there will also be the people we were providing for, the work we cared about, the music we played, the meals we shared, the losses we carried, and the ordinary afternoons we spent so many years trying to make possible. Those things were never distractions from the real project while we worked our way toward some final state of readiness. They were part of what all the preparation was for.

Perhaps wisdom is partly learning to recognize when we have arrived at something we once hoped to reach. The numbers are still there. They will be there tomorrow. But so will the world.

**Look up.**
