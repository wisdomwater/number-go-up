# Chapter 11 — Useful Servants

## So What Do We Put on the Dashboard?

At this point, a reasonable manager might be getting impatient with me.

Fine.

Metrics are incomplete.

Dashboards compress reality.

People optimize whatever we measure.

Numbers change behavior, shape culture, threaten identity, and occasionally attempt to tell us whether we're worthy human beings before breakfast.

Very interesting.

**I still have to run the company on Monday.**

Fair enough.

Budgets still have to balance.

Projects still need schedules.

Teams need goals.

Executives need to know what's happening.

Doctors need test results.

Investors need financial statements.

Churches need to know whether they can afford the electric bill.

And I would still very much like to know whether the changes I'm making to my diet are actually improving my health.

We are not getting rid of the numbers.

Nor should we.

The goal isn't to fire the numbers.

It's to clarify the reporting structure.

For most of this book, we've been examining what happens when measurement acquires too much authority. A proxy becomes a target. A target changes behavior. Behavior changes the signal. The number becomes a message about what matters. Eventually something that began as information can become judgment, identity, or culture.

But none of this makes measurement useless.

Quite the opposite.

Numbers are extraordinarily useful servants.

They reveal patterns we cannot see.

They challenge our intuitions.

They let us compare today with yesterday.

They make problems visible.

They help large organizations understand things no single person could possibly observe.

They tell us when reality disagrees with the story we've been telling ourselves.

I want that.

If my team believes we're getting faster at fixing bugs and the data says we're getting slower, I want to know.

If I believe I'm eating 2,000 calories a day and I'm actually eating 3,200, I would prefer that reality eventually interrupt the story.

If attendance at a church has been declining for three years, leadership should probably notice before arriving one Sunday to discover that the congregation consists of the pastor, the worship team, and one extremely committed person making coffee.

Numbers help us see.

The question is what happens next.

That's where our relationship with measurement often goes wrong.

We see a number.

We interpret the number.

We make a judgment.

We act.

All of this can happen so quickly that we barely notice the transitions.

The bug took too long.

Therefore the engineer is struggling.

Attendance is down.

Therefore the program isn't working.

Weight went up.

Therefore yesterday was a failure.

Sales declined.

Therefore customers don't value the product.

The number may be perfectly accurate.

It's the **therefore** that deserves our attention.

Because between measurement and judgment there is supposed to be something.

Conversation.

Context.

Curiosity.

The number tells us where to look.

Then we have to look.

That may mean talking to the engineer whose bug took three weeks.

Not:

**Why did this take you three weeks?**

But:

**What made this one difficult?**

Maybe the engineer underestimated it.

Maybe they got stuck and should have asked for help earlier.

That's useful to know.

Or maybe the bug crossed four subsystems, reproduced once every six hours, and turned out to expose an architectural weakness nobody knew existed.

That's useful to know too.

Perhaps the conversation reveals a problem with the engineer.

Perhaps it reveals a problem with the system.

Perhaps it reveals that there is no problem at all.

Some bugs are just hard.

The number cannot know which explanation is true.

It can only point.

This is a very different relationship with a dashboard.

The dashboard is no longer a remote-control panel for the organization.

It's a map of places worth visiting.

Something is happening over here.

Go look.

Ask.

Listen.

Understand.

Then decide.

This requires more work than simply setting thresholds and watching for red cells.

Human beings are inconvenient that way.

They insist on bringing context with them.

But context is not noise surrounding the data.

Often it is the information we need in order to understand what the data means.

This is especially important when there is a power difference between the measurer and the measuree.

From the manager's side, a red metric may look like a question.

From the employee's side, it may feel like an accusation.

We've seen this throughout the book.

**The measurer sees information.**

**The measuree experiences an incentive.**

And when livelihood, reputation, promotion, or belonging are involved, even a seemingly innocent question can arrive carrying considerably more weight than the person asking it realizes.

That's why the quality of the conversation matters.

If every outlier becomes a trial, people will learn to avoid outliers.

They will choose safer work.

They will make the number defensible.

They will spend energy preparing explanations instead of solving problems.

And eventually the organization may become wonderfully efficient at producing numbers nobody has to explain.

That isn't necessarily the same thing as producing good work.

A healthier conversation treats the outlier as an opportunity to learn.

**What happened?**

**What made this difficult?**

**What did we learn?**

**Is there something we could change that would make this easier next time?**

Those questions leave open the possibility of accountability.

Sometimes someone really did make a poor decision.

Sometimes a pattern really does need to change.

Curiosity is not the abandonment of standards.

But there is a significant difference between investigating what happened and prosecuting the person whose number turned red.

We've seen that distinction before too.

It applies when we manage ourselves just as much as when we manage other people.

The scale goes up.

What happened?

The project misses a milestone.

What happened?

The budget is over forecast.

What happened?

The attendance drops.

What happened?

Not every answer will make us happy.

That's okay.

The purpose of curiosity isn't to make reality pleasant.

It's to understand reality well enough to respond wisely.

And this may be the most useful role a metric can play.

Not to give us the answer.

To help us ask a better question.

A good metric should deepen the conversation, not end it.

It should move us toward the reality it represents.

Toward the work.

Toward the context.

Toward the people.

Because the dashboard is not where the organization lives.

The scale is not where health lives.

Attendance is not where community lives.

Sales are not where meaning lives.

The numbers are signs pointing toward something else.

So yes, keep the dashboard.

Put numbers on it.

Make them accurate.

Make them visible.

Pay attention when they change.

Just don't stop there.

Use numbers to know where to look.

Then go look.

## Start With the Point

Before we decide what belongs on the dashboard, there is a more important question.

**What's the point?**

We've asked this question repeatedly throughout the book, partly because I think it may be more useful than any framework I could give you.

Frameworks are easy to admire.

They have boxes.

Arrows.

Occasionally circles.

If someone is feeling particularly ambitious, the circles overlap.

Then we give the whole thing a name, put it on a slide, and spend the next three years measuring whether people are following it.

I would rather give you a question.

**What's the point?**

Before choosing the metric, ask what you're actually trying to accomplish.

This sounds painfully obvious.

It isn't.

Organizations rarely begin by saying, "Let's create a meaningless metric and spend thousands of hours optimizing it."

The metric usually begins close to something people genuinely care about.

We want to ship reliable software.

We want customers to be happy.

We want employees to be productive.

We want AI to make engineering more effective.

We want people to be healthy.

We want churches to help people grow.

We want books to reach readers.

Then comes the difficult part.

How will we know?

That's where measurement enters.

And because the thing we actually care about is often complicated, we find something nearby that is easier to count.

Bugs closed.

Customer satisfaction scores.

Commits.

AI usage.

Weight.

Attendance.

Copies sold.

There's nothing wrong with this.

We need proxies.

The trouble begins when the proxy survives and the purpose fades into the background.

Imagine an engineering organization wants to increase the use of AI.

That might be an entirely reasonable goal.

AI tools may help engineers understand unfamiliar code, generate tests, debug problems, explore designs, automate repetitive work, or simply spend less time staring at an error message wondering why the computer has chosen violence today.

So someone proposes measuring AI adoption.

How many engineers use the tools?

How often?

How many tokens do they consume?

How many workflows involve AI?

Again, reasonable questions.

But before we put any of them on a dashboard, we should walk backward.

Why do we want AI adoption?

Presumably not because the universe is improved when more tokens are consumed.

We want something else.

Maybe faster development.

Better quality.

Less repetitive work.

Faster learning.

More time for difficult problems.

Greater engineering capacity.

Now we're closer to the point.

That changes how we interpret the adoption metric.

Suppose AI usage goes up 40 percent while development time, quality, and employee experience remain unchanged.

Did we succeed?

We succeeded at AI usage.

That's what the number says.

Maybe the benefits simply haven't appeared yet.

Maybe we're measuring the wrong outcomes.

Maybe engineers are still learning.

Maybe AI is producing benefits we haven't recognized.

Or maybe people learned that management wanted the number to go up, so the number went up.

We need a conversation.

Now reverse it.

Suppose AI usage falls 20 percent, but teams are shipping faster with fewer defects because they have learned to use AI selectively on the tasks where it provides the most leverage.

Did the initiative fail?

Only if we forgot the point.

This is why purpose has to remain upstream from measurement.

The metric exists because something matters.

The thing does not matter because we found a metric for it.

That ordering sounds almost too simple to mention.

And yet much of this book has been about what happens when we reverse it.

We begin with:

**We want healthier people.**

So we measure weight.

Eventually:

**We want the weight to go down.**

We begin with:

**We want reliable software.**

So we measure bugs.

Eventually:

**We need to close more bugs.**

We begin with:

**We want people to experience meaningful community and spiritual growth.**

So we count attendance.

Eventually:

**We need more people in the room.**

We begin with:

**We want this book to reach people.**

So I look at copies sold.

Eventually I could find myself refreshing an Amazon page wondering whether my life's work has moved from number 43,812 to number 39,604 in a category I didn't know existed.

The proxy quietly becomes the goal.

There is a useful way to catch this happening.

Finish the sentence:

**We are measuring ______ because we care about ______.**

Bugs closed because we care about reliable software.

Weight because I care about health.

Attendance because we care about people and community.

Sales because I care about reaching readers and sustaining the work.

Revenue because the business needs to create economic value and remain viable.

Now look at the second blank.

That's the important one.

The first blank serves it.

This gives us our familiar ordering:

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment**

Meaning comes first.

What matters?

What are we trying to accomplish?

What kind of organization are we trying to build?

What kind of person am I trying to become?

What are we hoping will be different?

Then measurement.

What can we observe that will help us understand whether that is happening?

What signals should we pay attention to?

What evidence would challenge our assumptions?

Then judgment.

Given what the numbers tell us, what the people tell us, what the context tells us, and what we now understand, what should we do?

The order matters.

If measurement comes first, whatever is easiest to count can quietly determine what matters.

If judgment comes before measurement, we may simply use the data to confirm what we've already decided.

Meaning gives us direction.

Measurement gives us information.

Judgment gives us action.

And then reality responds.

Maybe our actions work.

Maybe they don't.

Maybe the metric turns out to be a poor proxy.

Maybe the goal itself needs refinement.

Maybe we discover that what we thought mattered isn't quite what matters after all.

That's allowed.

Purpose isn't immune from learning either.

The point isn't to carve a mission statement into stone and force every number beneath it forever.

It's to keep the relationship visible.

**Why are we measuring this?**

If we can't answer that anymore, the metric may have outlived its purpose.

If our answer is, "Because it's on the dashboard," we have a problem.

If the answer is, "Because leadership asks about it," ask why leadership asks about it.

If the answer is, "Because we've always measured it," congratulations. We have discovered a metric with tenure.

Keep walking backward.

Eventually we should reach something we actually care about.

And if we don't, perhaps we've found something we can stop measuring.

This is one reason **What's the point?** is such a useful question.

It works almost anywhere.

It works in a strategy meeting.

It works when designing an OKR.

It works when choosing a health goal.

It works when looking at a church budget.

It works when deciding whether to check your phone for the seventeenth time to see how many people liked something you wrote.

It interrupts the momentum of measurement.

It asks the number to explain why it is in the room.

And once we remember the point, we can ask the next question.

**What does this number actually know about it?**

## Know What the Number Knows

Once we know the point, we can choose something to measure.

Then we should immediately become suspicious of our own interpretation.

Not suspicious of the number.

Suspicious of how much we want the number to say.

Suppose my team closed fifty bugs this month.

That's information.

Fifty bugs were closed.

Depending on how the data is collected, we may need to clarify exactly what counts as closed, but let's not ruin a perfectly good example.

The number knows something.

Now watch how quickly we can expand its résumé.

Fifty bugs were closed.

Therefore the team was productive.

Therefore quality improved.

Therefore the engineers performed well.

Therefore the organization is healthy.

Therefore our management strategy is working.

That is an impressive career trajectory for a bug count.

The number hasn't changed.

Our claim about what it means has.

This is why every metric should have something like a job description.

**Metric: Bugs closed**

Qualified to discuss: how many bugs were closed during a particular period.

May provide evidence about: workload, maintenance activity, throughput, product quality, team capacity.

Not independently authorized to determine: engineering productivity, code quality, employee performance, team health, whether anyone deserves a promotion.

Definitely not qualified to answer: **Is Steve a good person?**

This sounds silly because the overreach is obvious when we spell it out.

Usually we don't.

The promotion happens gradually.

A metric starts as one signal among many.

Then it appears on a dashboard.

Then someone asks about it regularly.

Then targets are attached.

Then teams are compared.

Then performance conversations begin referencing it.

Eventually the proxy has acquired authority nobody explicitly decided to give it.

The same thing happens in our personal lives.

The scale measures weight.

That's it.

Weight matters.

For someone trying to lose a significant amount of weight, as I am, it can be extremely useful information.

Over time, the trend can tell me whether the things I'm doing are producing the outcome I want.

But the scale does not measure what I ate yesterday.

It doesn't measure whether I exercised.

It doesn't measure my cardiovascular fitness.

It doesn't measure my blood pressure.

It doesn't measure how I slept.

And despite the additional feature I apparently installed in my own head, it does not measure whether I have been good.

The scale hasn't overreached.

I have.

This distinction becomes especially important when a metric is genuinely correlated with something we care about.

Correlation gives the proxy legitimacy.

That's why we chose it.

If there were no relationship at all, the metric wouldn't be interesting.

Attendance can tell us something about whether people are connecting with a church.

Sales can tell us something about whether a product is providing value people are willing to pay for.

Customer satisfaction scores can tell us something about customer experience.

Bug counts can tell us something about software quality.

Performance ratings can tell us something about employee contribution.

Weight can tell us something about health.

The word **something** is doing important work.

A proxy does not have to be perfect to be useful.

It just needs to help us see.

The mistake is turning **related to** into **equal to**.

Attendance is related to community.

Attendance is not community.

Weight is related to health.

Weight is not health.

Sales are related to value.

Sales are not value.

A performance rating is related to performance.

A performance rating is not a person.

This is where precision can become deceptive.

Suppose one team has a productivity score of 83.7 and another has a score of 79.2.

Those decimal places look awfully confident.

The temptation is to think we therefore know exactly how much more productive the first team is.

Maybe we do.

But before we celebrate the 4.5-point productivity gap, it would be useful to ask what went into the score.

What was counted?

What wasn't?

How were the dimensions weighted?

How much judgment was embedded in the formula?

What assumptions connect the calculation to this wonderfully large word **productivity**?

The calculation may be precise.

The meaning may still be approximate.

We've seen this before:

**Precision in measurement is not precision in meaning.**

This doesn't invalidate the score.

It tells us how to hold it.

Modestly.

The same principle applies when the number is inconvenient.

If the metric goes against what I want to believe, I don't get to dismiss it by shouting, "Context!"

Context is not an emergency exit from evidence.

If my team has missed every delivery target for six months, I should pay attention.

If customers are consistently reporting the same problem, I should pay attention.

If my weight has been trending upward for a year while I insist that my current habits are working beautifully, I should pay attention.

Sometimes the number sees a pattern that the person standing inside the experience cannot.

That's one of the reasons we measure.

The humility has to work in both directions.

Don't ask the metric to know more than it knows.

Don't pretend it knows nothing when you dislike what it says.

Instead, ask:

**What can this number legitimately tell me?**

**What can it not tell me?**

**What larger reality am I using it as evidence for?**

**What other evidence would I need before making that claim?**

Those questions put boundaries around the metric without neutering it.

They give the number authority within its domain.

And they keep it from wandering around the organization making decisions for which it was never qualified.

Maybe dashboards should come with warning labels.

**BUGS CLOSED**

*Useful for understanding bug throughput. Results may vary. Do not use as a substitute for talking to engineers. Not evaluated by the FDA for determining human worth.*

I would enjoy that dashboard.

But the principle underneath the joke is serious.

The more consequential the judgment, the more careful we should be about the distance between what was measured and what we are claiming it means.

If I want to know how many bugs were closed, count the bugs.

If I want to know whether an engineer is doing good work, I need more.

If I want to know whether a team is healthy, I need more still.

If I want to know whether the organization is succeeding, now we're asking a very large question.

The metric can come with us.

It just shouldn't pretend it arrived there by itself.

This is the discipline of keeping the proxy in its place.

We don't diminish the number.

We give it a job it can actually do.

Then we listen carefully to what it tells us.

And before we turn that information into a target, we ask another question:

**What will people rationally do once they know we're watching it?**

## Ask What Behavior You’re Making Rational

Once we've chosen a metric and decided what it can legitimately tell us, there is one more thing to do before attaching a target to it.

Walk around to the other side of the dashboard.

Imagine you are the person being measured.

You know the number.

You know the target.

You know your manager knows the number.

You know your manager's manager probably knows the number.

Maybe it's discussed in staff meetings.

Maybe teams are compared.

Maybe bonuses, promotions, funding, or reputation are connected to it.

Now ask:

**What behavior have we made rational?**

Not:

**What behavior do we hope people will exhibit?**

That's easy.

We hope everyone will understand the spirit of the metric, pursue the underlying goal, exercise good judgment, preserve quality, collaborate generously, take appropriate risks, and somehow also hit the target.

Wonderful.

I hope that too.

But incentives have a way of becoming more concrete than intentions.

If we measure bugs closed, closing bugs becomes rational.

If we measure lines of code, writing code becomes rational.

If we measure customer calls handled, ending calls becomes rational.

If we measure attendance, attracting attendees becomes rational.

If we measure AI usage, using AI becomes rational.

This doesn't mean any of those behaviors are bad.

That's why we chose the metric in the first place.

The question is what happens when the measurable behavior and the underlying purpose begin to separate.

Suppose an organization wants engineers to use AI because leadership believes it can make development more effective.

So the organization creates an adoption target.

Perhaps 80 percent of engineers should use an approved AI tool every week.

What happens next?

Probably AI usage increases.

Excellent.

The metric worked.

People who weren't using AI begin experimenting with it.

Some discover genuinely useful workflows.

Some automate repetitive tasks.

Some learn unfamiliar code faster.

Some write better tests.

This is exactly what leadership hoped would happen.

But other things may happen too.

An engineer who doesn't find AI useful for their current work may use it anyway because they know usage is being tracked.

Someone may ask an AI tool a question they could answer faster themselves.

Someone may incorporate AI into a workflow not because it improves the workflow, but because **AI adoption** has become a visible organizational expectation.

Someone may discover that the easiest way to demonstrate enthusiastic participation is to generate a great deal of measurable activity.

Now the dashboard looks fantastic.

AI adoption: 94 percent.

Number go up.

Did engineering improve?

Maybe.

We have to go look.

This is Goodhart's Law returning one last time, not as a clever warning but as a design question.

Before attaching consequences to a metric, ask:

**How could someone make this number look fantastic without improving the thing we actually care about?**

This question is wonderfully uncomfortable.

It forces us to think like the person living under the measurement system.

If I reward bugs closed, could someone choose easy bugs?

Could they split one problem into several tickets?

Could they avoid difficult work?

Could they ship quickly and create more bugs to close later?

We've already met that last system.

It was extremely productive.

If I reward delivery speed, could people skip testing?

If I reward utilization, could people keep themselves visibly busy while avoiding work that requires thinking?

If I reward customer satisfaction, could employees become reluctant to tell customers something they don't want to hear?

If I reward attendance, could we optimize for getting people through the door while neglecting what happens once they're inside?

If I reward weight loss, could I choose behaviors that make the scale move while making my health worse?

These aren't accusations.

They're design questions.

That distinction matters.

It is easy for the measurer to look at undesirable behavior and conclude that the measuree lacks integrity.

Sometimes that's true.

People cheat.

People manipulate.

People lie.

No measurement philosophy will eliminate human dishonesty.

But often the behavior isn't dishonest at all.

It's adaptation.

People notice what the system rewards.

Then they allocate attention accordingly.

This is especially rational when resources are limited.

An engineer has forty hours.

They can spend ten of them mentoring a junior colleague, untangling a difficult architectural problem, improving documentation, or closing several visible bugs.

If only one of those activities reliably appears in the performance conversation, we should not be shocked when attention drifts toward it.

The system has provided information.

Not merely about performance.

About survival.

This is why I don't find it especially useful to complain that people are "gaming the system" without first examining the game.

We created the rules.

We published the score.

We attached consequences.

Then the players noticed.

What exactly did we expect?

Again:

**The measurer sees information.**

**The measuree experiences an incentive.**

The distance between those two perspectives can be enormous.

A leader may genuinely intend a metric as a lightweight indicator.

An employee may experience it as a statement about what they need to do to remain in good standing.

A manager may say:

**Don't overthink the number. It's just one data point.**

But if that data point appears every month, is reviewed by leadership, is compared across teams, and shows up during performance discussions, people will quite reasonably overthink it.

Authority gives numbers weight.

This doesn't mean we should never attach consequences to metrics.

Consequences are sometimes the point.

A sales target without any expectation that salespeople try to reach it is mostly decorative.

A safety threshold nobody is expected to respect isn't much of a safety threshold.

A budget that everyone can ignore is a spreadsheet-themed art project.

Standards matter.

Accountability matters.

Targets can focus effort.

The question is whether we've thought honestly about the behavior they are likely to produce.

And that includes behavior outside the target.

What gets neglected?

What becomes risky?

What becomes invisible?

What becomes embarrassing?

What work will a reasonable person stop doing because the system has made something else more valuable?

This is where the most interesting consequences often live.

The obvious effect of a bug-closure target is that more bugs may get closed.

The less obvious effect is that people may avoid the hardest bugs.

The obvious effect of a delivery target is that projects may move faster.

The less obvious effect is that engineers may become reluctant to stop and fix foundational problems.

The obvious effect of an individual productivity metric is that individual output may rise.

The less obvious effect is that helping someone else becomes expensive.

The dashboard sees what increases.

The culture may be shaped by what disappears.

So before launching a metric, try standing underneath it.

Imagine that your livelihood depends, at least partly, on making this number look good.

Imagine that you are busy.

Imagine that priorities conflict.

Imagine that your manager says quality matters, collaboration matters, learning matters, and this metric matters.

Then imagine that only one of those things turns red on a dashboard.

What would you do?

That's not cynicism.

It's empathy.

And empathy is surprisingly useful in metric design.

It helps us recognize that measurement is never merely something we do **to** a system.

In human systems, measurement becomes part of the system.

People see it.

Interpret it.

Respond to it.

Talk about it.

Worry about it.

Learn from it.

Work around it.

Sometimes resent it.

Sometimes use it to do better work.

The metric enters the environment and changes what happens next.

Which means we cannot evaluate a metric only by asking whether the number moved.

We also have to ask what happened to the people and the work underneath it.

And to understand that, eventually, we have to talk to them.

## Keep the Conversation Attached

Maybe the simplest way to describe a healthy relationship with metrics is this:

**Metrics should be talking points, not verdicts.**

A number gives us something to talk about.

Something changed.

Something is unusual.

A target was missed.

A pattern is emerging.

One team looks different from the others.

One project is taking longer than expected.

Good.

Let's talk about it.

The trouble begins when the number arrives with the conversation already finished.

**Target missed.**

Therefore performance problem.

**Bug took twelve days.**

Therefore engineer was too slow.

**Sales declined.**

Therefore strategy failed.

**Weight increased.**

Therefore bad week.

Once again, the dangerous word is **therefore**.

The number may deserve our attention.

It may even be pointing toward a serious problem.

But attention and judgment are not the same thing.

A red cell should mean:

**Look here.**

Not:

**Guilty.**

This distinction becomes especially important with outliers.

Suppose an engineering organization is tracking the time between when bugs are filed and when they're closed.

There are good reasons to care about this.

Bugs that sit around for months can frustrate customers, create technical debt, and make the backlog increasingly resemble an archaeological site.

If the average bug takes thirty days to resolve and we can bring that down to fifteen without sacrificing quality, wonderful.

But averages hide stories.

Imagine most bugs are closed within two weeks, while one takes forty-five days.

The dashboard highlights it.

Red.

Now we have a choice about what that red means.

We can ask:

**Why did this take forty-five days?**

Even written on the page, you can hear how easily that question can become an accusation.

The engineer already knows the number is bad.

They know their manager knows.

They may know leadership knows.

So they enter the conversation prepared to defend themselves.

I couldn't reproduce it.

Another team owned part of the code.

The logs weren't sufficient.

The first fix failed.

The requirements changed.

I was pulled onto another priority.

Maybe every explanation is legitimate.

Maybe some aren't.

But notice what we've created.

The conversation begins with prosecution.

The engineer's job is to establish innocence.

Now imagine starting somewhere else.

**What made this bug hard to solve?**

The difference is subtle.

The second question still allows accountability.

Maybe the answer is:

Honestly, I got stuck and waited too long before asking for help.

That's useful.

Perhaps the team needs clearer expectations about escalation.

Maybe the engineer needs coaching.

Maybe pairing earlier would have saved two weeks.

The conversation can still produce change.

But perhaps the answer is:

This bug only reproduced under a rare timing condition. We didn't have enough logging to see it. I eventually built a diagnostic tool, found a race condition in a shared component, and discovered three other bugs caused by the same architectural problem.

That's useful too.

Now the forty-five-day bug isn't merely an outlier to explain away.

It's information about the system.

Maybe the real question becomes:

**What could we change so the next person doesn't have to spend forty-five days discovering the same thing?**

Better logging?

Better tooling?

Documentation?

Automated testing?

A change in architecture?

Earlier collaboration across teams?

The metric has done something valuable.

It found a conversation.

This is what dashboards can be excellent at.

They help us notice where reality differs from expectation.

That's interesting.

**Why?**

Not **Who do we blame?**

**What can we learn?**

The distinction matters because people learn what kind of conversation follows a bad number.

If every outlier becomes a defense, people will eventually try not to become outliers.

That sounds desirable until you remember that some of the most valuable work is inherently risky.

The hardest bugs take longer.

The most ambitious projects have more uncertainty.

Experiments fail.

New engineers learn.

Experienced engineers sometimes take on problems precisely because nobody knows how to solve them.

If every ugly number requires a defense, the measurement system can quietly teach people:

**Choose work you can explain.**

**Choose work you can predict.**

**Choose work that keeps the dashboard clean.**

That may improve the dashboard.

It may not improve the organization.

This is why psychological safety and measurement are more connected than they first appear.

People need to be able to say:

**The number looks bad, but here's what happened.**

They also need to be able to say:

**The number looks great, but I don't think we should celebrate yet.**

The second sentence may be even more important.

Imagine bug closure time drops dramatically.

Excellent.

Then an engineer says:

We're closing bugs faster because we're categorizing more of the difficult ones as deferred.

Now we have information the dashboard couldn't provide.

Or sales jump.

Wonderful.

Then someone says:

Yes, but most of the increase came from a discount we can't sustain.

Or attendance rises.

Great.

Then someone says:

We're attracting a lot of new people, but we're also seeing long-time volunteers burning out.

Or my weight drops four pounds in a week.

Fantastic.

Then I remember I was sick for three days.

The conversation restores context.

This is why talking points work in both directions.

A bad number isn't automatically failure.

A good number isn't automatically success.

Both deserve curiosity.

That doesn't mean every metric review needs to become a philosophical seminar.

Sometimes the explanation is simple.

Why did we miss the target?

We made a mistake.

Okay.

Fix it.

Sometimes a bug really did take too long.

Sometimes spending really is out of control.

Sometimes performance really is poor.

Sometimes the scale is telling me that my wonderfully intuitive approach to portion size has become unusually generous.

Conversation does not mean endless excuse-making.

It means we understand before we judge.

That's the order.

**Measurement → Conversation → Judgment.**

And underneath all three:

**Meaning.**

What were we trying to accomplish?

What does the number tell us?

What happened?

What context matters?

What should we do?

That sequence leaves room for accountability without turning every deviation into a moral event.

It also changes the manager's role.

The manager is not merely the person who enforces the dashboard.

They become someone who helps translate between the dashboard and the work.

They can see patterns across people and projects that an individual engineer cannot.

The engineer can see details inside the work that the manager cannot.

Neither perspective is sufficient.

So they talk.

The manager comes down from thirty thousand feet.

The engineer helps them understand the ground.

Then both go back to the map with better information.

**The dashboard sends us toward reality.**

**Reality sends us back toward the dashboard with better questions.**

That's the loop.

And it requires relationship.

A sufficiently sophisticated dashboard cannot eliminate the need to know the people doing the work.

In fact, the more consequential the metric becomes, the more important the relationship may be.

Because numbers compress.

Relationships restore context.

A manager who knows their people can recognize when an outlier is unusual.

They know who tends to take difficult work.

They know who quietly helps everyone else.

They know when someone's performance has genuinely changed.

They know when the dashboard is revealing a problem and when it is simply revealing the limits of the dashboard.

Not perfectly.

Managers are human too.

They misread situations.

They have biases.

They need data precisely because relationship alone isn't enough.

But the answer to imperfect human judgment isn't necessarily to remove the humans.

It's to let the evidence and the relationship correct each other.

Use the metric to challenge the story.

Use the story to interpret the metric.

And keep the conversation attached.

Because a number without a conversation can become a verdict.

A number inside a conversation can become understanding.

**The metric should begin the investigation, not conclude it.**
