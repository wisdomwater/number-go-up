# 11 — Look Up

An old man sits outside an ice cream shop with his granddaughter. She is completely absorbed in a cone that is melting faster than she can eat it, while he works on his own with considerably more experience. They talk about whatever comes to mind, watch people pass on the sidewalk, and occasionally race the melting edges before they reach their hands. For a few minutes, there is nowhere else either of them needs to be.

From the outside, they appear to be doing exactly the same thing. They are eating ice cream and enjoying an afternoon together. Yet they have arrived at that little table from opposite ends of life. The child has not yet learned much about planning, productivity, mortgages, calories, retirement accounts, or the thousand other calculations adulthood eventually teaches us to make. The old man has.

He knows the ice cream has calories. He knows the afternoon will end, bodies age, money runs out if nobody pays attention to it, and the people sitting across from us will not always be there. He has spent a lifetime learning that choices have consequences and tomorrow deserves some consideration. None of that knowledge disappears while he sits at the table. And still he eats the ice cream.

The child is present because she has not yet learned mastery.

The old man is present because he has passed through it.

### We Don't Get to Become Children Again

There is a temptation in talking about wonder to romanticize childhood. Children seem wonderfully present, spontaneous, and unconcerned with productivity, which is easier when someone else is paying the mortgage. Their ability to disappear into an afternoon is beautiful, but it rests upon a world of adult competence they do not yet see. Someone remembered the schedule, earned the money, drove the car, and probably brought wipes.

We don't recover wonder by forgetting what adulthood taught us. I cannot become wiser by pretending money doesn't matter, ignoring my health, abandoning commitments, or deciding that deadlines are merely unfortunate manifestations of Western consciousness. If I stop paying the electric bill because I have transcended my attachment to measurable outcomes, the electric company will eventually provide a fairly concrete lesson in cause and effect. Reality still gets a vote.

Growing up required something valuable from us. We learned to anticipate consequences, practice difficult things, delay gratification, keep promises, solve problems, and care for people whose needs extended beyond the present moment. We became capable of building things that spontaneity alone could never build. Mastery enlarged what we were capable of doing in the world.

That was not a mistake.

It was development.

The child encounters the world before learning how to master it. The adult learns to stand back, understand how things work, and act deliberately upon them. Perhaps wisdom begins when we carry all of that knowledge forward and discover that we can encounter the world again. We do not return to where we started, because the person returning is no longer the person who left.

### The Engineer and the Contemplative

I have sometimes felt as though two ways of seeing the world live inside me. The engineer wants evidence, reproducibility, clear definitions, failure modes, and enough information to distinguish what I hope is happening from what is actually happening. That instinct has served me well for decades. It has taught me that sincerity does not make bad code work and that reality is under no obligation to cooperate with my preferred explanation.

The contemplative part of me asks different questions. What am I not noticing because I am trying to solve something? What happens if I remain here without immediately changing the experience? Can I listen to another person long enough for them to become more than the problem I think needs fixing? These questions do not compete with the engineer's questions so much as turn my attention in another direction.

For a long time, it was easy to divide the two worlds. Engineering belonged to work, where things had to function, while contemplation belonged to prayer, music, silence, and whatever parts of life began after the laptop closed. One dealt with reality and the other with meaning. The division became harder to maintain once I began noticing how often reality itself required both ways of seeing.

If software is running slowly, I should profile it. Sitting quietly before the source code and becoming receptive to its mystery is unlikely to improve latency. But if someone I love is grieving and there is nothing left for me to fix, another diagnostic procedure may be exactly the wrong response. Wisdom has less to do with choosing between competence and presence than with recognizing which one the moment requires.

The engineer asks, *What can I do?* The contemplative asks, *Can I stay?* Some moments call almost entirely for the first question, while others eventually bring us to the second. A mature life becomes large enough to hold both.

### Use the Instrument, Then Put It Down

Perhaps the simplest practical lesson I have found in all of this is to **use the instrument, then put it down**.

If I am trying to lose weight, the scale can tell me whether the long-term trend is moving in the direction I hope. I can take the information seriously, make whatever decisions it reasonably supports, and then step off. The scale has completed its part of the conversation. It does not need to accompany me to breakfast.

The same principle extends far beyond the bathroom. Numbers can help us see what intuition misses, expose self-deception, coordinate complicated work, protect our health, and prepare for futures that have not yet arrived. They deserve a place in our lives because they have earned one. The question throughout this book has never really been whether the instrument is useful, but how much authority we give it after it has answered the question we asked.

Every instrument has a range. A thermometer can tell me whether someone has a fever but not whether they are frightened, just as a balance sheet can tell me what I own without telling me what my life is for. Asking either instrument for more does not reveal its hidden wisdom. It simply asks it to answer a question it was never designed to answer.

Wisdom does not require distrusting the instrument. It requires recognizing when the instrument has finished speaking. There are moments when another measurement will make us wiser, and moments when another measurement merely postpones whatever comes next. Learning the difference may be one of the quieter skills of maturity.

The tragedy isn't that we learned to measure. It's that we forgot how to stop.

### Leave Room for Surprise

Mastery naturally tries to reduce surprise. In engineering, unexpected behavior usually means something has escaped our model, and much of my professional life has involved making systems behave more predictably. I want the brakes on my car to work every time, the airplane wing to remain attached, and payroll to contain very little creative improvisation. There are parts of life where predictability is a gift.

But a life from which surprise has been successfully eliminated would be strangely impoverished. Other people refuse to follow our models perfectly, conversations wander somewhere we did not intend, and music occasionally becomes more than the performance we rehearsed. We make plans because plans matter, then discover that some of the moments we remember most clearly were never on the schedule. Life keeps exceeding the structures we build for it.

This does not make preparation pointless. The musicians still rehearse, the trip still needs reservations, and the grandfather probably still has to remember where he parked the car. Preparation creates conditions in which experience can unfold. It simply does not need to determine everything that happens inside those conditions.

That distinction would once have sounded suspicious to the engineer in me. Unexpected behavior meant I had failed to understand the system well enough. Yet human life is not merely an engineering problem with an unusually large number of variables. Some of what I cannot control is not a defect waiting for a better model.

Sometimes it is the gift.

### The Second Encounter

The old man and the child are still sitting outside the ice cream shop. By now her hands are sticky, his cone is nearly gone, and whatever conversation they were having has probably wandered through several subjects without producing anything that could reasonably be entered on a progress report. Nothing about the afternoon is especially impressive. That may be part of what makes it important.

The child does not know how much work went into making this moment possible. She encounters the afternoon directly because she has not yet learned all the reasons she might stand outside it and evaluate it. One day she will learn those things. She will learn to plan, save, practice, compare, improve, worry, provide, and prepare.

The old man has already learned them. Perhaps he spent much of his life becoming very good at them, and perhaps that competence helped create the circumstances that allow him to sit here now. He does not need to regret the years of mastery in order to recognize that mastery has completed its work for the afternoon. There is nothing left that needs optimizing between the first bite and the last.

For a few minutes, grandfather and granddaughter meet in the same place from opposite directions.

The child has not yet learned to leave the moment.

The old man has learned how to return.

That is the second encounter. It is not innocence recovered by erasing experience, but wonder recovered through experience, after knowledge has had its say and competence has done its work. The first encounter does not yet know how to master the world; the second knows something about mastery and no longer needs to exercise it everywhere.

Perhaps the whole journey can be said simply.

**Innocence encounters. Mastery acts. Wisdom participates.**

Wisdom contains what came before it. Tomorrow the old man will still make plans, keep appointments, pay bills, watch his health, and solve whatever problems arrive. He has not transcended ordinary responsibility; he has simply stopped requiring responsibility to occupy every moment of his attention.

Right now, the ice cream is melting.

### Look Up

For most of this book, we have been looking at numbers. We have watched them describe reality, improve systems, change behavior, travel through organizations, follow us home, and eventually find their way into the stories we tell about ourselves. We have seen how useful they can be and how easily usefulness becomes authority. None of that requires us to abandon them.

The invitation is much smaller.

When the number has told you what you needed to know, look up.

There is still a world on the other side of the measurement. There are people who cannot be exhausted by what we know about them, experiences that do not need to improve us in order to matter, and moments whose value will never appear on a dashboard. We can enter those moments carrying everything adulthood has taught us.

That is what distinguishes wisdom from innocence. The child does not yet know the numbers. The mature person can know them, use them, respect them, and then allow them to become quiet.

The instrument has done its work.

The afternoon is still here.

And the ice cream is melting.
