# Chapter 11 — Useful Servants

## So What Do We Put on the Dashboard?

At this point, a reasonable manager might be getting impatient with me.

Fine.

Metrics are incomplete.

Dashboards compress reality.

People optimize whatever we measure.

Numbers change behavior, shape culture, threaten identity, and occasionally attempt to tell us whether we're worthy human beings before breakfast.

Very interesting.

**I still have to run the company on Monday.**

Fair enough.

Budgets still have to balance.

Projects still need schedules.

Teams need goals.

Executives need to know what's happening.

Doctors need test results.

Investors need financial statements.

Churches need to know whether they can afford the electric bill.

And I would still very much like to know whether the changes I'm making to my diet are actually improving my health.

We are not getting rid of the numbers.

Nor should we.

The goal isn't to fire the numbers.

It's to clarify the reporting structure.

For most of this book, we've been examining what happens when measurement acquires too much authority. A proxy becomes a target. A target changes behavior. Behavior changes the signal. The number becomes a message about what matters. Eventually something that began as information can become judgment, identity, or culture.

But none of this makes measurement useless.

Quite the opposite.

Numbers are extraordinarily useful servants.

They reveal patterns we cannot see.

They challenge our intuitions.

They let us compare today with yesterday.

They make problems visible.

They help large organizations understand things no single person could possibly observe.

They tell us when reality disagrees with the story we've been telling ourselves.

I want that.

If my team believes we're getting faster at fixing bugs and the data says we're getting slower, I want to know.

If I believe I'm eating 2,000 calories a day and I'm actually eating 3,200, I would prefer that reality eventually interrupt the story.

If attendance at a church has been declining for three years, leadership should probably notice before arriving one Sunday to discover that the congregation consists of the pastor, the worship team, and one extremely committed person making coffee.

Numbers help us see.

The question is what happens next.

That's where our relationship with measurement often goes wrong.

We see a number.

We interpret the number.

We make a judgment.

We act.

All of this can happen so quickly that we barely notice the transitions.

The bug took too long.

Therefore the engineer is struggling.

Attendance is down.

Therefore the program isn't working.

Weight went up.

Therefore yesterday was a failure.

Sales declined.

Therefore customers don't value the product.

The number may be perfectly accurate.

It's the **therefore** that deserves our attention.

Because between measurement and judgment there is supposed to be something.

Conversation.

Context.

Curiosity.

The number tells us where to look.

Then we have to look.

That may mean talking to the engineer whose bug took three weeks.

Not:

**Why did this take you three weeks?**

But:

**What made this one difficult?**

Maybe the engineer underestimated it.

Maybe they got stuck and should have asked for help earlier.

That's useful to know.

Or maybe the bug crossed four subsystems, reproduced once every six hours, and turned out to expose an architectural weakness nobody knew existed.

That's useful to know too.

Perhaps the conversation reveals a problem with the engineer.

Perhaps it reveals a problem with the system.

Perhaps it reveals that there is no problem at all.

Some bugs are just hard.

The number cannot know which explanation is true.

It can only point.

This is a very different relationship with a dashboard.

The dashboard is no longer a remote-control panel for the organization.

It's a map of places worth visiting.

Something is happening over here.

Go look.

Ask.

Listen.

Understand.

Then decide.

This requires more work than simply setting thresholds and watching for red cells.

Human beings are inconvenient that way.

They insist on bringing context with them.

But context is not noise surrounding the data.

Often it is the information we need in order to understand what the data means.

This is especially important when there is a power difference between the measurer and the measuree.

From the manager's side, a red metric may look like a question.

From the employee's side, it may feel like an accusation.

We've seen this throughout the book.

**The measurer sees information.**

**The measuree experiences an incentive.**

And when livelihood, reputation, promotion, or belonging are involved, even a seemingly innocent question can arrive carrying considerably more weight than the person asking it realizes.

That's why the quality of the conversation matters.

If every outlier becomes a trial, people will learn to avoid outliers.

They will choose safer work.

They will make the number defensible.

They will spend energy preparing explanations instead of solving problems.

And eventually the organization may become wonderfully efficient at producing numbers nobody has to explain.

That isn't necessarily the same thing as producing good work.

A healthier conversation treats the outlier as an opportunity to learn.

**What happened?**

**What made this difficult?**

**What did we learn?**

**Is there something we could change that would make this easier next time?**

Those questions leave open the possibility of accountability.

Sometimes someone really did make a poor decision.

Sometimes a pattern really does need to change.

Curiosity is not the abandonment of standards.

But there is a significant difference between investigating what happened and prosecuting the person whose number turned red.

We've seen that distinction before too.

It applies when we manage ourselves just as much as when we manage other people.

The scale goes up.

What happened?

The project misses a milestone.

What happened?

The budget is over forecast.

What happened?

The attendance drops.

What happened?

Not every answer will make us happy.

That's okay.

The purpose of curiosity isn't to make reality pleasant.

It's to understand reality well enough to respond wisely.

And this may be the most useful role a metric can play.

Not to give us the answer.

To help us ask a better question.

A good metric should deepen the conversation, not end it.

It should move us toward the reality it represents.

Toward the work.

Toward the context.

Toward the people.

Because the dashboard is not where the organization lives.

The scale is not where health lives.

Attendance is not where community lives.

Sales are not where meaning lives.

The numbers are signs pointing toward something else.

So yes, keep the dashboard.

Put numbers on it.

Make them accurate.

Make them visible.

Pay attention when they change.

Just don't stop there.

Use numbers to know where to look.

Then go look.

## Start With the Point

Before we decide what belongs on the dashboard, there is a more important question.

**What's the point?**

We've asked this question repeatedly throughout the book, partly because I think it may be more useful than any framework I could give you.

Frameworks are easy to admire.

They have boxes.

Arrows.

Occasionally circles.

If someone is feeling particularly ambitious, the circles overlap.

Then we give the whole thing a name, put it on a slide, and spend the next three years measuring whether people are following it.

I would rather give you a question.

**What's the point?**

Before choosing the metric, ask what you're actually trying to accomplish.

This sounds painfully obvious.

It isn't.

Organizations rarely begin by saying, "Let's create a meaningless metric and spend thousands of hours optimizing it."

The metric usually begins close to something people genuinely care about.

We want to ship reliable software.

We want customers to be happy.

We want employees to be productive.

We want AI to make engineering more effective.

We want people to be healthy.

We want churches to help people grow.

We want books to reach readers.

Then comes the difficult part.

How will we know?

That's where measurement enters.

And because the thing we actually care about is often complicated, we find something nearby that is easier to count.

Bugs closed.

Customer satisfaction scores.

Commits.

AI usage.

Weight.

Attendance.

Copies sold.

There's nothing wrong with this.

We need proxies.

The trouble begins when the proxy survives and the purpose fades into the background.

Imagine an engineering organization wants to increase the use of AI.

That might be an entirely reasonable goal.

AI tools may help engineers understand unfamiliar code, generate tests, debug problems, explore designs, automate repetitive work, or simply spend less time staring at an error message wondering why the computer has chosen violence today.

So someone proposes measuring AI adoption.

How many engineers use the tools?

How often?

How many tokens do they consume?

How many workflows involve AI?

Again, reasonable questions.

But before we put any of them on a dashboard, we should walk backward.

Why do we want AI adoption?

Presumably not because the universe is improved when more tokens are consumed.

We want something else.

Maybe faster development.

Better quality.

Less repetitive work.

Faster learning.

More time for difficult problems.

Greater engineering capacity.

Now we're closer to the point.

That changes how we interpret the adoption metric.

Suppose AI usage goes up 40 percent while development time, quality, and employee experience remain unchanged.

Did we succeed?

We succeeded at AI usage.

That's what the number says.

Maybe the benefits simply haven't appeared yet.

Maybe we're measuring the wrong outcomes.

Maybe engineers are still learning.

Maybe AI is producing benefits we haven't recognized.

Or maybe people learned that management wanted the number to go up, so the number went up.

We need a conversation.

Now reverse it.

Suppose AI usage falls 20 percent, but teams are shipping faster with fewer defects because they have learned to use AI selectively on the tasks where it provides the most leverage.

Did the initiative fail?

Only if we forgot the point.

This is why purpose has to remain upstream from measurement.

The metric exists because something matters.

The thing does not matter because we found a metric for it.

That ordering sounds almost too simple to mention.

And yet much of this book has been about what happens when we reverse it.

We begin with:

**We want healthier people.**

So we measure weight.

Eventually:

**We want the weight to go down.**

We begin with:

**We want reliable software.**

So we measure bugs.

Eventually:

**We need to close more bugs.**

We begin with:

**We want people to experience meaningful community and spiritual growth.**

So we count attendance.

Eventually:

**We need more people in the room.**

We begin with:

**We want this book to reach people.**

So I look at copies sold.

Eventually I could find myself refreshing an Amazon page wondering whether my life's work has moved from number 43,812 to number 39,604 in a category I didn't know existed.

The proxy quietly becomes the goal.

There is a useful way to catch this happening.

Finish the sentence:

**We are measuring ______ because we care about ______.**

Bugs closed because we care about reliable software.

Weight because I care about health.

Attendance because we care about people and community.

Sales because I care about reaching readers and sustaining the work.

Revenue because the business needs to create economic value and remain viable.

Now look at the second blank.

That's the important one.

The first blank serves it.

This gives us our familiar ordering:

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment**

Meaning comes first.

What matters?

What are we trying to accomplish?

What kind of organization are we trying to build?

What kind of person am I trying to become?

What are we hoping will be different?

Then measurement.

What can we observe that will help us understand whether that is happening?

What signals should we pay attention to?

What evidence would challenge our assumptions?

Then judgment.

Given what the numbers tell us, what the people tell us, what the context tells us, and what we now understand, what should we do?

The order matters.

If measurement comes first, whatever is easiest to count can quietly determine what matters.

If judgment comes before measurement, we may simply use the data to confirm what we've already decided.

Meaning gives us direction.

Measurement gives us information.

Judgment gives us action.

And then reality responds.

Maybe our actions work.

Maybe they don't.

Maybe the metric turns out to be a poor proxy.

Maybe the goal itself needs refinement.

Maybe we discover that what we thought mattered isn't quite what matters after all.

That's allowed.

Purpose isn't immune from learning either.

The point isn't to carve a mission statement into stone and force every number beneath it forever.

It's to keep the relationship visible.

**Why are we measuring this?**

If we can't answer that anymore, the metric may have outlived its purpose.

If our answer is, "Because it's on the dashboard," we have a problem.

If the answer is, "Because leadership asks about it," ask why leadership asks about it.

If the answer is, "Because we've always measured it," congratulations. We have discovered a metric with tenure.

Keep walking backward.

Eventually we should reach something we actually care about.

And if we don't, perhaps we've found something we can stop measuring.

This is one reason **What's the point?** is such a useful question.

It works almost anywhere.

It works in a strategy meeting.

It works when designing an OKR.

It works when choosing a health goal.

It works when looking at a church budget.

It works when deciding whether to check your phone for the seventeenth time to see how many people liked something you wrote.

It interrupts the momentum of measurement.

It asks the number to explain why it is in the room.

And once we remember the point, we can ask the next question.

**What does this number actually know about it?**
