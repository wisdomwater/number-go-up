# Chapter 1: What Gets Measured Gets Managed

## The World Becomes Measurable

Human beings have been measuring things for a very long time.

This makes sense. If your neighbor owns the field next to yours, at some point you're going to need to agree about where his field ends and yours begins. If you're trading grain, it helps if both parties have roughly the same understanding of what a bushel is. If you're building a pyramid, "make that side about the same length as the other one" is probably not sufficient guidance.

We measured distance. We measured weight. We watched the movement of the sun and stars. We divided time into increasingly precise pieces. We counted livestock, marked property boundaries, collected taxes, and kept records of harvests.

Numbers are not a modern invention.

But something important began to change as the methods of modern science developed.

Measurement moved closer to the center of how we decided what was true.

It wasn't enough to say that something appeared heavier, faster, hotter, brighter, or farther away. Increasingly, the question became: **How much?**

How heavy?

How fast?

How hot?

How far?

And perhaps most importantly: can someone else measure it too?

That last question changed the world.

My experience belongs to me. I can tell you that the water feels warm, the object seems heavy, or the medicine made me feel better. Those observations may be perfectly sincere. They may even be correct.

But they're difficult to verify.

Measurement gives us a way to move beyond the limits of individual perception.

If I say the room feels cold and you say it feels comfortable, we can spend the afternoon discussing our respective relationships with temperature.

Or we can look at the thermometer.

Seventy-two degrees.

Now we have something neither of us owns.

We may still disagree about whether seventy-two degrees is cold. My wife and I can probably sustain that disagreement indefinitely. But we've separated two questions that previously ran together.

**What is the temperature?**

And:

**How do we experience it?**

That separation is enormously powerful.

The Scientific Revolution didn't happen because Europeans suddenly discovered rulers. It involved a much larger change in how people investigated nature: observation, experimentation, mathematical description, reproducibility, and a growing willingness to let evidence challenge what everyone thought they already knew.

Galileo became one of the great symbols of this shift.

Stories about him dropping objects from the Leaning Tower of Pisa are probably more legend than laboratory notebook, but the underlying change he represents is real. Instead of merely reasoning about how objects *should* move, investigators could observe motion, measure it, describe it mathematically, change the conditions, and see what happened.

Nature got a vote.

That's one of the things I love about science.

You can have a beautiful theory.

It can be elegant. It can make perfect sense. Everyone in the room can agree that it simply *must* be correct.

Then you perform the experiment.

Nope.

Reality can be terribly inconsiderate that way. Measurement gives reality a way to talk back. And as our measurements became more precise, remarkable patterns emerged.

The movements of planets could be described mathematically. Falling objects behaved in predictable ways. Pressure, temperature, electricity, magnetism, light—all of these phenomena could be observed, quantified, compared, and eventually woven into theories that did more than explain what had already happened.

They predicted what would happen next.

That is an extraordinary leap.

If a theory tells me not only why something happened yesterday but what will happen tomorrow under conditions that have never existed before, and then I create those conditions and the prediction comes true, I have good reason to pay attention.

Do that often enough and you begin building things.

Engines.

Electrical grids.

Radios.

Airplanes.

Computers.

Satellites.

The modern world is filled with objects that work because somebody measured something very carefully. This success has shaped us far beyond the laboratory. We learned a powerful lesson:

**If you want to understand something, measure it.**

And it's hard to argue with the results.

Measurement helped transform our ability to understand the physical world. It gave us a common language for comparing observations. It protected us, imperfectly but importantly, from our own biases and intuitions. It allowed knowledge to accumulate because one person's results could be tested by another.

Numbers earned our trust.

That's important to remember, because this book is going to spend a lot of time questioning the authority we give them.

But that authority didn't come from nowhere. We trust measurement because measurement works.

The interesting question is what happened when we took a tool that worked spectacularly well for understanding the measurable properties of the physical world and began asking it to help us understand nearly everything else.

## The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Numbers

There is something almost magical about measurement.

I don't mean magical in the supernatural sense. Your tape measure is probably not enchanted.

I mean that there is something genuinely strange about our ability to take the enormous complexity of the physical world, translate some part of it into numbers, manipulate those numbers on a piece of paper or a computer screen, and then use the result to predict what reality will do.

Think about a bridge.

Before anyone pours concrete or raises steel, engineers can calculate how much weight the bridge should support. They can model how the structure will respond to wind, traffic, temperature changes, and its own enormous weight. They can calculate forces acting on pieces of steel that don't exist yet.

Then people build the thing.

Cars drive across it.

Trucks drive across it.

The wind blows.

The seasons change.

And the bridge stays up.

I find that astonishing.

Or consider spaceflight. We can launch an object from a rotating planet moving around the sun, send it through millions or billions of miles of mostly empty space, and arrange for it to arrive near another object that has also been moving the entire time.

Nobody aims a spacecraft at Mars by looking through a window and saying, "A little to the left."

It's numbers all the way down.

Distance.

Mass.

Velocity.

Acceleration.

Gravity.

Time.

Tiny measurements and mathematical relationships describing a reality far too large for any human mind to experience directly.

And somehow they correspond.

This is what physicist Eugene Wigner famously called the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics." There is something remarkable about how well mathematical structures developed in our minds describe patterns in the world outside them.

But you don't need to launch a spacecraft to experience this.

Bake a loaf of bread.

Build a house.

Take medication.

Check your blood pressure.

Drive across a bridge.

Use GPS.

Turn on your phone.

The modern world is an enormous monument to the usefulness of quantification.

As an engineer, I live inside this world. If a piece of software is too slow, I measure it. Maybe I discover that an operation takes 800 milliseconds. I change something. Now it takes 300.

That's useful.

I don't need to convene a focus group to determine whether 300 milliseconds is less than 800 milliseconds.

Number go down.

Good.

This is measurement at its best.

There is a thing we care about. There is some property of that thing we can observe. We measure it consistently. The measurement corresponds closely enough to reality that changes in the number tell us something meaningful about changes in the system.

And because the measurement is external to us, it can correct us.

This may be measurement's greatest gift.

Human intuition is powerful, but it is also spectacularly capable of being wrong.

We remember selectively. We notice what confirms our expectations. We overestimate dramatic events and underestimate ordinary ones. We can become convinced that a software change made something faster because we spent three days making the change and would very much like those three days to have mattered.

Then we measure it.

Nope.

Still 800 milliseconds.

Reality remains stubbornly unimpressed by our effort.

That is a feature, not a bug.

Measurement gives the world a voice in our reasoning. It lets something outside our expectations push back.

And when many people measure the same thing using agreed-upon methods, it creates a shared point of reference. We can argue about what the result means, but at least we're arguing about the same result.

This is why I don't want to dismiss our cultural affection for numbers as some kind of modern sickness.

We've earned that affection. Numbers have been astonishingly good to us.

If I'm getting on an airplane, I want the engineers who designed it to have been almost obsessively interested in measurement. I do not want to hear that they decided the wing was strong enough after spending some quiet time honoring their intuition.

Measure the wing.

Please.

Measure it again.

There are places where precision isn't oppressive. It's merciful.

Medicine offers another example. Symptoms matter. A doctor's experience matters. A patient's description of how they feel matters.

But so does the blood test.

So does the scan.

So does the blood pressure reading.

Measurements can reveal something happening inside the body before the person feels anything at all. This is where numbers acquire their extraordinary authority.

They don't merely describe reality. Sometimes they reveal reality that our senses cannot perceive.

And once you've watched measurement accomplish this again and again—once you've seen it build bridges, land spacecraft, diagnose disease, and make computers work—it becomes very tempting to draw a larger conclusion.

If measurement gives us a clearer understanding of bridges, planets, computers, and bodies, perhaps measurement gives us a clearer understanding of everything.

Maybe every important question simply needs the right number.

How healthy is this organization?

How productive is this employee?

How well is this child learning?

How happy is this population?

How successful is this church?

How good is this life?

Those questions feel like natural extensions of the same idea. But something subtle has changed.

When I measure the length of a board, I'm measuring a property I can define fairly precisely.

When I measure the productivity of a human being, I have a different problem.

There is no productivity ruler.

Before I can measure it, I have to decide what I mean by it. Then I have to find something observable that I believe represents it.

We've moved from measuring a property to measuring a **proxy**.

That doesn't mean we've stopped doing science. It doesn't mean the number is meaningless.

It means we've introduced another layer between the number and the reality we're trying to understand.

And every layer introduces assumptions.

The magic still works.

We just have to start paying much closer attention to the trick.

## From Measuring Things to Measuring Systems

Measuring the length of a board is relatively easy. The board doesn't know you're measuring it. This turns out to be a significant advantage.

Lay down a tape measure and the board doesn't become anxious about its performance. It doesn't stretch itself an extra half inch because annual reviews are coming up. It doesn't ask the board next to it what length management is looking for this quarter.

It just sits there.

Many of the measurements that built our confidence in numbers work something like this. We're measuring a reasonably well-defined property of something that doesn't particularly care about the result.

A meter is a meter.

A kilogram is a kilogram.

A second is a second.

The real world makes even these measurements more complicated than they first appear, of course. Temperature changes dimensions. Instruments have tolerances. Conditions matter. Scientists and engineers spend enormous amounts of effort defining precisely what their measurements mean.

But there's still something relatively stable underneath them.

Then we begin measuring systems.

A system is different because its behavior emerges from relationships among many things changing at once.

Consider health. What does it mean to be healthy?

Suddenly one number won't do.

We can measure weight. Blood pressure. Resting heart rate. Cholesterol. Blood glucose. Body composition. Strength. Cardiovascular fitness. Sleep.

Each tells us something.

None is health.

A person can improve one while another gets worse. A medication can improve a laboratory value while causing side effects that make the person miserable. Someone can fall comfortably within a population average while knowing that something in their body doesn't feel right.

The measurement problem has changed. We are no longer asking, "How long is this board?" We're asking, "How well is this enormously complicated biological system functioning?"

Organizations make the problem even harder.

A company is a system made out of departments, processes, technologies, incentives, markets, customers, managers, and employees, all interacting with one another.

And unlike the board, the people know they're being measured.

We'll come back to that.

But even before measurement changes anyone's behavior, we encounter a more basic problem:

**What exactly are we measuring?**

Suppose I have ten software engineers. It is enormously tempting to think of those ten engineers as ten units of engineering capacity.

If one engineer can produce X amount of work, ten engineers should produce 10X.

Add another engineer.

Now we have 11X.

Look at that.

Number go up.

Anyone who has worked on a sufficiently complicated software project is already smiling.

Human beings do not work like that.

One engineer may know a particular part of the system so well that she can solve in an afternoon what would take someone else a week.

Another may write code slowly but have an uncanny ability to recognize architectural problems before they become expensive.

Another may be an extraordinary debugger.

Another may make everyone around him better because he explains difficult concepts clearly and generously reviews other people's work.

Another may produce an astonishing amount of code while creating enough chaos to consume the time of three other engineers.

How many units of productivity is each one? There is no obvious answer.

And even if we somehow assigned each engineer a productivity coefficient—which is exactly the kind of sentence that makes me nervous—the coefficients wouldn't remain stable.

Put the same engineer on a different project and her productivity changes.

Give her a good manager and it changes.

Give her a bad manager and it changes.

Pair her with someone she works well with.

Change the requirements.

Interrupt her every twenty minutes.

Give her six hours of uninterrupted concentration.

Ask her to solve a problem she has seen twenty times before.

Ask her to invent something nobody knows how to build.

Same human. Different system. Different result.

This is one of the great difficulties of measuring complex systems: **the properties we care about often emerge from interactions rather than residing neatly inside individual components.**

A basketball team isn't simply the sum of five individual player ratings.

A classroom isn't simply the sum of thirty test scores.

A marriage isn't two happiness scores added together.

An engineering organization isn't a bucket containing a certain number of productivity units.

The relationships matter.

The environment matters.

The history matters.

The task matters.

The interactions matter.

And all of them are changing.

This doesn't make systems impossible to measure. Quite the opposite. Measurement is often the only practical way to understand what a complicated system is doing. But it changes what we should expect from the measurement.

If I'm measuring the length of a board, I can reasonably expect the number to tell me the length of the board.

If I'm measuring the productivity of an engineering organization, I should be considerably more humble.

I probably need multiple measurements.

I need context.

I need trends over time.

I need to understand the conditions under which the measurements were taken.

And I may need something that doesn't look like a number at all: the observations of people who actually live inside the system.

This is where our cultural faith in quantification can get us into trouble. The precision of the number can remain exactly the same while the certainty of its meaning decreases dramatically.

I can tell you that a board is 72.4 inches long.

I can tell you that an engineer completed 37 story points.

Both statements contain numbers. Both may be perfectly accurate. They do not carry the same kind of knowledge.

The first describes a property fairly directly. The second describes an activity inside a complicated human system and asks us to infer something larger from it.

Maybe productivity.

Maybe progress.

Maybe value.

To get from 37 story points to any of those conclusions, we need interpretation.

And that's the point where another idea becomes essential.

When we cannot measure the thing we actually care about directly, we look for something we can measure that we believe tells us something about it.

We create a proxy.

## The Proxy

When we can't measure the thing we care about directly, we do something remarkably useful.

We measure something else.

That sounds suspicious when I put it that way, but much of modern measurement works precisely like this.

Suppose I want to know whether I'm healthy.

There is no health meter.

I cannot walk into my doctor's office, place my finger on a machine, and receive a reading:

**HEALTH: 83.7**

Pretty good.

Instead, we measure things associated with health.

Blood pressure.

Blood glucose.

Cholesterol.

Weight.

Heart rate.

Oxygen saturation.

Each of these is observable. Each can be measured reasonably consistently. And each tells us something about a larger reality that we cannot measure directly.

That's a proxy.

A proxy is something measurable that we use as an indicator of something else we care about. And proxies are extraordinarily useful.

Without them, we'd be forced to navigate complicated systems largely through intuition and anecdote. We'd know how things seemed. We'd know what happened to the person standing in front of us. We'd know what we remembered happening last year.

Proxies allow us to step outside that limited perspective.

If blood pressure across a population is trending upward, that's worth knowing.

If students consistently struggle with a particular category of question, that's worth knowing.

If software failures increase dramatically after a release, that's worth knowing.

If attendance at a program declines by half over six months, that's worth knowing.

The proxy gives us a signal.

But every signal comes with a tradeoff.

The reason a proxy is useful is also the reason it is dangerous:

**It simplifies.**

Reality contains too much information.

Imagine trying to create a complete measurement of my health. Not just my weight and blood pressure. Everything.

Every cell.

Every hormone.

Every organ.

Every genetic expression.

Every meal.

Every movement.

Every hour of sleep.

Every environmental exposure.

Every emotional stressor.

Every relationship affecting my well-being.

Every change occurring inside my body from one second to the next.

At some point, we've stopped creating a useful health assessment and started trying to create another Michael.

The complexity is the problem. So we compress. We select a few things that we believe tell us something important about the whole.

That is the bargain.

**We sacrifice completeness in exchange for visibility.**

And usually that's a very good bargain.

My doctor doesn't need to know everything happening inside my body to notice that my blood pressure is dangerously high. A software manager doesn't need to read every line of code written by every engineer to notice that defect rates have doubled. A school doesn't need to know every thought inside every student's head to notice that a large group is struggling with fractions.

The proxy allows us to see patterns that would otherwise disappear inside the complexity. But we have to remember what we traded away.

Context.

Detail.

Relationships.

Exceptions.

Sometimes causation.

Often meaning.

The proxy gives us a smaller picture because a smaller picture is something we can actually use. This is why I don't think the right question is whether a proxy is *good* or *bad*.

A better question is:

**Good for what?**

Weight is a useful proxy for some aspects of health. It is a terrible proxy for whether I made good choices yesterday.

Lines of code can tell me something about the amount of code being produced. They tell me very little about whether that code should have been written.

Church attendance can tell me how many people showed up. It cannot tell me whether anyone was transformed.

A standardized test can tell us something about what a student can demonstrate under the conditions of that test. It cannot contain the entirety of that student's intelligence, curiosity, creativity, or potential.

Problems begin when we ask a proxy to carry more meaning than it can support. And there is a powerful temptation to do exactly that. Because once something becomes a number, it becomes easy to compare.

This student scored 92. That student scored 84.

This engineer closed 17 bugs. That engineer closed 11.

This church grew 14 percent. That church shrank 3 percent.

This employee's performance score is 4.2. That employee's is 3.7.

The complexity disappears.

Now we can sort.

Rank.

Graph.

Reward.

Punish.

Allocate.

Decide.

That efficiency is enormously attractive, especially when the system is large.

Imagine trying to run an organization with fifty thousand employees entirely through deep personal knowledge of every individual. You can't. At some scale, abstraction isn't optional.

You need proxies.

But scale creates distance, and distance makes it easier to forget what the proxy leaves out.

The person closest to the work sees a complicated reality.

The person several layers removed may see a number.

Neither perspective is automatically superior.

The person close to the work can be biased. They can miss larger patterns. They can rationalize poor performance. They may see their own situation clearly while having no idea what is happening across the rest of the organization.

The person looking at the data can see patterns invisible from the ground. That's the gift of abstraction.

But they can also see a clean line where the underlying reality is anything but clean.

That's the cost.

This is why proxies work best when we hold them lightly.

Not dismissively.

Lightly.

We let them tell us what they know. We resist the temptation to let them tell us what they don't.

A good proxy should make us more curious about the system it represents, not less.

If the number changes, we ask why.

If two measurements disagree, we investigate.

If the people closest to the system consistently say the metric doesn't match their experience, we don't automatically assume either the people or the metric is wrong.

We look deeper.

Because the purpose of the proxy was never to replace reality. It was to help us see it. The moment we forget that distinction, something subtle happens.

The number stops being a window.

It becomes the view.

## The Map Is Useful Because It Isn’t the Territory

There is an old idea that the map is not the territory.

It seems obvious.

If I unfold a map of California on my kitchen table, nobody is going to mistake it for California. There are no redwoods growing out of Sacramento. I cannot drive my car down Interstate 5 between the salt and pepper shakers.

The map represents the territory.

And what makes the map useful is precisely what it leaves out.

Imagine a map that included everything.

Every road. Every building. Every tree. Every crack in every sidewalk. Every parked car. Every person. Every blade of grass.

Now keep it continuously updated.

Congratulations.

You have created California.

The usefulness of a map comes from compression.

A road map leaves out almost everything about the landscape so I can see the roads.

A topographical map leaves out different information so I can see elevation.

A weather map shows something else.

None is the complete truth about California. All can be true. And each becomes useful when I understand the question it was designed to answer.

Metrics work the same way.

A dashboard is a map of a system. It deliberately strips away enormous amounts of detail so someone can see patterns that would otherwise be invisible.

This is not a defect.

Imagine the CEO of a company with fifty thousand employees trying to understand the organization entirely from ground level.

She could spend an afternoon sitting beside one engineer.

That experience might be extraordinarily valuable. She might learn things no dashboard could ever tell her.

She would also know almost nothing about what the other 49,999 employees did that day.

Move upward and the perspective changes.

Individual stories become team summaries.

Team summaries become organizational metrics.

Organizational metrics become charts.

Eventually an enormous, messy collection of human activity may become a handful of lines on a screen.

Something has been lost.

Something has also been gained.

From ground level, I can see texture. I can understand why a particular engineer struggled with a task. I can see the architectural problem that consumed three days without producing much code. I can hear the frustration in a meeting. I can understand the context surrounding a decision.

But I can't easily see the whole system.

From the dashboard, I may be able to see that defect rates have risen across twelve teams for three consecutive quarters.

The individual engineer may have no idea that's happening.

The abstraction reveals a pattern.

Neither view is the real view. They're different maps.

This matters because discussions about metrics can easily turn into a contest between numbers and experience.

The dashboard says things are improving. The people doing the work say things are getting worse.

Choose your side.

But perhaps that's the wrong response. The disagreement may be telling us something.

Maybe the dashboard is capturing a genuine improvement that isn't obvious from one person's position.

Maybe the ground-level experience is revealing a deterioration the dashboard wasn't designed to see.

Maybe both are true.

Customer tickets could be closing faster while customers are becoming more frustrated.

Software releases could be happening more frequently while technical debt accumulates.

Employees could be producing more while becoming exhausted.

A system can improve along one dimension while deteriorating along another.

The map isn't necessarily wrong. It may simply be showing roads when what we need to understand is elevation. That's why good judgment requires moving between levels.

Zoom out.

Look for patterns.

Zoom in.

Look for context.

Then zoom out again.

The danger comes when we become trapped at either level.

Ground-level experience without abstraction can become anecdote. We assume what happened to us is what is happening everywhere.

Abstraction without ground-level experience can become fantasy. The dashboard looks healthy while the people living underneath it know the system is breaking.

Wisdom requires both.

The people closest to reality need the humility to recognize that their experience is incomplete. The people looking at the numbers need the humility to recognize the same thing.

A metric is a map.

Use the map.

Just remember to occasionally look out the window.

## Numbers Feel More Certain Than They Are

There is something psychologically comforting about a precise number. Suppose I tell you, “Our engineering organization seems to be getting more productive.” That sounds tentative. You might reasonably ask how I know.

Now suppose I tell you, “Engineering productivity increased 7.3 percent this quarter.” That sounds different. I have a decimal point. Clearly I know things. Somewhere, presumably, there is a spreadsheet—perhaps several spreadsheets—and maybe even a dashboard with a green arrow pointing upward.

We have entered the realm of facts.

Except we haven’t necessarily learned anything more about engineering productivity. Maybe we learned that whatever we decided to measure increased by 7.3 percent. Those are not always the same thing.

This is more than a problem with statistics. It touches something deeper in us, because human beings don’t particularly enjoy uncertainty. We like knowing where we are. We like knowing whether things are getting better or worse, whether we’re safe, whether we’re succeeding, whether the plan is working, and whether we’re making progress.

Uncertainty denies us those answers. A number seems to offer them.

My weight is 247.6 pounds.

Revenue increased 4.2 percent.

Customer satisfaction is 8.7.

The project is 73 percent complete. 

Suddenly something complicated has boundaries. We can name it, and once we can name it, we feel as though we can manage it.

There is a psychological progression at work here:

**Measurement creates precision.**

**Precision creates the feeling of certainty.**

**Certainty creates the feeling of control.**

** And control creates emotional security.**

The important words in that sentence are *the feeling of*.

Sometimes the control is real. If I’m driving and my speedometer says I’m going seventy-eight miles per hour, I have useful information about something I can control. I can take my foot off the accelerator. Measurement has reduced uncertainty and given me a meaningful way to act.

Complex systems don’t always cooperate so nicely.

Consider a project that is reported as 73 percent complete. What does that mean? Maybe we’ve completed 73 percent of the planned tasks. Perhaps we’ve spent 73 percent of the budget. Maybe someone asked twelve project leads for estimates, combined them according to a formula, and produced a number with sufficient mathematical dignity to survive the executive review.

Whatever the method, 73 percent feels reassuringly specific. But the hardest problem in the project might still be unsolved. The remaining 27 percent could take twice as long as everything that came before it. A requirement could change tomorrow, or a critical dependency could fail.

We have measured something. We may even have measured it accurately. But the precision of the measurement can conceal the uncertainty of the system.

This is one of the strange things numbers do to the human mind:

**Precision in measurement does not guarantee precision in meaning.**

If I measure a proxy very carefully, I get a very accurate measurement of the proxy. That’s all. Additional decimal places don’t somehow close the distance between the proxy and the thing it represents.

Imagine a company develops an employee engagement score. They survey thousands of employees, carefully normalize the responses, adjust for department size, apply a statistically respectable methodology, and announce that employee engagement is 76.4. That number may be based on excellent work.

But what exactly does 76.4 units of engagement feel like?

Nobody knows. The precision belongs to the calculation; the underlying human reality remains complicated.

This distinction is easy to lose because numbers look so wonderfully solid. Human experience, by contrast, is messy. Someone says, “Morale feels low.” Someone else says, “My team seems fine.” A manager says, “I’m hearing concerns.” None of this fits neatly into a quarterly presentation.

Then the survey arrives: **ENGAGEMENT: 76.4.** Finally, something we can work with.

And perhaps we can. The survey may contain genuinely useful information. But there is an emotional temptation hiding inside the relief: the number doesn’t merely give us information. It gives us the sensation that the uncertainty has been contained.

We know where we are now. We have a baseline and a target. We can create initiatives, assign owners, and track progress. Next quarter we’ll get another number, and if it goes up, we’ll know the initiatives worked.

Probably.

This is how measurement can become a form of emotional reassurance, and not because anyone involved is foolish.

Managing complex systems is genuinely frightening. Leaders are asked to make decisions about organizations they cannot possibly understand in full. 

Doctors make decisions about bodies containing trillions of cells.

Parents make decisions about children whose inner worlds they can never completely know. 

We make decisions about our own lives without knowing what tomorrow will bring.

Numbers give uncertainty edges. Sometimes those edges correspond beautifully to reality. Sometimes we draw them ourselves.

There is another reason precision feels like control. Once something has a number, we can create a target.

If engagement is 76.4, perhaps we can make it 80. If productivity increased 7.3 percent, perhaps next year it can increase 10 percent.

If I weigh 247.6 pounds, perhaps I can reach 245 by Friday.

The number gives desire a destination, and destinations are comforting. But reality does not become controllable simply because Excel allows us to enter a target.

This is especially important in systems involving human beings. We can influence them. We can create conditions, change incentives, learn from feedback, and make thoughtful decisions. But control is often much more limited than the dashboard suggests.

This doesn’t mean we should abandon targets or stop measuring progress. It means we should remain aware of what the number is doing both **informationally and emotionally**. What does this measurement actually tell me? And what is it making me feel that I know?

Those can be very different questions.

Numbers can reduce uncertainty, but they can also disguise it. They can give us genuine control, but they can also give us the comfort of control. The mature response isn’t to distrust precision; it’s to refuse to confuse precision with certainty.

And sometimes the most accurate statement we can make about a system is simply: **we don’t know yet.**

No decimal point required.

## When the Proxy Becomes Reality

Most proxies begin their lives with appropriate humility.

Someone wants to understand engineering productivity, so they start tracking completed work. Or they want to understand customer satisfaction, so they create a survey. A school wants to know whether students are learning, so it gives them a test.

At first, everyone understands the arrangement.

The number is an indicator.

It tells us something about the thing we care about.

Then something subtle happens.

Consider software bugs. A manager wants some visibility into the work happening across an engineering organization, so the team begins tracking the number of bugs engineers close. This isn't necessarily a bad idea. Bugs are real. Closing them is useful. A sudden change in the number might reveal something worth investigating.

The original claim is modest:

**Bugs closed gives us one indication of engineering activity.**

Over time, however, the language can shift.

**Bugs closed tells us how productive the team is.**

And eventually:

**This engineer closed fewer bugs, so this engineer is less productive.**

Notice what happened.

The number didn't change.

Its authority did.

We moved from *this tells us something* to *this tells us the thing*.

That transition is easy to miss because it usually happens gradually. Nobody schedules a meeting titled **“Today We Will Confuse Our Proxy with Reality.”** There is no ceremonial handing over of authority.

Instead, the metric gets used.

It appears in a report. Then a dashboard. Someone asks about it in a quarterly review. Leaders begin comparing teams. Targets are established. Performance conversations reference the number. Eventually people who weren't around when the metric was created encounter it without knowing why it was chosen in the first place.

The assumption is simply built into the system.

This is productivity.

This is engagement.

This is quality.

This is learning.

This is success.

The noun changes.

We stop saying that the metric *indicates* productivity and begin calling the metric *productivity*. We stop saying the test provides evidence about learning and start calling the score *student achievement*. We stop saying attendance tells us something about participation and begin talking about attendance as though it were the health of the organization itself.

Language quietly erases the distance between the map and the territory.

Once that happens, the things the metric leaves out become harder to see.

Imagine two engineers.

One closes twenty bugs this month. The other closes eight.

If bugs closed equals productivity, the conclusion is easy. Twenty is greater than eight. We have found our more productive engineer.

But perhaps the engineer who closed eight bugs spent much of the month investigating a difficult architectural problem that was causing bugs across the entire product. Maybe she eventually found the underlying defect and prevented fifty future bugs from being created.

The dashboard still says eight.

The number isn't wrong. That's what makes this difficult. She closed eight bugs.

The mistake occurs when we ask the number a question it cannot answer.

**How much value did this person create?**

The metric never promised to know that. We decided that it did.

This is one of the reasons bad metrics can survive for so long. They are often not actually *bad data*. The number may be perfectly accurate. The problem is the story we attach to it.

Twenty bugs were closed.

Revenue increased 6 percent.

The student scored 82.

Attendance declined 10 percent.

The employee engagement survey came back at 76.4.

Those can all be true statements.

Trouble begins with the word **therefore**.

Attendance declined 10 percent, **therefore** the program is failing.

The student scored 82, **therefore** the student doesn't understand the material.

Bug closures increased, **therefore** engineering productivity improved.

Employee engagement increased, **therefore** morale is better.

Sometimes the conclusion is correct. Sometimes it isn't. The number alone cannot tell us, because the conclusion requires interpretation.

This is where the three perspectives we introduced earlier begin to matter.

The **measurer** looks at the metric and sees information. Maybe useful information. Maybe even very good information.

The **measuree** experiences something different. The moment that number appears in a performance review, school transcript, health app, ranking, or organizational dashboard, it begins to acquire personal significance.

And the **culture** watches what happens next.

Which numbers get discussed?

Which ones appear on the executive dashboard?

Which ones determine bonuses?

Which ones trigger uncomfortable meetings?

Which ones get celebrated when they go up?

Over time, the organization learns what matters by watching what receives attention.

A number that began as an attempt to understand reality can gradually become part of the reality itself.

We'll explore that much more in the next chapter, because something fascinating happens when human beings discover which numbers are being watched.

For now, the important point is simpler.

A proxy should always retain some distance from the thing it represents. That distance isn't a weakness we need to eliminate. It is information we need to remember.

Weight can tell me something about my body. It is not my health.

A test can tell us something about learning. It is not learning.

Bug counts can tell us something about engineering activity. They are not productivity.

Attendance can tell us something about participation. It is not community, transformation, belonging, or meaning.

The moment we collapse those distinctions, the proxy gains an authority it was never designed to carry. We no longer look through the number at reality.

We look at the number and call it reality.
