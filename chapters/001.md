# 1: What Gets Measured Gets Managed

### The World Becomes Measurable

Human beings have been measuring things for a very long time.

We measured land because someone needed to know where one field ended and another began. We measured grain because someone needed to know how much was stored. We measured time because planting crops becomes considerably easier when everyone has some idea what season it is.

Measurement helped turn an unpredictable world into something we could describe, compare, and eventually control.

Then science came along and made measurement central to how we decide what is true.

That shift is difficult to overstate. Instead of relying primarily on authority, intuition, tradition, or observation alone, we began asking questions that could be tested against the physical world. How far? How fast? How heavy? How hot?

The answers gave us something enormously valuable: a shared reference point.

Imagine two people standing in a room.

"It's cold in here."

"No, it isn't."

This argument can continue indefinitely because both people may be accurately describing their experience. One is cold. The other isn't.

Then someone looks at the thermometer.

Seventy-two degrees.

The thermometer does not tell us whether either person should feel cold. It tells us something different: the temperature of the room.

That distinction is extraordinarily useful. We have separated a property of the environment from our individual experience of it. Now we have something we can compare across rooms, across days, and across people.

Measurement gives reality a way to talk back.

That is one reason numbers became so powerful. Human perception is inconsistent. Memory is selective. Intuition is vulnerable to bias. We notice some things and miss others, and we are remarkably good at constructing explanations after the fact.

Numbers can interrupt that.

As a software engineer, I've experienced this countless times. I make a change and I'm convinced the system is faster. The code is cleaner. The algorithm is better. I can practically feel the performance improvement radiating from the screen.

Then we measure it.

Eight hundred milliseconds.

Exactly what it was before.

Well.

That's disappointing.

But it's also useful. Reality has disagreed with me, and reality gets a vote.

We trust measurement because experiences like this happen constantly, and because the larger results are difficult to argue with. Bridges stay up because engineers measure loads and materials. Medicines are tested because feeling like a treatment should work is not enough. When I get on an airplane, I very much want someone to have measured the wing.

Numbers earned our trust.

They earned it by helping us predict things, build things, test things, and discover when we were wrong. Modern life would be impossible without them.

Which makes it easy to carry that trust into places where the relationship between the number and reality becomes more complicated.

Measuring the length of a board is one thing.

Measuring the productivity of a software engineer is another.

### From Things to Systems

Suppose I hand you a board and ask you to measure its length.

This is not a particularly difficult philosophical problem.

You put a tape measure against it. Maybe you measure twice if the board is important. Assuming the tape is accurate and you know how to use it, we can be reasonably confident that the resulting number represents something quite specific about the board.

Now suppose I ask you to measure the board's productivity.

We have a problem.

Productivity is not a property sitting inside the wood waiting for an instrument. We first have to decide what we mean.

The same difficulty appears as the things we measure become more complex.

Consider health.

We can measure blood pressure, weight, cholesterol, heart rate, blood glucose, sleep, and dozens of other things. Each can tell us something meaningful. But if I ask you for my *health*, there is no single instrument you can attach to me that produces the answer.

Organizations have the same problem.

A company wants to know whether a software team is productive. That's a reasonable thing to care about. Engineers are expensive, projects matter, customers are waiting, and managers are responsible for knowing whether work is moving.

But what exactly should they measure?

Lines of code?

Bugs closed?

Features completed?

Schedule performance?

Each seems plausible because each has some relationship to productive work. The difficulty is that none of them *is* productivity.

One engineer may write relatively little code but prevent an architectural mistake that would have consumed months. Another may generate enormous amounts of visible output while quietly creating work for everyone around them.

From close to the work, those differences can be obvious.

From several levels up, they may be almost invisible.

This is not because management is foolish. It's because organizations create a problem of scale. A manager cannot personally inspect every line of code, sit in every design conversation, watch every debugging session, and somehow still have time to manage.

The larger the system becomes, the more abstraction becomes necessary.

So we look for things we can see.

This is where the proxy enters.

### The Proxy

A proxy is something measurable that we use as an indicator of something else we care about.

We use proxies constantly.

If I'm trying to understand my health, I might look at my blood pressure. Blood pressure is not health, but it tells me something useful about it.

If I'm trying to understand whether a product is succeeding, I might look at sales. Sales are not customer value, but they tell me something about whether people are willing to pay for what we've built.

If I'm trying to understand software quality, I might look at defect rates. Bugs are not quality, but they give me information about it.

Proxies are not a compromise we reluctantly tolerate because our measurement systems aren't sophisticated enough. They are often the only practical way to understand complex realities.

Imagine trying to create a complete measurement of my health.

We would need my genes, blood chemistry, organs, diet, sleep, movement, stress, environment, medical history, relationships, and whatever several trillion cells happen to be doing at the moment.

At some point we've stopped creating a health assessment and started trying to create another Michael.

So we simplify.

We choose a handful of measurements that give us useful information about the larger reality.

**We sacrifice completeness in exchange for visibility.**

That's not a flaw. It's the bargain.

The important question is whether we remember that we made it.

Suppose my blood pressure is excellent. That's good news. It tells us something meaningful.

Does it mean I'm healthy?

Not necessarily.

Maybe my cholesterol is terrible. Maybe I haven't slept properly in six months. Maybe I'm carrying significantly more weight than is good for me. Maybe everything else looks wonderful.

The blood-pressure reading hasn't failed. It answered the question it was capable of answering.

The mistake would be asking it to answer a larger one.

This is why one of the most useful questions we can ask of any metric is:

**Good for what?**

Is weight a good metric?

Good for what?

Is bug count a good metric?

Good for what?

Is revenue a good metric?

Good for what?

Is AI usage a good metric?

Good for what?

There is no universally good metric because metrics are instruments. Their usefulness depends on the question we're asking.

A thermometer is an excellent instrument for measuring temperature and a terrible instrument for measuring friendship. Nobody finds this surprising because we understand the thermometer's domain.

Things get murkier when the relationship between the measurement and the larger concept feels intuitive.

More bugs closed sounds like more productivity.

Higher attendance sounds like a healthier organization.

More AI usage sounds like successful AI adoption.

Lower weight sounds like better health.

Each may be true.

The problem is the distance hidden inside the word *sounds*.

A proxy gives us evidence about something larger. Evidence is not the same thing as identity.

That distinction matters because once we forget it, we begin giving the proxy responsibilities it was never designed to carry.

### The Map Is Not the Territory

A useful way to think about a metric is as a map.

Maps are astonishingly helpful precisely because they leave things out.

Imagine asking for a map of California that contains every road, building, tree, person, crack in the pavement, blade of grass, electrical wire, and current weather condition at full scale and perfect resolution.

Congratulations.

You have created California.

The usefulness of the map comes from compression. A highway map removes nearly everything about the state so you can answer a particular question: *How do I get from here to there?*

A topographic map removes different things because it answers a different question. A weather map removes still others.

None is the complete California.

All can be accurate.

Metrics work the same way.

A dashboard might tell a manager that bug-closure time increased from twelve days to eighteen. That is useful information. It may reveal a real change in the system that deserves attention.

What the dashboard cannot show by itself is everything that produced the change.

Maybe the team encountered a cluster of unusually difficult bugs. Maybe a key engineer was pulled onto another project. Maybe the product entered a more complex phase of development. Maybe the team is simply working less effectively than it was before.

The number doesn't know.

It gives us a compressed representation of what happened. To understand why it happened, we often have to return to the territory.

This creates a tension between two perspectives.

The person close to the work sees details that disappear in aggregation. They know which bug required three weeks of investigation, which customer problem looked trivial until someone reproduced it, and which design decision prevented a much larger failure.

But proximity has limits too.

The person closest to the work may not see patterns across ten teams. They may not know that similar problems are appearing elsewhere. They may have their own biases, blind spots, and perfectly human ability to explain away inconvenient evidence.

The manager looking at the dashboard may see a pattern the individual engineer cannot.

Neither view automatically wins.

The map can reveal patterns that are difficult to see while standing on the ground. The ground can reveal realities the map had to remove.

Wisdom requires moving between them.

This becomes especially important because numbers have a psychological advantage over the messy realities they represent.

They look certain.

Suppose someone tells you:

> Engineering productivity increased 7.3 percent this quarter.

That sounds impressive.

Not necessarily the productivity increase. The *7.3*.

It has a decimal point.

Decimal points have authority.

If someone told us productivity "seems somewhat better," we would immediately ask questions. Based on what? How much better? According to whom?

But 7.3 percent arrives already dressed for the meeting.

The number may be perfectly legitimate. Perhaps the organization has carefully defined its productivity measure, collected high-quality data, and calculated the change correctly.

But notice how easily precision in the calculation becomes confidence in the concept.

The same thing happens when a project is reported as 73 percent complete.

What does that mean?

Maybe 73 percent of the planned tasks are finished. That's measurable. But what if the remaining 27 percent contains the hardest technical problem? What if the tasks were estimated poorly? What if we discover next week that something we thought was complete needs to be redesigned?

The project may genuinely be 73 percent complete according to the chosen method.

The question is what we believe that number entitles us to conclude.

Human beings do not particularly enjoy uncertainty. We like to know where things stand. Numbers give uncertainty edges. They allow us to put a value in a cell, assign it a color, and compare it with last quarter.

Sometimes those edges correspond beautifully to reality.

Sometimes we drew them ourselves.

**Precision in measurement is not precision in meaning.**

This does not make precise measurements suspicious. It means we need to distinguish between confidence in the measurement and confidence in what the measurement represents.

I can be extremely confident that an engineer closed twenty bugs.

I should be more cautious about what those twenty bugs tell me about the engineer's productivity.

And more cautious still about what they tell me about the engineer.

The farther the conclusion travels from the thing directly measured, the more judgment enters the picture.

### Look Through the Number

This is where proxies can quietly acquire more authority than we intended to give them.

Imagine two engineers.

One closes twenty bugs this quarter. The other closes ten.

Those numbers may be completely accurate.

Then the language begins to change.

The first engineer closed more bugs.

Therefore the first engineer was more productive.

Therefore the second engineer is underperforming.

Notice what happened.

The number didn't change.

Its authority did.

We moved from a directly observed fact to a claim about productivity and then to a judgment about a person. Each step may be reasonable. Each also requires information the original number did not contain.

**Trouble begins with the word therefore.**

The word itself isn't the problem, of course. We have to draw conclusions from evidence. That's one of the reasons we measure things.

The problem is forgetting that a conclusion has been drawn.

"Twenty bugs were closed" is a measurement.

"Therefore this engineer created more value" is an interpretation.

The interpretation may be right. It may even be obviously right once we understand the context.

But the number did not make the judgment.

We did.

That distinction becomes particularly important when a metric disagrees with lived experience.

Suppose customer complaints increase 18 percent.

One response is to treat the number as the conclusion: customer satisfaction is getting worse, someone needs to fix it.

Another response is to dismiss the number because the people closest to customers insist everything feels fine.

Neither response is especially wise.

The increase tells us something happened.

Go look.

Maybe customers really are less satisfied. Maybe the product has become harder to use. Maybe a new release introduced a problem. Or perhaps the company made it much easier for customers to report issues, and the increase is partly evidence that the feedback system is working.

The metric has done its job.

It got our attention.

A good number should often make us more curious, not less.

What changed?

Why?

What does the person closest to the work see?

What does the aggregate data see that the individual person might not?

What other evidence would help us understand what is happening?

These questions put the number back in its proper relationship with reality.

The number becomes a place to begin rather than a place to stop.

This is why the question from the Introduction matters so much:

**What's the point?**

If the point of tracking customer complaints is to understand customer experience, then the complaint count serves that purpose. If the count stops helping us understand the experience, we change how we interpret it, supplement it with other information, or perhaps choose a different measure.

The point stays upstream.

The same is true for engineering productivity, health, AI adoption, attendance, financial performance, and nearly everything else we'll discuss in this book.

First, something matters.

Then we find a way to measure it.

Then we use judgment to decide what the measurement is telling us.

The purpose of measurement is not to eliminate judgment. It is to give judgment better information.

That requires a little humility from everyone involved.

The person looking at the dashboard has to remember that the clean number is a compressed view of a messier reality. The person living inside that reality has to remember that proximity does not make them omniscient. Sometimes the dashboard sees something they don't.

When the two disagree, the disagreement itself is information.

What does the number see that I don't?

What do I see that the number doesn't?

Those are much more useful questions than deciding in advance which perspective deserves to win.

The map is not the territory.

But that doesn't make the map useless.

Quite the opposite.

A good map helps us navigate reality precisely because it simplifies it. A good metric can do the same. It can reveal patterns, challenge assumptions, direct attention, and help us decide where to look more closely.

We just have to remember to look through the number rather than stop at it.

So far, though, we've been assuming something rather convenient.

We've been assuming that the thing we're measuring doesn't change simply because we're measuring it.

That works pretty well for boards.

Human beings are another matter.
