# 1 — What Gets Measured Gets Managed

### The World Becomes Measurable

Human beings have been measuring things for a very long time. We measured land because someone needed to know where one field ended and another began, grain because someone needed to know how much was stored, and time because planting crops becomes considerably easier when everyone has some idea what season it is. Measurement gave us a way to take an unpredictable world and describe it in terms we could compare, communicate, and eventually use to make decisions.

Science took this much further by making measurement central to how we decide what is true. Instead of relying primarily on authority, tradition, intuition, or observation alone, we learned to ask questions that could be tested against the physical world. How far? How fast? How heavy? How hot? The answers gave us something enormously valuable: a shared reference point that didn't depend entirely on who happened to be doing the observing.

Imagine two people standing in the same room, one complaining that it's cold and the other insisting that it isn't. They can continue this argument indefinitely, and the interesting thing is that neither one necessarily has to be wrong; one person may genuinely feel cold while the other feels perfectly comfortable. Then someone looks at the thermometer and sees that the room is seventy-two degrees.

The thermometer hasn't settled the argument about how either person feels, because that isn't the question it knows how to answer. What it has given us is something different: a measurement of the temperature of the room. We have separated a property of the environment from our individual experience of it, which means we can now compare this room with another room, today with yesterday, and perhaps our own perceptions with something outside ourselves.

This is one of measurement's great gifts. It gives reality a way to talk back.

As a software engineer, I've experienced this more times than I care to admit. I'll make a change to a piece of software and become convinced that I've made it faster; the code looks cleaner, the algorithm seems better, and I can practically feel the performance improvement radiating from the screen. Then we measure it and discover that the operation still takes eight hundred milliseconds, exactly what it took before I performed my act of engineering brilliance.

That's disappointing, naturally, but it's also useful. Whatever story I had been telling myself about the improvement now has to contend with something outside the story. Reality has disagreed with me, and reality gets a vote.

We trust measurement because experiences like this happen constantly, and because the larger results are difficult to argue with. Bridges stay up because engineers measure loads and materials, medicines are tested because feeling that a treatment *should* work isn't enough, and when I get on an airplane I very much want someone to have measured the wing. Modern life depends on our ability to observe the world carefully enough that our assumptions can be corrected by it.

Numbers earned our trust this way. They helped us predict things, build things, test things, and discover when we were wrong, so it makes perfect sense that we learned to take them seriously. The difficulty comes when we carry that well-earned trust into places where the relationship between the number and the thing we care about is much less direct.

Measuring the length of a board is one thing. Measuring the productivity of a software engineer turns out to be considerably more interesting.

### From Things to Systems

Suppose I hand you a board and ask you to measure its length. This isn't a particularly difficult philosophical problem. You put a tape measure against it, perhaps measure twice if the board is important, and assuming the tape is accurate and you know how to use it, we can be reasonably confident that the resulting number describes something quite specific about the board.

Now suppose I hand you the same board and ask you to measure its productivity.

The problem isn't that we need a more sophisticated tape measure. Productivity isn't a property sitting somewhere inside the wood waiting for the right instrument to discover it; before we can measure anything, we have to decide what we mean by the word. As the things we care about become more complicated, this problem appears more and more often.

Consider health. We can measure blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, blood glucose, heart rate, sleep, and dozens of other things, and each of those measurements can tell us something useful. But if I walk into a doctor's office and ask for a measurement of *my health*, there is no single instrument the doctor can attach to me that produces the answer.

Organizations run into the same problem. A company may quite reasonably want to know whether a software team is productive: engineers are expensive, projects matter, customers are waiting, and managers have a legitimate responsibility to understand whether work is moving. The problem comes when we try to decide what productivity looks like from far enough away that the work itself is no longer directly visible.

We could count lines of code, bugs closed, features completed, or how closely projects followed their schedules. Each of those has some plausible relationship with productive work, but none of them *is* productivity. An engineer might write relatively little code while catching an architectural mistake that would have cost the team months, while another might generate an impressive amount of visible output while quietly creating work for everyone around them.

From close to the work, the difference between those two engineers may be obvious. From several levels up in the organization, it may be almost invisible, and that isn't because management is foolish or doesn't understand engineering. Organizations create a problem of scale: a manager cannot inspect every line of code, sit in every design conversation, observe every debugging session, understand every customer interaction, and somehow find time left over to manage.

The larger the system becomes, the more abstraction becomes necessary. We need some way of compressing all that messy activity into something a person can actually see and reason about.

So we look for things we can measure.

And this is where the proxy enters.

### The Proxy

A proxy is simply something measurable that we use as an indicator of something larger that we care about, and we rely on them constantly. If I'm trying to understand my health, I might look at my blood pressure; if I'm trying to understand whether a product is succeeding, I might look at sales; if I'm trying to understand software quality, I might look at defect rates. None of these measurements contains the whole thing we're interested in, but each gives us information about it.

It's tempting to think of proxies as an unfortunate compromise, something we would no longer need if only our measurement systems became sophisticated enough. I don't think that's right. Complex realities contain too much information to represent completely, so some form of simplification isn't merely convenient; it's often the only practical way we can understand them at all.

Imagine, for example, that instead of using a handful of indicators to understand my health, we decided to create a truly comprehensive measurement. We would need my genes, blood chemistry, organs, diet, sleep, movement, stress, environment, medical history, relationships, and whatever several trillion cells happen to be doing at this particular moment. At some point we have stopped creating a health assessment and started trying to create another Michael, which seems like an unnecessarily expensive way to prepare for my annual physical.

So we simplify. We choose a handful of measurements that give us useful visibility into a much larger reality, knowing—at least in principle—that information has been discarded in the process. We sacrifice completeness in exchange for visibility, and that isn't a defect in measurement so much as the bargain that makes measurement useful.

The important question is whether we remember that we made the bargain.

Suppose my blood pressure is excellent. That's genuinely good news, and I don't need to become suspicious of it simply because I've written a book about the limitations of metrics. But an excellent blood-pressure reading doesn't necessarily mean that I am healthy; my cholesterol might be terrible, I might not have slept properly in six months, or I might have other problems the cuff around my arm has absolutely no opinion about.

The blood-pressure reading hasn't failed us. It answered the question it was capable of answering. The mistake would be asking it to answer a larger question and then blaming the instrument when reality turns out to be more complicated.

This is why I find a simple question useful whenever I'm evaluating a metric: **Good for what?**

Weight can be a useful metric if I'm trying to understand something about body mass or a long-term trend in weight. Bug count can be useful if I'm trying to understand something about defects moving through a system. Revenue can tell me something important about the financial activity of a business, and AI usage can tell me whether people are actually using AI tools. None of those measurements is inherently good or bad; their usefulness depends on the question we expect them to answer.

A thermometer is an excellent instrument for measuring temperature and a terrible instrument for measuring friendship. Nobody finds this particularly confusing because we understand the thermometer's domain, but the distinction becomes murkier when the relationship between the thing we measure and the thing we care about feels intuitively obvious.

More bugs closed *sounds* like more productivity. Higher attendance *sounds* like a healthier organization. More AI usage *sounds* like successful AI adoption, just as lower weight *sounds* like better health. Any of those relationships may turn out to be true, but there is a distance hidden inside the word *sounds*, and that distance is where judgment begins to enter the picture.

A proxy gives us evidence about something larger. Once we forget that distinction, we begin asking the proxy to carry responsibilities it was never designed to carry.

### The Map Is Not the Territory

One of the most useful ways I've found to think about a metric is as a map. Maps are enormously useful, but their usefulness depends on something that initially sounds like a weakness: they leave almost everything out.

Imagine asking for a map of California that contains every road, building, tree, person, crack in the pavement, blade of grass, electrical wire, and current weather condition, all represented at full scale and perfect resolution. Congratulations. You have created California.

A highway map works because it removes nearly everything about the state so that one particular feature becomes easy to see. If I'm trying to get from Sacramento to San Diego, I don't need to know where every oak tree is along the way, while a person studying wildfire risk may care very much about things my highway map ignores. A topographic map removes one set of details, a weather map another, and each can be accurate without pretending to be the complete California.

Metrics work much the same way. A dashboard might tell a manager that average bug-closure time has increased from twelve days to eighteen, and that may be useful information about a real change in the system. What the dashboard cannot show by itself is everything that produced the change.

Perhaps the team encountered a cluster of unusually difficult bugs, a key engineer was pulled onto another project, or the product entered a phase of development where the remaining problems were simply harder. It's also possible that the team really is working less effectively than it was before. The number doesn't know which explanation is correct; it has compressed what happened into a form that allows us to notice that something changed.

To understand why it changed, we often have to return to the territory.

This creates an interesting tension between the person looking at the map and the person standing on the ground. Someone close to the work knows which bug required three weeks of investigation, which customer problem looked trivial until somebody finally reproduced it, and which design decision prevented a much larger failure that will never appear in the defect count because the defect never happened.

But proximity has limits of its own. The person closest to the work may not see a pattern occurring across ten teams, may not know that other groups are encountering the same problem, and is just as capable as anyone else of having biases or explaining away evidence that doesn't fit their expectations. A manager looking across the organization may see something in the aggregate that no individual engineer could see from inside one project.

Neither perspective automatically wins. The ground reveals details the map had to remove, while the map reveals patterns that can be nearly impossible to see while standing on the ground. Wisdom isn't choosing one and distrusting the other; it's learning when to move between them.

That movement becomes harder because numbers have a psychological advantage over the messy realities they represent: they look certain.

Suppose I tell you that engineering productivity increased 7.3 percent this quarter. There is something reassuringly substantial about that statement, and I'm not even talking about the productivity increase. I'm talking about the **7.3**.

It has a decimal point.

If I told you engineering productivity “seems somewhat better,” you would immediately start asking questions. Better according to whom? Based on what evidence? How much better? But 7.3 percent arrives already dressed for the meeting, carrying the quiet authority of a number that looks as though somebody must have done quite a lot of math to produce it.

Perhaps they did. The organization may have carefully defined its productivity measure, collected excellent data, and calculated the change with complete accuracy. The danger isn't that precision is fake; it's that precision in the calculation can quietly become confidence in the concept being calculated.

The same thing happens when somebody reports that a project is 73 percent complete. Perhaps 73 percent of the planned tasks are finished, which is a perfectly measurable statement, but the remaining 27 percent might contain the hardest technical problem in the entire project. The tasks might have been estimated badly, or next week we might discover that something we thought was finished has to be redesigned.

The project may genuinely be 73 percent complete according to the method we've chosen. The question is what that number entitles us to conclude.

Human beings don't particularly enjoy uncertainty. We like to know where things stand, and numbers give uncertainty edges: they let us put something in a cell, assign it a color, compare it with last quarter, and perhaps draw a little arrow beside it. Sometimes those edges correspond beautifully to reality, and sometimes we drew them ourselves.

This is why precision in measurement isn't necessarily precision in meaning. I can be extremely confident that an engineer closed twenty bugs and somewhat less confident about what those twenty bugs tell me about the engineer's productivity. I should become more cautious still before deciding what they tell me about the engineer as a person.

The farther our conclusion travels from the thing we directly measured, the more human judgment has entered the journey.

### Look Through the Number

Imagine two engineers. One closes twenty bugs during the quarter while the other closes ten, and there is nothing suspicious about either number; as far as the tracking system is concerned, that is simply what happened. But almost immediately, and often without anyone noticing the transition, we begin asking those numbers to tell us something they did not directly measure.

Twenty bugs becomes evidence that the first engineer was more productive. From there it is a short trip to wondering whether the second engineer is underperforming, and by the time that conclusion reaches a performance conversation, the original bug count may feel less like one piece of evidence and more like a verdict. The number itself never changed along the way. What changed was the amount of authority we gave it.

There is nothing inherently wrong with making that inference. Perhaps the first engineer really was more productive, and perhaps the difference in bug closures is meaningful evidence of that. But productivity wasn't what we measured; we measured bugs closed, and then human judgment carried us the rest of the distance.

That's the part of the process I want us to become better at noticing, because trouble often begins with the word *therefore*. Twenty bugs were closed; **therefore** this engineer created more value. The conclusion may be right, even obviously right once we understand the surrounding context, but the number did not make that judgment.

We did.

This distinction becomes particularly important when a metric disagrees with the experience of the people closest to whatever is being measured. Suppose customer complaints increase 18 percent. We could treat the number as the conclusion and announce that customer satisfaction is getting worse, or we could dismiss it because the people working with customers insist that everything feels fine; neither response strikes me as especially wise.

Something happened. That's what the number has told us, so now we go look.

Perhaps customers really are less satisfied because the product has become harder to use or a recent release introduced a problem. But perhaps the company recently made it much easier for customers to report issues, in which case some of the increase might actually be evidence that the feedback system is working better. The complaint count cannot settle the question by itself, but it has done something valuable: it has directed our attention toward a part of reality worth understanding.

A good metric should often make us more curious, not less. We can ask what changed, what the people closest to the situation are seeing, whether the aggregate data reveals something they might be missing, and what other evidence would help us understand what is happening. Those questions put the number back into a healthier relationship with the reality it represents.

The number becomes a place to begin rather than a place to stop.

This brings us back to the question from the Introduction that will keep appearing throughout this book: **What's the point?** If the reason we're tracking customer complaints is to understand the customer's experience, then the complaint count exists in service of that purpose. When it stops helping us understand the experience, we can change how we interpret it, supplement it with other information, or decide that another measure would serve us better.

The point stays upstream.

The same pattern applies to engineering productivity, health, AI adoption, attendance, financial performance, and nearly everything else we'll encounter in the chapters ahead. Something matters to us first; then we find a way to measure some aspect of it; then we use judgment to decide what that measurement is telling us. Measurement doesn't eliminate judgment. At its best, it gives judgment better information.

That requires some humility from both sides of the dashboard. The person looking at the clean number has to remember that it is a compressed view of a messier reality, while the person living inside that reality has to remember that proximity doesn't make them omniscient. Sometimes the dashboard really does reveal something that the people closest to the work haven't noticed.

When those perspectives disagree, the disagreement itself is useful information. Instead of deciding in advance whether the number or the lived experience deserves to win, we can ask what the number sees that we don't and what we see that the number doesn't. Often the most interesting part of the story is hiding in the distance between those answers.

The map isn't the territory, but that doesn't make the map useless. Quite the opposite: a good map helps us navigate reality precisely because it simplifies it, and a good metric can reveal patterns, challenge assumptions, direct attention, and help us decide where to look more closely. We simply have to remember to look *through* the number toward the reality it represents rather than allowing our attention to stop at the number itself.

So far, though, we've been assuming something rather convenient: that the thing we're measuring remains unchanged by the act of measuring it. That assumption works pretty well for boards.

Human beings are another matter.
