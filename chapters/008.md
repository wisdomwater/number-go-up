# Chapter 8 — When the Number Becomes Me

## The Grammar Changes

Something happens to a number when we repeat it often enough.

It stops describing something.

It starts describing us.

At first, the language is straightforward.

**I weigh 247 pounds.**

That's a measurement.

A scale collected some information about my body at a particular moment and reported it back to me.

Then the grammar changes.

**I am fat.**

Something just happened.

The number disappeared.

So did the scale.

A measurement became a description.

And the description became an identity.

We do this remarkably easily.

**I received a poor performance rating.**

Becomes:

**I'm a poor performer.**

**My book didn't sell very well.**

Becomes:

**I'm not a very good writer.**

**I made less money this year.**

Becomes:

**I'm falling behind.**

**My business failed.**

Becomes:

**I'm a failure.**

The movement is subtle enough that we may not notice it.

The number measures something I did.

Then the number measures something about me.

Then the number tells me what I am.

Those are three very different claims.

The first two may contain useful information.

The third deserves considerably more suspicion.

We've already seen this mistake throughout the book.

A company measures software defects.

Defects become quality.

A company measures AI usage.

Usage becomes adoption.

Adoption becomes progress.

A company measures tasks completed.

Tasks become productivity.

Each step compresses reality a little further.

Eventually the proxy and the thing it represents become almost indistinguishable in our language.

Now we're doing the same thing with a human being.

Except the territory is me.

That's why this particular collapse matters so much.

If we shouldn't expect a single metric to completely describe the health of an organization, why would we expect one to describe a person?

People are considerably messier than dashboards.

I have written good code and bad code.

I have made wise decisions and foolish ones.

I have been disciplined and undisciplined.

I have succeeded at things and failed spectacularly at others.

I have been generous and selfish, courageous and afraid, focused and distracted, insightful and completely wrong.

Sometimes before lunch.

Which one is me?

All of it, apparently.

Human beings contain an inconvenient amount of data.

A metric solves that problem by compressing.

That's what metrics do.

A performance rating takes an enormous amount of work and reduces it to something manageable.

A weight measurement reduces the physical state of my body to a number.

A credit score compresses part of my financial history.

A sales ranking compresses commercial performance.

An online follower count compresses one dimension of audience.

These numbers can be useful precisely because they leave almost everything else out.

The danger begins when I forget the leaving-out part.

Suppose I receive a disappointing performance rating at work.

Maybe the rating is fair.

That's important to acknowledge.

Maybe I didn't perform as well as I thought.

Maybe I missed commitments.

Maybe I need to improve something.

Maybe the number is telling me something uncomfortable that I need to hear.

But even if the rating is completely fair, the rating is still a measurement of performance within a particular role, during a particular period, according to particular expectations, interpreted through a particular system.

That's already a lot of qualifiers.

The human mind is wonderfully efficient at removing them.

**I got a low rating.**

Becomes:

**I'm not doing well.**

Then:

**I'm not very good at this.**

Then, if we're having a particularly productive evening:

**Maybe I'm not very good at anything.**

That escalated quickly.

Nothing in the measurement system actually calculated the last statement.

There was no field labeled:

**Overall Value of Michael as a Human Being: Below Expectations.**

I added that myself.

This is what makes identity such fertile ground for metrics.

Numbers feel authoritative.

We have spent an entire book establishing why.

They are precise.

Comparable.

Repeatable.

Visible.

They look less biased than feelings.

They arrive with the quiet confidence of arithmetic.

So when a number says something uncomfortable about me, it can feel more trustworthy than my own broader understanding of myself.

The number is objective.

I am obviously biased in favor of me.

Case closed.

Except the number isn't making most of the claims I'm attributing to it.

The scale says I weigh 247 pounds.

It does not say I am lazy.

A performance rating says something about how my work was evaluated.

It does not say I am unintelligent.

A sales figure says how many copies of a book sold.

It does not say whether the book was worth writing.

An income figure says how much money I earned.

It does not say whether my life was successful.

We add those meanings.

And because the meanings feel connected to the measurement, they can borrow the measurement's authority.

This is where precision becomes psychologically dangerous.

**247 pounds** is precise.

**I lack discipline** is an interpretation.

**3 out of 5** is precise.

**I'm mediocre** is an interpretation.

**1,200 copies sold** is precise.

**Nobody cares what I have to say** is an interpretation.

The first statement in each pair may be measurable.

The second is a story.

That doesn't mean the story is always false.

Maybe my habits really do need more discipline.

Maybe my work really does need improvement.

Maybe readers really aren't connecting with something I've written.

The point isn't to protect myself from every uncomfortable conclusion.

The point is to notice when I have crossed from measurement into meaning.

Because meaning requires more evidence than a number can provide.

This is especially difficult when the same measurement appears repeatedly.

A single number can be dismissed.

A number that follows us starts to feel like a characteristic.

My weight appears every morning.

My performance rating follows me through the year.

My job title sits beneath my name.

My bank balance updates.

My ranking moves.

My follower count rises or falls.

Repetition gives the measurement permanence.

It begins to answer a question it was never designed to answer:

**Who am I?**

And this is where we need to be careful.

A measurement can be true without being the truth about you.

That distinction isn't sentimental.

It's simply accurate.

A metric is a compressed representation of some dimension of reality.

We've insisted on that when discussing companies, employees, software, productivity, health, and every other complex system in this book.

A human being doesn't suddenly become an exception.

I can take the measurement seriously.

I can learn from it.

I can change because of it.

I can even admit that it reveals something about me that I would rather not see.

But I should be very careful when the verb changes.

**I have.**

**I did.**

**I received.**

**I weigh.**

Those are statements about things that can be measured.

**I am** is a much bigger claim.

And no dashboard we've invented is remotely qualified to finish that sentence.

## Some Numbers Can Hurt You

There is an easy piece of advice I could give at this point.

Don't let the numbers define you.

Your performance rating doesn't determine your worth.

Your ranking doesn't determine your worth.

Your salary doesn't determine your worth.

Beautiful.

Put it on a mug.

There is only one problem.

Some of these numbers can hurt you.

A performance rating may not determine my worth as a human being, but it can determine my raise.

It can affect my bonus.

It can influence whether I get promoted.

It can change which opportunities I'm offered.

It can shape how future managers perceive me.

In some organizations, it can influence whether I continue to have a job.

That's not imaginary.

I can repeat **I am not my performance rating** while driving home from work, and I think that's a healthy thing to remember.

But if that rating affects whether I can pay the mortgage, the number has acquired a certain amount of legitimate psychological significance.

This is where corporate metrics become different from many of the personal metrics we've been discussing.

If I miss my step goal, my watch cannot fire me.

It may vibrate disapprovingly, but its enforcement powers are limited.

Work is different.

There is authority attached to the measurement.

And authority changes the experience.

We've seen this throughout the book.

A manager may look at a metric and see information.

An employee may look at exactly the same metric and see consequences.

That's not because the employee is overly sensitive.

It's because consequences exist.

Suppose a dashboard says my productivity is below target.

From one perspective, that's simply a data point.

Maybe management wants to understand what's happening.

Maybe there is a perfectly reasonable explanation.

Maybe nobody has drawn any conclusion at all.

But my mind is capable of traveling considerably faster than the investigation.

**My productivity is down.**

Becomes:

**My manager is going to notice.**

Then:

**My manager is going to think I'm underperforming.**

Then:

**This will affect my rating.**

Then:

**What if I lose my job?**

We have gone from a dashboard to unemployment in approximately eight seconds.

That may be catastrophizing.

It may also reflect the fact that jobs matter.

For most people, employment is not merely a scorekeeping system.

It's how we live.

The paycheck becomes housing.

Food.

Healthcare.

Transportation.

Education.

Retirement savings.

Security for people we love.

A performance metric can therefore touch parts of life that seem very far removed from the thing being measured.

A number on a dashboard can find its way into the kitchen.

That's worth understanding.

It helps explain why people react so strongly to measurements that, from the measurer's perspective, may look relatively harmless.

Management might say:

**We're just trying to understand performance.**

And they may mean exactly that.

The employee hears:

**Someone is trying to understand whether I'm worth keeping.**

Those are very different experiences of the same measurement system.

This doesn't mean every employee concern is justified.

Not every red metric is the beginning of a termination process.

Not every question from a manager is an accusation.

Not every performance discussion is a threat.

Sometimes a manager really is just asking what happened.

But power creates ambiguity.

When the person asking the question also influences my compensation, opportunities, reputation, and continued employment, I cannot experience the question exactly as I would if it came from a coworker over coffee.

**Authority gives numbers weight.**

It also gives questions weight.

This is one reason corporate measurement can become so stressful.

A metric does not need to explicitly threaten us in order to feel threatening.

The consequences may sit several steps downstream.

Imagine two engineers have the same bug metric.

One looks at it and thinks:

**Interesting. My average closure time increased this quarter.**

The other thinks:

**Management has been emphasizing closure time lately. Mine is getting worse. I need to fix this before someone notices.**

Same number.

Different interpretation.

Now suppose the second engineer starts avoiding complicated bugs.

That makes sense.

Not necessarily because the engineer is dishonest.

Not because they don't care about customers.

Not because they have suddenly lost all professional integrity.

The measurement system has made complicated work personally expensive.

Take the difficult bug and you might spend three weeks producing one closure.

Take six easy bugs and the dashboard looks considerably better.

We have already discussed how metrics can make strange behavior rational.

Here we encounter the emotional mechanism underneath it.

People protect themselves.

That's not a particularly surprising discovery.

If a number affects my future, I will pay attention to the number.

If a number affects my livelihood, I will care about how I look through it.

And if I believe the system cannot see the full complexity of my work, I may become anxious about the parts it does see.

This is where invisible work becomes personally dangerous.

Maybe I spend hours mentoring another engineer.

Maybe I prevent a bad architectural decision.

Maybe I take the complicated bug nobody else wants.

Maybe I calm down a difficult situation.

Maybe I quietly make the team better.

I know those things happened.

My coworkers may know.

My manager may even know.

But if the formal measurement system primarily sees commits, bugs, schedules, or whatever else we've chosen to count, there can be an uncomfortable gap between my contribution and my measurable contribution.

Then performance review season arrives.

Suddenly that gap matters.

This is why telling employees to simply detach their identity from work metrics can sound hollow.

They may be trying.

But the organization itself is telling them that the metric matters.

It is discussed.

Tracked.

Compared.

Reviewed.

Sometimes rewarded.

Sometimes punished.

The employee did not invent its authority.

So we need to hold two truths at the same time.

**The metric does not determine my worth.**

And:

**The metric may have real consequences for my life.**

Neither truth cancels the other.

Pretending the second isn't true turns wisdom into denial.

Pretending the first isn't true gives the organization far more authority than it deserves.

The challenge is learning to take a number seriously without allowing it to become total.

That's hard.

Especially when the stakes are high.

And before we put all the blame for this tension on management, we need to look at the other side of the dashboard.

Because organizations have a problem too.

They have limited resources.

They have decisions to make.

They have people to evaluate.

They have customers, competitors, budgets, investors, and goals.

They need some way to decide what happens next.

The employee experiences a life.

The organization has to run a business.

And sometimes those two realities collide.

## It's Not Personal. It's Business.

There is another phrase we use when the machinery of an organization produces an unpleasant result.

**It's not personal. It's business.**

This is usually said to the person for whom the result feels extremely personal.

Your position has been eliminated.

Your project has been canceled.

Your promotion went to someone else.

Your raise is smaller than you expected.

Nothing personal.

Just business.

The phrase can sound cold, but there is something important underneath it.

A business has to make decisions.

It has limited money.

Limited people.

Limited time.

Limited opportunities.

There may be five employees ready for promotion and two available positions.

There may be ten projects worth funding and enough budget for six.

There may be a hundred employees doing valuable work and a compensation budget that cannot reward all of them equally.

Someone has to decide.

And "everyone is wonderful" turns out to be a poor resource-allocation strategy.

Organizations therefore look for ways to distinguish.

Which projects are producing results?

Which products are growing?

Which teams need investment?

Which employees are performing well?

Where are costs increasing?

Where is productivity improving?

Where are we falling behind?

These are legitimate questions.

In a large organization, they are unavoidable.

And measurement helps answer them.

In fact, the alternative to measurement isn't necessarily a kinder, more human workplace.

Imagine eliminating performance metrics entirely.

No goals.

No documented results.

No attempt to compare outcomes.

No structured evaluation.

Managers simply decide who is doing well.

Based on what?

Memory?

Impression?

Who spoke most confidently in the last meeting?

Who happens to work on the project receiving the most executive attention?

Who has the best relationship with the manager?

Who sends email at 11:47 p.m. so everyone knows how committed they are?

That system has metrics too.

They're just invisible.

Measurement can provide evidence against favoritism, charisma, recency bias, and office politics.

It can make expectations clearer.

It can give employees something more concrete than whether the boss seems pleased.

Numbers don't automatically make a system fair.

But removing numbers doesn't automatically make it fair either.

This is worth remembering because it's easy to criticize corporate measurement from the receiving end.

I have certainly done it.

Engineers can be remarkably sophisticated critics of management metrics, particularly when those metrics suggest we should change something we'd prefer not to change.

Sometimes we're right.

Sometimes the metric really is terrible.

And sometimes management is trying to solve a legitimate problem that we have the luxury of not having to solve.

Someone has to run the organization.

Someone has to allocate resources.

Someone has to decide whether what we're doing is working.

Someone has to look beyond my individual experience and see the system.

That's what management is for.

And above management sits another layer of reality.

Businesses have to survive.

They compete.

They have customers.

They have costs.

They have payroll.

They invest capital with the expectation that something useful will come back.

Public companies have shareholders who expect leadership to steward their investment responsibly.

Growth matters.

Profit matters.

Efficiency matters.

Productivity matters.

Number go up, as it turns out, is not an entirely ridiculous objective.

If revenue never goes up, costs always go up, productivity continually goes down, and nobody particularly cares because the employees are having meaningful experiences, we may have accidentally created a retreat center.

Possibly a very nice one.

But not a particularly durable business.

Optimization has a legitimate place.

The problem throughout this book has never been that organizations want things to improve.

The problem is what happens when **the thing we can optimize becomes a substitute for the thing we actually want to improve.**

Managers live inside that tension too.

It's tempting to imagine the manager sitting on the other side of the dashboard, gleefully reducing complicated human beings to cells in a spreadsheet.

I have known enough managers to know that many of them find the compression uncomfortable.

They know one employee took on the ugly project.

They know another spent half the year mentoring people.

They know a third inherited a disaster and somehow kept it from becoming a catastrophe.

They know two employees with similar numbers may have had very different years.

Then the performance system asks:

**Rating?**

Eventually, the box needs a number.

The manager may recognize everything that disappeared in order to produce it.

They still have to fill in the box.

That's the managerial version of the problem we've been discussing throughout this book.

The dashboard is not the world.

But managers still need dashboards.

The map is not the territory.

But they still need maps.

The metric is not the employee.

But they still need some way to evaluate performance.

There is no magical management system in which every decision can preserve the full complexity of every human being involved.

Scale requires compression.

The question is what we do after the compression occurs.

This is where judgment matters.

A manager can treat the metric as a verdict.

Or the manager can treat it as evidence.

They can ask why the number looks the way it does.

They can consider context.

They can notice invisible work.

They can recognize when someone took on something unusually difficult.

They can challenge a metric that is producing destructive behavior.

They can advocate upward.

They can sometimes say the sentence every healthy measurement system needs to permit:

**The number looks bad, but here's what actually happened.**

None of this means managers are free from constraints.

Their performance is measured too.

Their team has targets.

Their organization has targets.

Their division has targets.

Executives answer to boards.

Companies answer to customers and markets.

Pressure moves through systems.

Sometimes what feels like a manager's obsession with a metric is partly the manager responding to a metric being applied to them.

There is a less elegant phrase for this:

**Don't hate the player. Hate the game.**

I wouldn't go quite that far.

Players still make choices.

Managers have different amounts of power, and power creates responsibility.

"The system made me do it" can become its own convenient way of avoiding judgment.

A manager can use a metric thoughtlessly.

A leader can create destructive incentives.

An executive can choose an easy number over a difficult truth.

We are still responsible for what we do inside the systems we inhabit.

But systems shape what becomes rational.

That applies to managers just as much as it applies to employees.

So perhaps the better approach is not to hate the player or the game.

It's to understand the game well enough to see what it is asking everyone to do.

What behavior has it made rational?

What information is traveling upward?

What pressure is traveling downward?

What disappears as reality gets compressed at each level?

And where does someone still have enough agency to exercise judgment?

There is a tension here that cannot be engineered away.

The organization needs compression in order to make decisions at scale.

The employee experiences those decisions at full resolution.

At the organizational level, ten people may receive a particular performance rating.

That's a distribution.

At the individual level:

That's my rating.

My raise.

My career.

My reputation.

Maybe my job.

The organization experiences a resource-allocation problem.

The manager experiences a performance-management problem.

The employee experiences a life.

All three are real.

The mistake is assuming that because the abstraction is necessary, the abstraction is complete.

"It's not personal. It's business" may explain why the decision had to be made.

It doesn't make the consequences any less personal.

And when something deeply personal is being decided through something as compressed as a metric, it shouldn't surprise us that the person underneath it feels threatened.

## When Measurement Becomes Threat

A red number on a dashboard is not dangerous.

It cannot hurt you.

It has no teeth.

It cannot enter your home, empty your bank account, or cancel your health insurance.

But if enough consequences are attached to it, your body may not appreciate the distinction.

This is one of the strange things about corporate measurement.

From a distance, it looks wonderfully rational.

Target.

Actual.

Variance.

Green.

Yellow.

Red.

Clean little categories arranged in rows.

Nothing emotional here.

Just data.

Then one of the red cells has your name next to it.

Suddenly the dashboard feels different.

Maybe your bug closure time is too long.

Maybe your project slipped.

Maybe your sales numbers are down.

Maybe your utilization is below target.

Maybe your performance rating came in lower than expected.

Maybe everyone else on the team seems to be producing more than you.

The specific metric doesn't matter much.

What matters is the question that appears underneath it:

**Am I okay?**

Not philosophically.

Practically.

Am I okay here?

Does my manager still trust me?

Am I falling behind?

Do people think I'm capable?

Will this affect my career?

Is my job safe?

The number has moved from information to threat.

And once that happens, our relationship with it changes.

Curiosity gets harder.

Imagine your manager asks why a bug took three weeks to close.

There are at least two ways to hear the question.

The first is:

**What made this bug difficult?**

That's a useful question.

Maybe the architecture is fragile.

Maybe the debugging tools are inadequate.

Maybe the bug crossed several components.

Maybe reproducing it required an unusual configuration.

Maybe solving it revealed a deeper problem that needs attention.

The outlier becomes a learning opportunity.

But the employee may hear a different question:

**Why did you take three weeks to close this bug?**

Those sentences are almost identical.

Psychologically, they can be worlds apart.

One investigates the work.

The other feels like it is investigating the worker.

Now the conversation has a defendant.

The engineer begins explaining.

There were dependencies.

The reproduction was difficult.

Another team had to help.

The first fix caused a regression.

The problem turned out to be deeper than expected.

All of those things may be completely true.

But when the employee feels threatened, explanation can start sounding like defense.

And defense can start sounding like excuse.

Now both sides are in an awkward position.

The manager may genuinely be trying to learn.

The employee may genuinely be trying to provide context.

Yet the power relationship changes the conversation.

One person can affect the other's rating.

The other knows it.

That knowledge sits in the room even if nobody mentions it.

This is why metrics tied to performance can produce such peculiar behavior.

People don't merely optimize for the number.

They optimize for safety.

The engineer who takes the easy bugs may not be trying to manipulate the system.

They may simply have learned that difficult bugs create difficult conversations.

Take an easy bug.

Close it.

Number improves.

Nobody asks questions.

Take the strange intermittent bug that appears once every four days on one configuration and may require weeks of investigation.

Now you're an outlier.

And outliers require explanations.

After enough repetitions, the lesson becomes obvious:

**Don't become the interesting data point.**

This is how a measurement system can quietly discourage exactly the work an organization most needs.

The complicated problems are often complicated because nobody knows how to solve them yet.

They contain uncertainty.

They take longer.

They fail more often.

They produce uglier metrics.

Innovation has many of the same characteristics.

So does experimentation.

So does learning.

If every deviation from the target creates a performance conversation, people will eventually learn to avoid deviation.

Not because they're lazy.

Because they're paying attention.

This is the measuree responding rationally to the system the measurer created.

And the manager may be frustrated by this too.

They may want people to take risks.

They may genuinely mean it when they say:

**Take on hard problems.**

But if the measurement system says:

**And please make sure the hard problem behaves exactly like an easy one,**

the metric is communicating more loudly than the encouragement.

Chapter 5 called this culture.

People learn what matters around here.

They also learn what is safe around here.

That's an important distinction.

Culture isn't only built from what gets rewarded.

It is built from what people learn to fear.

Which numbers require explanation?

Which misses are tolerated?

Which failures produce curiosity?

Which failures produce consequences?

Can someone say, "This took longer because I discovered the problem was much harder than we thought," and have that treated as useful information?

Or does every explanation feel like a plea for mercy?

These questions determine whether metrics produce learning or anxiety.

Again, none of this requires a villain.

A manager can ask reasonable questions.

An employee can experience those questions as threatening.

Both can be true.

The manager has responsibilities.

The employee has a nervous system.

Organizations sometimes behave as though people should be able to cleanly separate the two.

**This is just a business discussion.**

Perhaps.

But the person sitting across the table may be thinking about a mortgage.

A spouse.

Children.

Retirement.

What happens if they need to find another job.

Whether they're still good at the profession they've practiced for twenty years.

Whether they're becoming obsolete.

Whether the younger person two desks away is faster.

Whether the new technology everyone is talking about can do half of what they do.

The dashboard doesn't contain any of this.

It just says:

**Below target.**

This is another form of compression.

The organization compresses performance into a metric.

The employee expands the metric into an entire possible future.

Neither expansion nor compression is necessarily accurate.

But that's what fear does.

It takes a piece of information and asks how far the consequences could travel.

This is why psychological safety matters when organizations use metrics.

Not because nobody should ever feel uncomfortable.

Accountability is sometimes uncomfortable.

Feedback can sting.

A manager should be able to say that performance needs to improve.

A business cannot promise that every job will exist forever.

Psychological safety cannot mean insulation from reality.

It means something more practical.

**Can we examine a bad number without immediately turning it into a bad person?**

Can we ask what happened?

Can we distinguish a difficult problem from a poorly performing employee?

Can we distinguish a failed experiment from incompetence?

Can we distinguish an explanation from an excuse long enough to actually listen to it?

Can the employee tell us something the dashboard doesn't know?

That last question may be the most important.

Because once people believe that context will not matter, they stop bringing context.

They adapt instead.

They choose safer work.

They manage appearances.

They avoid experiments.

They make commitments they believe leadership wants to hear.

They optimize what can be seen.

The dashboard gets cleaner.

Reality gets harder to see.

And the employee learns something else along the way.

They learn to watch themselves.

They begin checking the numbers before the manager does.

They anticipate the questions.

They rehearse the explanations.

They compare themselves with coworkers.

They wonder how their work will look at review time.

Eventually, nobody has to be looking at the dashboard for the dashboard to exert pressure.

The employee carries it with them.

The laptop closes.

The meeting ends.

They drive home.

The measurement system comes too.

## We Bring the Dashboard Home

The workday ends.

I close the laptop.

I leave the office.

The dashboard stays at work.

Except it doesn't.

It comes home with me.

Maybe there was a meeting that afternoon.

Nothing catastrophic happened.

My manager asked a question about a project.

A metric was below target.

I explained what happened.

My manager nodded.

We moved on.

Meeting over.

Then, at 9:47 that evening, my brain decides it would be useful to hold a follow-up meeting.

Attendance is mandatory.

**Why did they ask about that number?**

Probably because it was below target.

**Were they concerned?**

Maybe.

**Did my explanation sound defensive?**

Possibly.

**Do they think I should have handled it differently?**

I don't know.

**Is this going to affect my rating?**

Welcome home.

Nobody from work is in the room.

No manager.

No dashboard.

No performance review.

But the measurement system is still running.

I have internalized it.

This may be one of the most powerful effects measurement has on us.

External evaluation teaches us how to evaluate ourselves.

At first, someone else watches the number.

Eventually, we learn to watch it for them.

We anticipate the question before it is asked.

We notice when our numbers are falling behind.

We compare our output with the people around us.

We collect evidence that our work matters.

We rehearse explanations for anything that might look bad.

We begin viewing our own work through the imagined eyes of whoever might evaluate it later.

The manager doesn't have to be in the room anymore.

We've installed a little version of the manager inside our heads.

Chapter 6 already introduced this character.

Apparently he has a corporate job too.

This isn't necessarily unhealthy.

Some degree of internal accountability is part of being a professional.

I shouldn't need my manager standing behind me to care whether I do good work.

I should notice when I'm falling behind.

I should care whether commitments are being met.

I should ask whether my work is useful.

Maturity means internalizing standards.

The problem begins when we internalize not only the standard, but the surveillance.

There is a difference between:

**I want to do good work.**

And:

**I need to make sure my work looks good.**

Those motivations can coexist for a surprisingly long time.

Most professionals probably know the feeling.

You do something genuinely useful and immediately wonder whether anyone noticed.

You spend hours helping someone else and later realize there is no obvious artifact showing what you accomplished.

You solve a problem before it becomes a problem, which means there is now very little evidence that there was ever a problem.

Prevention has terrible marketing.

Meanwhile, someone else gives a presentation about something highly visible.

You are happy for them.

Mostly.

Then a small internal accountant opens a spreadsheet.

**Interesting.**

You don't actually know how your work and theirs will be evaluated.

But measurement culture teaches you to think in those terms.

Visibility.

Impact.

Deliverables.

Ratings.

Promotion readiness.

Whatever vocabulary your organization uses.

Again, none of these concepts are inherently bad.

Impact matters.

Results matter.

Organizations need to know what people contribute.

Employees should be able to explain the value of their work.

But there is a psychological cost when the evaluation layer becomes permanently active.

You stop simply doing the work.

You start watching yourself do the work.

Part of your attention remains outside the experience, asking how it will be interpreted later.

This is especially exhausting when the work is ambiguous.

If I produce one hundred widgets and the goal was ninety, things are relatively straightforward.

Knowledge work is rarely that polite.

Was the architecture discussion productive?

Was mentoring that engineer worth three hours?

Did I make the right decision to investigate the deeper issue rather than ship the quick fix?

Was the meeting where we decided **not** to build something valuable?

How much credit belongs to me for something ten people created?

How do I measure the bug I prevented?

How do I demonstrate the value of telling everyone we were heading in the wrong direction before we spent six months getting there?

These are judgment calls.

But when I know judgment is coming, I may start manufacturing evidence for the judgment.

This is where self-surveillance begins changing behavior.

I don't just ask:

**What does the work need?**

I ask:

**What will this look like?**

That's a dangerous substitution.

Not because appearances never matter.

In an organization, communicating your work is part of the work.

If nobody knows what you're doing, that can create real problems.

But there is a difference between making valuable work visible and choosing work because it is visible.

The first helps the organization see reality.

The second allows the measurement system to reshape reality.

And once the system is inside my head, nobody needs to explicitly tell me to make that trade.

I can do it myself.

This is why metrics can create stress even in organizations with decent managers.

The pressure doesn't have to arrive as an angry email.

It can arrive as anticipation.

Maybe my manager would understand.

Maybe the rating system has more nuance than I think.

Maybe nobody is comparing me to the coworker I'm comparing myself to.

It doesn't matter.

I know comparison is possible.

So I perform it myself.

This is one reason uncertainty can be more stressful than explicit feedback.

If someone tells me:

**You're doing well. Keep going.**

I have information.

If someone tells me:

**This isn't good enough. Here's what needs to change.**

That may hurt, but I still have information.

Ambiguity leaves room for imagination.

And imagination is remarkably talented at performance management.

A manager asks one unusual question on Tuesday.

By Thursday, I have constructed an organizational restructuring in which my position no longer exists.

No executive involvement required.

This is where the distinction from the beginning of the chapter matters.

A performance metric starts as something I **have**.

Then it becomes something I monitor.

Then something I worry about.

Then something I use to evaluate myself.

Eventually:

**How am I performing?**

can quietly become:

**How am I doing?**

Those are not the same question.

One is about work.

The other can swallow a life.

If work is going well, I feel good.

If work is going poorly, I feel bad.

If someone praises me, I expand.

If someone criticizes me, I shrink.

A promotion becomes evidence that I'm succeeding.

Being passed over becomes evidence that I'm falling behind.

The organization's evaluation system becomes part of my emotional regulation system.

That's a lot of authority to give a performance-management process.

And I don't think the solution is detachment.

I care about my work.

I want to care about my work.

I have spent a large part of my life developing skills, solving problems, contributing to teams, and trying to become better at what I do.

There is something healthy about taking pride in that.

The answer cannot be:

**None of this matters to me.**

If it genuinely didn't matter, I probably wouldn't be very good at it.

The healthier distinction is between **caring** and **being defined**.

I can care deeply about doing good work without requiring work to tell me whether I am good.

I can receive criticism without turning criticism into identity.

I can want a promotion without making promotion the measure of whether my career has meaning.

I can be disappointed by a rating without allowing a rating to become a verdict on twenty years of professional life.

That's difficult because work gives us so many numbers that invite exactly that conclusion.

Ratings.

Levels.

Compensation.

Bonuses.

Rankings.

Years to promotion.

Projects shipped.

Revenue generated.

Patents.

Publications.

Awards.

And increasingly, all the little digital traces of work itself.

Once those numbers exist, another question becomes almost irresistible:

**How am I doing compared with everyone else?**

Now the dashboard doesn't merely evaluate me.

It gives me a ruler for measuring the person sitting next to me.

And comparison has never needed much encouragement.

## Comparison Comes Standard

The moment we can measure something, we can compare it.

My number.

Your number.

Higher.

Lower.

Ahead.

Behind.

Better.

Worse.

Comparison comes standard with measurement.

This is enormously useful.

If I'm trying to understand whether my salary is competitive, knowing what other people in similar roles earn is useful information.

If a factory has ten production lines and one consistently produces twice as many defects as the others, somebody should probably take a look.

If one software team takes six months to do something comparable teams complete in six weeks, that difference may teach us something.

Comparison helps us notice patterns we might otherwise miss.

It gives us reference points.

Without comparison, a number can be surprisingly meaningless.

Is a resting heart rate of 65 good?

Is a 4 percent defect rate high?

Is $100,000 a large salary?

Is selling 5,000 books successful?

Compared with what?

Context gives numbers meaning, and comparison is one way we create context.

But comparison requires something first.

**Compression.**

Before I can compare two things numerically, I have to decide which parts of them I am going to ignore.

Suppose two engineers each closed twenty bugs this quarter.

Excellent.

They are equal.

Except one fixed twenty straightforward bugs in a well-understood component.

The other fixed eighteen straightforward bugs and two deeply complicated problems that required weeks of investigation across multiple teams.

Twenty equals twenty.

Arithmetic has spoken.

Or suppose one engineer closed thirty bugs and another closed fifteen.

Now we have a winner.

Unless the second engineer spent half the quarter designing the architecture that will prevent two hundred bugs next year.

The comparison isn't necessarily wrong.

Thirty really is more than fifteen.

The mistake is believing that because the numbers are comparable, the people are.

Metrics create common units.

That's part of their power.

Money lets us compare economic value.

Time lets us compare duration.

Weight lets us compare mass.

Ratings let organizations compare performance.

Rankings let us compare position.

But the cleaner the comparison becomes, the easier it is to forget what had to disappear to make the comparison possible.

Two employees become two ratings.

Two authors become two sales numbers.

Two bodies become two weights.

Two careers become two salaries.

Two lives become two net worths.

And suddenly realities that contain thousands of dimensions are standing beside each other on a single axis.

One is ahead.

One is behind.

This is where comparison becomes psychologically dangerous.

Because human beings are extraordinarily good at choosing the axis on which we are losing.

I can walk into a room filled with accomplished people and find the person who makes more money than I do.

Or has the better title.

Or is thinner.

Or younger.

Or more successful.

Or more confident.

Or has written more books.

Or has more hair.

There are a remarkable number of categories in which to experience inadequacy.

And metrics make the process efficient.

I don't need to know your life.

I just need your number.

This is part of what people mean when they say comparison is the thief of joy.

I can be perfectly satisfied with something until I discover someone else's measurement.

I like my house.

Then I see yours.

I feel good about my salary.

Then I learn what someone else earns.

I'm proud of losing twenty pounds.

Then I meet someone who lost fifty.

I'm pleased that a thousand people read something I wrote.

Then I discover another writer has a hundred thousand subscribers.

Nothing about my reality changed.

My house didn't shrink.

My salary didn't decrease.

The twenty pounds did not climb back onto my body.

The thousand readers did not vanish.

Only the reference point changed.

Yet my experience of the number changed with it.

This tells us something important.

Numbers don't arrive with their own emotional meaning.

We supply it.

And comparison is one of the fastest ways to change that meaning.

A salary of $150,000 can feel abundant in one comparison and disappointing in another.

A five-kilometer run can feel like an accomplishment until someone tells me their marathon time.

A performance rating can feel respectable until I discover my coworker received the next rating up.

Then the number begins answering a different question.

Not:

**How am I doing?**

But:

**Where do I stand?**

Organizations sometimes need to ask that question.

Promotion decisions are comparative.

Compensation decisions are comparative.

Hiring is comparative.

When resources are limited, someone eventually has to distinguish among alternatives.

Again, comparison itself isn't the villain.

But we should notice what happens when an organizational necessity becomes a personal habit.

I start ranking myself even when nobody has asked me to.

A coworker announces a promotion.

I congratulate them.

I mean it.

Then, somewhere beneath the congratulations, another process starts running.

**They're younger than me.**

Or:

**We've been here about the same amount of time.**

Or:

**I thought I was doing just as well.**

Their success becomes information about me.

That's a strange transformation.

Nothing happened to my career in that moment.

Someone else received good news.

Yet comparison can translate:

**Something good happened to them**

into:

**Something bad happened to me.**

This is not our finest psychological feature.

But it is deeply human.

Metrics give it infrastructure.

Levels.

Ratings.

Rankings.

Salary bands.

Leaderboards.

Percentiles.

Follower counts.

Sales charts.

Performance distributions.

We no longer have to vaguely suspect that someone is ahead of us.

We can know to two decimal places.

And the modern world has dramatically expanded the group available for comparison.

For most of human history, our comparison set was relatively small.

Family.

Neighbors.

Coworkers.

People in the community.

Now I can compare myself with almost anyone on Earth.

If I want to feel inadequate as a writer, the internet can help.

If I want to feel inadequate financially, there are people willing to show me their investment portfolios.

If I want to feel inadequate physically, I can find someone my age with abdominal muscles I had previously assumed were a special effect.

Whatever part of my life I would like to feel bad about, there is probably a leaderboard available.

This creates a peculiar asymmetry.

I experience my own life at full resolution.

I know my failures.

My doubts.

My boring days.

My unfinished work.

My compromises.

My mistakes.

I experience other people's lives through compressed signals.

Title.

Salary.

House.

Body.

Followers.

Accomplishments.

Awards.

I compare my territory with their map.

That is not a fair fight.

And yet metrics make it feel fair because the numbers appear objective.

They really do have more followers.

They really do earn more money.

They really did get promoted.

The measurement may be completely accurate.

But once again:

**Accurate is not the same as complete.**

Their number tells me something about them.

It does not tell me their life.

My number tells me something about me.

It does not tell me mine.

Comparison becomes dangerous when we forget that both sides have been compressed.

There is nothing wrong with looking sideways occasionally.

Other people can give us useful reference points.

They can show us what is possible.

They can expose places where we've become complacent.

They can teach us.

They can inspire us.

Sometimes I genuinely need to know whether I'm falling behind.

But comparison works best as information.

It becomes miserable as identity.

Because once I need my numbers to establish where I stand among everyone else, there is an obvious problem.

The comparison never ends.

There is always another person.

Another level.

Another salary.

Another accomplishment.

Another number.

Someone is always ahead.

And in a world containing billions of people and an internet designed to introduce me to the exceptional ones, finding that person has never been easier.

## Someone Is Always Winning

There is a simple way to win at comparison.

Choose the right comparison group.

If I want to feel successful, I can find someone earning less money than I do.

If I want to feel unsuccessful, I can find someone earning more.

If I want to feel healthy, I can compare myself with someone in worse shape.

If I want to ruin the afternoon, I can compare myself with a professional athlete.

The data is flexible that way.

Given a large enough population, I can usually find evidence for whatever story I would like to tell about myself.

The problem is that human beings don't seem naturally inclined to compare upward and downward with equal enthusiasm.

We tend to notice the person ahead.

This makes sense.

Comparison is partly a learning mechanism.

Someone doing better can show us what is possible.

The engineer who solves problems faster may know something I don't.

The coworker who communicates more effectively may have a skill worth learning.

The person who manages money well may have habits I should adopt.

Upward comparison can create growth.

It can also create misery.

Because there is always another level.

Suppose I get the promotion.

For a little while, it feels wonderful.

I worked hard.

Someone noticed.

The organization trusted me with greater responsibility.

My title changes.

My compensation changes.

Number go up.

Then something interesting happens.

My comparison group changes.

Yesterday I was comparing myself with people at my old level.

Today I begin noticing people at the new one.

Soon I notice the people above that.

The finish line moves because I moved.

This happens with money too.

There is a number that would make me feel secure.

Until I reach it.

Then perhaps there is another number that would make me feel *really* secure.

Then enough for retirement.

Then enough for a more comfortable retirement.

Then enough that I don't have to worry about market downturns.

Then enough that my children will be secure.

Then enough that apparently I am establishing a small sovereign wealth fund.

The problem isn't wanting more money.

Money provides real options and real security.

The problem is expecting a relative number to eventually announce:

**You have won. You may stop comparing now.**

Leaderboards rarely contain that feature.

There is always another position.

Another percentile.

Another person doing better.

And modern life has made those people extraordinarily easy to find.

A few generations ago, the most successful person I knew might have been someone in my town.

Now I can wake up, reach for my phone, and within five minutes encounter a twenty-seven-year-old entrepreneur who has sold three companies, run a marathon, written a bestselling book, and is explaining his morning routine from the kitchen of a house I cannot afford.

I haven't had coffee yet.

This seems unnecessarily aggressive.

The internet does not provide a representative sample of human life.

It provides access to extremes.

The most beautiful.

The wealthiest.

The fastest.

The most productive.

The most successful.

The most controversial.

The most visible.

Then we compare these compressed highlights with the full-resolution mess of our own existence.

I know exactly how long I procrastinated yesterday.

I do not know how long the entrepreneur procrastinated.

I know about the argument I had with my spouse.

I see their anniversary photo.

I know the chapter I wrote that didn't work.

I see the bestseller ranking.

I know what my body looks like when I get out of the shower.

I see someone else's carefully selected photograph after six months of training under ideal lighting.

The comparison was broken before the numbers arrived.

The numbers just make it look scientific.

Corporate life has its own version of this.

There is always a high performer.

Every organization has someone whose name seems to appear whenever success is discussed.

They shipped the big project.

Won the major customer.

Received the award.

Got promoted quickly.

Presented to senior leadership.

Maybe they deserve every bit of recognition.

Probably they do.

But their success can quietly become a reference point for everyone around them.

Now good performance doesn't mean doing good work.

It means doing as well as *that person*.

Then someone exceeds them.

The benchmark moves again.

This is one reason relative performance systems can create pressure even when everyone's absolute performance is improving.

A team of excellent people can still contain a bottom 10 percent.

That's arithmetic.

If every runner in a race breaks the previous world record, someone still finishes last.

Relative ranking answers:

**Where are you compared with everyone else?**

It does not necessarily answer:

**Are you doing well?**

Those are different questions.

Sometimes organizations need the first one.

If there is one promotion and five candidates, relative differences matter.

If resources are limited, comparison may be unavoidable.

But the employee should be careful about importing that logic into the rest of life.

Life does not have one promotion slot.

Someone else's marriage does not reduce the amount of love available to mine.

Someone else's book sales do not remove words from my manuscript.

Someone else's health does not make my walk less valuable.

Someone else's salary does not subtract money from my bank account.

Someone else's success is often not evidence of my failure.

Yet ranking teaches us to experience life as though it were a tournament.

There is another danger here.

Once we become accustomed to comparison, we can lose the ability to experience accomplishment without immediately locating it on a leaderboard.

I wrote a book.

How many copies did it sell?

I ran a race.

What was my time?

I got promoted.

How long did it take compared with my peers?

I saved enough for retirement.

What percentile does that put me in?

I had a good year.

Better than whom?

The accomplishment is no longer allowed to stand on its own.

It needs a ranking.

This is where comparison really does rob joy.

Not because comparison is always bad.

Because it makes satisfaction conditional on someone else having less.

That's a difficult foundation for enough.

If my success requires being ahead, there must always be someone behind.

And if my joy depends on remaining ahead, everyone else's progress becomes a small threat.

That is an exhausting way to live among other human beings.

It also makes success surprisingly fragile.

There will almost always be someone younger, richer, thinner, faster, more productive, more recognized, more followed, or more accomplished.

And if there isn't, wait a few minutes.

The internet is refreshing.

This is why the question from Chapter 7 matters so much.

**What's the point?**

Why did I want the promotion?

Why am I saving money?

Why am I trying to lose weight?

Why am I writing?

Why am I exercising?

Why am I doing this work?

If the point is meaningful work, security, health, creativity, contribution, relationship, or joy, then comparison may provide useful information.

But it cannot define success.

The point has to do that.

Otherwise, there is no destination.

There is only position.

And position is unstable.

Someone is always moving.

Someone is always gaining.

Someone is always winning.

Eventually, if I spend enough time looking at the leaderboard, I may stop saying:

**They are ahead of me.**

I may start saying:

**They are better than me.**

And now comparison has completed the same transformation we saw at the beginning of this chapter.

A number became a ranking.

The ranking became a judgment.

The judgment became an identity.

The leaderboard has become a verdict.

## The Number Becomes a Verdict

A verdict is different from a measurement.

A measurement says:

**Here is what we observed.**

A verdict says:

**Here is what it means.**

We cross that distance remarkably quickly.

The scale says my weight went up.

I say I lacked discipline.

The performance system says I received a lower rating.

I say I'm not as capable as my peers.

The sales report says the book didn't sell.

I say I'm not a real writer.

The bank account says someone else has more money.

I say they're more successful.

The follower count says someone has a larger audience.

I say they're more relevant.

The number reports.

I pronounce sentence.

This is the final stage of the compression we've been following throughout the chapter.

A complicated reality becomes a metric.

The metric becomes a comparison.

The comparison becomes a judgment.

The judgment becomes an identity.

Eventually, I no longer need the number.

I know what I am.

This is where metrics acquire an authority they were never designed to hold.

Consider a performance rating.

Let's say I receive a 2 on a five-point scale.

That number may contain useful information.

Perhaps I really did underperform.

Maybe I missed important commitments.

Maybe the quality of my work wasn't where it needed to be.

Maybe my manager saw patterns I didn't.

Maybe the rating is fair.

We don't need to discredit the measurement in order to limit its authority.

That's important.

Otherwise, the only way to protect ourselves psychologically is to prove that every disappointing metric is wrong.

And sometimes it isn't.

Sometimes the scale is right.

Sometimes the feedback is right.

Sometimes the sales are bad.

Sometimes someone else really did perform better.

Sometimes I failed.

If my sense of worth requires all the numbers to be favorable, I have created a very fragile system.

The healthier move is harder.

**The number can be right without becoming a verdict on me.**

I can have performed poorly without being a poor human being.

I can fail without becoming a failure.

I can make a bad decision without becoming an idiot.

I can gain weight without becoming lazy.

I can be passed over for a promotion without becoming irrelevant.

I can write a book nobody buys without retroactively making the writing meaningless.

This isn't positive thinking.

It's precision.

If we're going to be so impressed by numbers because they're precise, we should at least be equally precise about what they actually measure.

A performance rating measures—or attempts to measure—performance.

It does not measure intelligence.

It does not measure kindness.

It does not measure courage.

It does not measure whether I'm a good spouse, parent, friend, neighbor, or citizen.

It does not measure what I've survived.

It does not measure what I've learned.

It does not measure the person I may become five years from now.

It doesn't even perfectly measure performance.

That's a lot of authority to surrender to one number.

And yet we do.

One reason is that metrics attach themselves easily to culturally powerful ideas.

Weight attaches itself to discipline.

Income attaches itself to success.

Productivity attaches itself to usefulness.

Job title attaches itself to status.

Followers attach themselves to relevance.

Performance ratings attach themselves to competence.

Once those connections become culturally familiar, the metric seems to reveal something deeper than it actually does.

A high salary doesn't merely mean someone earns a high salary.

It starts to mean:

**They made it.**

A low salary doesn't merely describe income.

It can begin to whisper:

**You should be further along by now.**

Notice how quickly a financial number becomes a story about a life.

This is especially potent with work because work occupies so much of adult identity.

One of the first questions we ask someone new is:

**What do you do?**

It's a reasonable question.

It's also interesting grammar.

Not:

**What kind of work do you do?**

But:

**What do you do?**

As though the forty or fifty hours spent earning a living might provide a useful summary of the entire person standing in front of us.

For many of us, career becomes one of the primary stories through which we understand ourselves.

I am an engineer.

I am a manager.

I am a teacher.

I am a doctor.

I am an entrepreneur.

There's nothing wrong with that.

Work can be meaningful.

Mastery matters.

Contribution matters.

It is good to take pride in becoming excellent at something.

But when identity becomes tightly bound to work, workplace measurements gain access to much deeper territory.

A bad rating doesn't merely say:

**Something about my performance needs attention.**

It says:

**Something about me is slipping.**

A missed promotion doesn't merely mean someone else was selected.

It says:

**I'm falling behind.**

A layoff doesn't merely mean the company eliminated my position.

It can say:

**I wasn't valuable enough to keep.**

That last one can be especially brutal because the language of business itself often revolves around value.

Business value.

Market value.

Employee value.

Shareholder value.

Value creation.

We use the word constantly.

Then something happens at work and the word crosses domains.

**My work was valued less.**

Becomes:

**I am worth less.**

That's not what the spreadsheet said.

But once again, I supplied the interpretation.

The same thing happens outside work.

We even use the word **worth** directly with money.

Net worth.

It's a perfectly ordinary financial term.

Assets minus liabilities.

Useful calculation.

But consider the phrase for a moment.

**Net worth.**

There I am.

Apparently reducible to a subtraction problem.

Of course nobody seriously believes a person's human worth equals the value of their assets.

And yet money has such cultural power that financial success can easily become personal success.

The number gets promoted.

It moves from accounting into identity.

This is what metrics do when we stop watching the boundaries around them.

They migrate.

A measurement from one domain begins making claims in another.

Weight becomes character.

Income becomes success.

Performance becomes competence.

Popularity becomes significance.

Productivity becomes usefulness.

And eventually all of them compete to answer the biggest question:

**Am I enough?**

No metric is qualified to answer that.

Not because numbers are cold and human beings are magical.

Because the question exceeds the measurement.

It's a category error.

A scale cannot measure enoughness.

Neither can a salary.

Neither can a performance system.

Neither can an Amazon ranking.

Neither can a follower count.

We keep handing them the question anyway.

Then we're surprised by the answer.

The cruel part is that metrics are almost perfectly designed to answer **not yet**.

There is always another pound to lose.

Another dollar to earn.

Another level to reach.

Another rating to improve.

Another follower to gain.

Another person ahead.

If worth is attached to progress, enough remains permanently one target away.

Number must go up.

And then go up again.

This may be the most dangerous form of optimization.

Not optimizing a business.

Not optimizing a process.

Optimizing the self.

Because the project has no obvious stopping condition.

There is always something about me that could be improved.

More productive.

More successful.

More disciplined.

More knowledgeable.

More attractive.

More efficient.

More impressive.

Eventually, self-improvement can carry a strange hidden premise:

**The current version is not acceptable.**

That's a difficult way to live.

And it isn't necessary.

We can want to change without making contempt the fuel.

I can want to lose weight because health matters.

I can want to improve at work because excellence matters.

I can want to earn more because security and opportunity matter.

I can want more people to read what I write because I believe the ideas are worth sharing.

I can pursue all of those things vigorously.

The goal doesn't have to disappear.

The verdict does.

This brings us back to the grammar from the beginning of the chapter.

**I weigh.**

**I earned.**

**I received.**

**I sold.**

**I performed.**

**I failed.**

Those verbs can tell us important things.

Then comes the dangerous one.

**I am.**

That sentence requires considerably more humility.

Because the most dangerous dashboard may be the one we eventually use to explain ourselves to ourselves.

And a human being is far too large to fit inside a cell.

## You Are Larger Than the Measurement

We've spent a lot of time in this book talking about maps.

A map is useful because it leaves things out.

If a map contained every tree, every crack in the sidewalk, every person, every sound, every smell, every blade of grass, and every interesting place to get tacos, it would stop being particularly useful as a map.

Compression is the feature.

We accept this easily when we're talking about geography.

We accept it when we're talking about businesses.

Revenue doesn't tell us everything about a company.

Bug counts don't tell us everything about software quality.

Attendance doesn't tell us everything about an organization.

Productivity metrics don't tell us everything about work.

The number is a view into reality.

Not reality itself.

Then the dashboard turns toward us and we forget everything we just learned.

Suddenly the compression feels complete.

My weight becomes my body.

My rating becomes my performance.

My performance becomes my competence.

My income becomes my success.

My success becomes my worth.

At each step, more reality disappears.

Yet somehow the conclusion feels larger.

This is backward.

If anything we've encountered in this book deserves skepticism about compression, it is a human being.

Think about what would be required to completely measure one person.

Not just height and weight.

Not just income and productivity.

Not just intelligence or education.

Everything.

Every skill.

Every weakness.

Every relationship.

Every act of generosity.

Every selfish choice.

Every promise kept.

Every promise broken.

Every fear overcome.

Every fear still hiding.

Every person helped.

Every person hurt.

Every lesson learned.

Every opportunity missed.

Every private grief.

Every joke.

Every act of courage nobody saw.

Every time they showed up when they didn't feel like it.

Every time they failed.

Every time they tried again.

Every memory.

Every hope.

Every contradiction.

Good luck fitting that into the quarterly review.

Human beings are not difficult to measure because we haven't invented enough sensors yet.

We're difficult to measure because there is too much going on.

We are high-dimensional creatures living high-dimensional lives.

A performance system necessarily takes a tiny slice of that reality.

It asks a legitimate but narrow question:

**How did this person perform in this role during this period according to these expectations?**

Even that question is difficult.

But notice how different it is from:

**What kind of person is this?**

Or:

**What is this person worth?**

The metric has no idea.

That doesn't make the metric useless.

It makes it bounded.

This distinction matters because there is a temptation, especially after a painful evaluation, to fight compression by denying the measurement.

The rating is unfair.

The scale is misleading.

The sales numbers don't matter.

The promotion process is political.

Sometimes those things may be true.

But sometimes they're defenses.

Maybe the rating is fair.

Maybe the scale is showing a real problem.

Maybe the book didn't connect with readers.

Maybe I wasn't ready for the promotion.

If the only way I can preserve my identity is by discrediting every number I dislike, then the number still has too much power.

I still need it to cooperate with the story I want to tell about myself.

There is another way.

I can say:

**That's true. And it's not all that's true.**

I performed poorly on that project.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

I made a bad decision.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

I gained weight.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

Someone else was better than I was.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

This isn't a loophole.

It doesn't erase accountability.

If anything, it makes accountability easier.

When failure threatens my identity, I have an enormous incentive to explain it away.

If being wrong means **I am stupid**, I need to prove that I wasn't wrong.

If poor performance means **I am incompetent**, I need to prove that the performance wasn't poor.

If gaining weight means **I am weak**, I need to find a reason the scale doesn't count.

Identity turns feedback into a courtroom.

But if I am larger than the measurement, I can afford to look at the measurement more honestly.

Maybe I really did screw that up.

Okay.

What happened?

What can I learn?

What should I do differently?

The verdict disappears, which leaves room for investigation.

That's the irony.

Separating worth from performance does not have to make us less accountable.

It may make us more accountable.

I don't need to defend my entire self every time someone points out a mistake.

They're not evaluating my entire self.

They're evaluating the work.

At least, that's what they should be evaluating.

This distinction can be especially important for people who have spent decades building a career.

Work accumulates.

Skills accumulate.

Reputation accumulates.

Identity accumulates.

After enough years, **what I do** and **who I am** can become difficult to separate.

I am an engineer.

I am a manager.

I am an executive.

I am a teacher.

I am a physician.

Then something changes.

A reorganization.

A layoff.

A bad year.

A new technology.

A younger generation enters the field with different skills.

A role that once seemed indispensable becomes less important.

Suddenly the market is sending information we would rather not receive.

This can be painful even when nobody has done anything wrong.

Markets change.

Companies change.

Professions change.

Skills that were enormously valuable twenty years ago may become less valuable.

That is economic information.

It is not a retrospective judgment on the life spent developing them.

The world can stop paying for something without proving that the years spent doing it were meaningless.

That's an important distinction.

Markets measure demand.

They do not measure significance.

Employers measure usefulness to the organization.

They do not measure human worth.

Performance systems measure performance.

They do not measure the total contribution of a life.

Different instruments answer different questions.

We get into trouble when we ask one instrument to answer all of them.

A bathroom scale is terrible at measuring whether I'm a good father.

My bank account has very little insight into whether I'm a good friend.

My performance rating knows almost nothing about whether I am becoming wiser.

My job title cannot tell me whether people feel safe around me.

My follower count has no idea whether anything I've said has helped someone.

These aren't sentimental objections to measurement.

They're specification errors.

We're asking the instrument to measure something it was never designed to measure.

And once we see that, perhaps we can recover a little perspective.

The number can still matter.

The rating can still sting.

The promotion can still matter to me.

Losing a job can still be frightening.

Watching someone else succeed can still awaken envy.

Seeing the scale move in the wrong direction can still be disappointing.

Being larger than the measurement does not mean becoming emotionally immune to it.

It means putting the measurement back inside the life instead of putting the life inside the measurement.

That's the direction we keep getting backward.

My career is part of my life.

My life is not part of my career.

My weight is part of my health.

My health is not the entirety of who I am.

My income supports my life.

My life does not exist to maximize my income.

My accomplishments belong to my story.

My story does not exist to accumulate accomplishments.

The metric belongs inside the thing it measures.

The thing does not belong inside the metric.

And perhaps this is the map-and-territory problem at its most personal.

Earlier, we asked managers to remember that the dashboard isn't the work.

Now I have to remember that the dashboard isn't me.

It may contain information about me.

Important information.

Uncomfortable information.

Information I need to act on.

But it is still a map.

I am the territory.

And there is always more territory than the map can hold.
