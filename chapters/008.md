# 8 — Is This It?

For much of adulthood, there is usually something next. Finish school, find a job, become competent, earn more responsibility, build a home, care for a family, save money, stay healthy, prepare for whatever comes after work. The details vary enormously from one life to another, but the pattern is familiar: there is a future state we are trying to reach, and today's decisions help move us toward it. Having something next gives shape to time.

This can continue for decades without feeling particularly strange. The early goals give way to larger ones, and the problems we once hoped to have someday become the problems we complain about now. The entry-level employee wants responsibility, the experienced employee wants influence, and the manager who once wanted influence may eventually wonder why every decision now requires three meetings. Progress has a way of turning yesterday's aspirations into today's calendar.

Then something can change in middle age. It does not necessarily involve buying a sports car, quitting a job, or making an announcement that causes the family to begin exchanging concerned text messages. Often it is quieter than that. You simply notice that you already know what the next achievement is going to feel like.

There are still goals available. There will always be another project, another financial milestone, another improvement to make, another place to travel, another skill to learn. Some of them may be worth pursuing, and some may bring genuine pleasure. But the assumption that the next one will answer something important begins to weaken.

This is a strange place to arrive because nothing is obviously wrong.

In fact, that may be the unsettling part.

The system worked.

### Later Starts Arriving

For thirty years, thinking ahead may have been one of the most responsible things we did. We went to work when we didn't particularly feel like going, saved money we could have spent, learned things that were difficult, cared for people who depended on us, and accepted responsibilities that made life more complicated. We made choices today because of what they might make possible tomorrow.

There was usually a reason. The mortgage represented a home, savings represented security, professional competence made us useful to other people, and a promotion might create opportunities that did not exist before. Raising children, caring for a family, becoming someone others can depend upon, and preparing for an uncertain future are not symptoms of a shallow achievement culture. Much of adulthood really does require building things that take years to build.

For a long time, later remains comfortably in the distance. Retirement is something older people do, the mortgage has decades remaining, the children need us constantly, and the person we hope to become still feels noticeably different from the person we are. There is a lot of road ahead, so looking toward the horizon makes sense.

Eventually, later starts arriving.

The person who once wondered whether he would ever become competent now has decades of experience. The retirement account that began with almost nothing has become a serious number, and children who once needed help putting on their shoes may now have lives increasingly their own. The house is no longer the place you just moved into. The career that once seemed impossibly long has more behind it than ahead.

There is no ceremony for this transition. Nobody sends an email announcing that the building phase of adulthood has entered maintenance mode. Tuesday continues to be Tuesday, there are still meetings on the calendar, and something in the house probably needs fixing. Yet the horizon has moved close enough that a different question can begin to appear.

*What exactly have I been moving toward?*

### The Strange Disappointment of Success

We have plenty of stories about people asking large questions after failure. A career collapses, a relationship ends, illness interrupts the plan, or circumstances dismantle a future someone expected to have. When the old story stops working, it makes sense that a person would ask what remains.

Success can produce a quieter version of the same question.

Suppose you receive the promotion you wanted. There may be congratulations, a salary increase, a celebratory dinner, and genuine satisfaction that years of work have been recognized. Perhaps the new role gives you more interesting problems and a larger ability to influence things you care about. Then you wake up a few mornings later and discover that the promotion has become your job.

The same thing can happen with money. There may have been a number that once represented security to you, a balance so large that reaching it seemed as though it would permanently alter your relationship with the future. Years pass, contributions accumulate, markets do whatever markets do, and eventually the account crosses the line. You notice, perhaps tell your spouse, feel pleased for a while, and make coffee.

Nothing went wrong in either story. That is important.

The promotion may genuinely improve your career, and the money may create exactly the security you hoped it would create. A goal can deliver what it promised. The strange disappointment comes from discovering that we sometimes expected it to deliver something it never promised.

There is a particular kind of disorientation in getting what you wanted and discovering how quickly it becomes normal. The new title becomes the title, the new house becomes the house, and the financial milestone that once seemed enormous becomes the new balance from which future calculations begin. Human beings are remarkably talented at turning miracles into furniture.

That ability is probably necessary. We could not function if every achievement retained the emotional intensity of the day it arrived, and nobody wants to spend twenty years walking through the kitchen astonished that they own a refrigerator. Familiarity lets life continue.

But familiarity also reveals something about achievement. It can change our circumstances without permanently answering our questions about those circumstances.

This is where the middle-aged achiever can become confused. We did what we were supposed to do, or at least enough of it that the story basically worked. We learned the system, became competent, accepted responsibility, made plans, solved problems, and built something resembling the life we once hoped to have.

So why does the question remain?

Perhaps because the tools that help us build a life cannot tell us what the life is for.

### When More Stops Being an Answer

For much of life, *more* is a perfectly sensible direction. If I have very little savings, more creates security. If I lack the skills required for my work, more experience creates competence. If my health is poor, improvement may give me greater freedom to live the life I want.

The problem isn't more.

The problem comes when more stops answering a question and becomes the substitute for one.

This can be difficult to notice because numbers never run out. There is always a larger account balance, another professional level, another accomplishment, another improvement that could be made. Arithmetic contains no natural moment when it clears its throat and says, “That should do it.”

Meaning has to supply the stopping point.

Money makes this especially obvious. *How much do I have?* is a question a financial statement can answer with remarkable precision. *Do I have enough?* cannot be answered until I know what the money is for.

Enough to retire at a certain age is partly a financial calculation. Enough to provide security for people I love is a larger question. Enough to make me finally stop worrying about money may turn out not to be a number at all.

The distinction matters because *more* can continue long after the original purpose has been satisfied. I can keep climbing professionally because climbing is what careers do, keep accumulating because accumulation has become familiar, or keep improving myself because improvement feels more responsible than asking whether the improvement is still serving something I care about.

This does not make ambition suspect. I hope to remain curious, productive, and capable of learning for as long as I can. There may be work ahead of me that matters enormously.

But eventually *What comes next?* needs a companion.

*What is all of this for?*

That is a different kind of question.

### The Story We Spent Our Lives Creating

Perhaps the first part of adulthood is necessarily devoted to creating a story. We choose a career, form relationships, make commitments, develop abilities, and gradually become recognizable to ourselves and other people. I am an engineer, a spouse, a parent, a friend, a musician, someone who believes certain things and has spent years trying, with mixed success, to live accordingly.

We need that story. A young person cannot spend forever contemplating every possible life; eventually some possibilities have to become choices. Careers require specialization, relationships require commitment, and communities depend upon people becoming sufficiently stable that others can know what to expect from them. A life acquires shape partly because we stop trying to remain every possible person.

For years, much of our energy goes into creating that shape. We build the career, establish the household, learn the responsibilities, develop routines, and accumulate enough history that the person we have become begins to feel inevitable. Of course I am this person. I have been him for a long time.

Then, somewhere along the way, we begin commenting on the story we created.

Why did that accomplishment matter so much to me when I was thirty? Why did that failure change the direction of my life? Why do I remember an unplanned conversation with someone I loved more vividly than a project that consumed two years?

These are different questions from the ones that built the story. Earlier we asked what career to pursue, whether to take the job, where to live, how to solve the problem, and what goal should come next. Now we begin asking what those choices have meant.

We move from constructing a life toward interpreting one.

The shift is subtle because both activities can happen at the same time. I still have decisions to make and things I hope to accomplish. Middle age does not turn us into historians of lives that are already over.

But there is finally enough story behind us to notice things we could not see while we were busy creating it. Some achievements that seemed enormous at the time have faded into the background, while moments that looked almost insignificant have remained. A conversation in a parking lot can somehow survive in memory after the details of a two-year project have disappeared.

This doesn't mean the project was meaningless or the memory is a more accurate measure of value. They may simply belong to different dimensions of the story. One changed what happened next. The other changed us in a way that became visible only later.

The life behind us begins asking questions of the life ahead.

And for perhaps the first time, the next thing is not automatically the answer.

### The Empty Space

The competent response to this realization is, naturally, to develop a plan.

If work no longer provides enough meaning, perhaps I need a hobby, a second career, a volunteer role, more travel, or a spiritual practice. These may all be excellent ideas. Give an experienced professional an existential crisis and there is a decent chance they will have milestones established by Friday.

Mastery is remarkably adaptable.

We can optimize our way into becoming less obsessed with optimization. We can schedule twenty minutes of meditation, create a reading list on meaning, block out quality time with people we love, and perhaps build a spreadsheet to ensure that we are spending enough time being spontaneous. Soon we are making excellent progress toward becoming the sort of person who no longer needs to make excellent progress.

I recognize the impulse because doing something feels much better than not knowing. Problems are comforting in a peculiar way because a problem implies a solution. If I am dissatisfied, restless, or uncertain, I want to identify the cause, determine the corrective action, and get the whole thing moving in a healthier direction.

But perhaps not knowing what comes next is not always a defect.

There may be periods of life when the story that organized us for years has reached some kind of boundary and the next one has not yet taken shape. Nothing is necessarily broken. We may simply be standing somewhere without a visible milestone, which can feel remarkably similar to being lost when we have spent decades navigating by milestones.

This does not mean every feeling of emptiness contains hidden wisdom. Sometimes a job really does need to change, a relationship needs attention, or a problem requires competent action. Confusion is not automatically profound, and I have no desire to turn ordinary dissatisfaction into a mystical achievement.

Still, some questions become smaller when we demand immediate answers from them.

*What do I want the rest of my life to be about?*

*What have I learned from the life I built?*

*What matters to me now that I already know what achievement feels like?*

I don't know that those questions improve when converted into objectives and key results.

For someone accustomed to solving things, leaving a question open can feel almost irresponsible. We have learned to reduce uncertainty, identify ambiguity, and keep work moving. Yet perhaps there are questions we need to live beside for a while before we understand what they are asking.

That possibility asks something unfamiliar from the achiever. Not passivity, and not rejection of the person we became. We still have work to do, responsibilities to carry, people to love, and decisions to make.

But maybe the tools that helped us construct the story are not enough to interpret it.

### At the Edge of the Map

For most of my adult life, when I did not know what to do, I looked for the next thing. There was usually something available: another problem, another project, another goal, another part of myself that could use improvement. That habit served me remarkably well. It helped me build a career, care for people I love, prepare for the future, and create a life for which I am deeply grateful.

I don't want to reject any of that.

What I am beginning to question is the assumption that the same posture must carry me through every part of what comes next.

For years, the central question was some version of *What should I do?* What should I learn, build, fix, save, improve, or prepare? Those questions developed competence and gave shape to a life.

Now another kind of question has begun appearing. Not *What should I accomplish next?* Not even yet *What should replace accomplishment?* Something less settled than either of those.

*What have I been too busy building to notice?*

I don't have a framework for answering that question. I am not sure it needs one yet. For once, perhaps the next step is not to find the next step.

Perhaps it is simply to leave the question open.
