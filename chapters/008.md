# Chapter 8 — When the Number Becomes Me

## The Grammar Changes

Something happens to a number when we repeat it often enough.

It stops describing something.

It starts describing us.

At first, the language is straightforward.

**I weigh 247 pounds.**

That's a measurement.

A scale collected some information about my body at a particular moment and reported it back to me.

Then the grammar changes.

**I am fat.**

Something just happened.

The number disappeared.

So did the scale.

A measurement became a description.

And the description became an identity.

We do this remarkably easily.

**I received a poor performance rating.**

Becomes:

**I'm a poor performer.**

**My book didn't sell very well.**

Becomes:

**I'm not a very good writer.**

**I made less money this year.**

Becomes:

**I'm falling behind.**

**My business failed.**

Becomes:

**I'm a failure.**

The movement is subtle enough that we may not notice it.

The number measures something I did.

Then the number measures something about me.

Then the number tells me what I am.

Those are three very different claims.

The first two may contain useful information.

The third deserves considerably more suspicion.

We've already seen this mistake throughout the book.

A company measures software defects.

Defects become quality.

A company measures AI usage.

Usage becomes adoption.

Adoption becomes progress.

A company measures tasks completed.

Tasks become productivity.

Each step compresses reality a little further.

Eventually the proxy and the thing it represents become almost indistinguishable in our language.

Now we're doing the same thing with a human being.

Except the territory is me.

That's why this particular collapse matters so much.

If we shouldn't expect a single metric to completely describe the health of an organization, why would we expect one to describe a person?

People are considerably messier than dashboards.

I have written good code and bad code.

I have made wise decisions and foolish ones.

I have been disciplined and undisciplined.

I have succeeded at things and failed spectacularly at others.

I have been generous and selfish, courageous and afraid, focused and distracted, insightful and completely wrong.

Sometimes before lunch.

Which one is me?

All of it, apparently.

Human beings contain an inconvenient amount of data.

A metric solves that problem by compressing.

That's what metrics do.

A performance rating takes an enormous amount of work and reduces it to something manageable.

A weight measurement reduces the physical state of my body to a number.

A credit score compresses part of my financial history.

A sales ranking compresses commercial performance.

An online follower count compresses one dimension of audience.

These numbers can be useful precisely because they leave almost everything else out.

The danger begins when I forget the leaving-out part.

Suppose I receive a disappointing performance rating at work.

Maybe the rating is fair.

That's important to acknowledge.

Maybe I didn't perform as well as I thought.

Maybe I missed commitments.

Maybe I need to improve something.

Maybe the number is telling me something uncomfortable that I need to hear.

But even if the rating is completely fair, the rating is still a measurement of performance within a particular role, during a particular period, according to particular expectations, interpreted through a particular system.

That's already a lot of qualifiers.

The human mind is wonderfully efficient at removing them.

**I got a low rating.**

Becomes:

**I'm not doing well.**

Then:

**I'm not very good at this.**

Then, if we're having a particularly productive evening:

**Maybe I'm not very good at anything.**

That escalated quickly.

Nothing in the measurement system actually calculated the last statement.

There was no field labeled:

**Overall Value of Michael as a Human Being: Below Expectations.**

I added that myself.

This is what makes identity such fertile ground for metrics.

Numbers feel authoritative.

We have spent an entire book establishing why.

They are precise.

Comparable.

Repeatable.

Visible.

They look less biased than feelings.

They arrive with the quiet confidence of arithmetic.

So when a number says something uncomfortable about me, it can feel more trustworthy than my own broader understanding of myself.

The number is objective.

I am obviously biased in favor of me.

Case closed.

Except the number isn't making most of the claims I'm attributing to it.

The scale says I weigh 247 pounds.

It does not say I am lazy.

A performance rating says something about how my work was evaluated.

It does not say I am unintelligent.

A sales figure says how many copies of a book sold.

It does not say whether the book was worth writing.

An income figure says how much money I earned.

It does not say whether my life was successful.

We add those meanings.

And because the meanings feel connected to the measurement, they can borrow the measurement's authority.

This is where precision becomes psychologically dangerous.

**247 pounds** is precise.

**I lack discipline** is an interpretation.

**3 out of 5** is precise.

**I'm mediocre** is an interpretation.

**1,200 copies sold** is precise.

**Nobody cares what I have to say** is an interpretation.

The first statement in each pair may be measurable.

The second is a story.

That doesn't mean the story is always false.

Maybe my habits really do need more discipline.

Maybe my work really does need improvement.

Maybe readers really aren't connecting with something I've written.

The point isn't to protect myself from every uncomfortable conclusion.

The point is to notice when I have crossed from measurement into meaning.

Because meaning requires more evidence than a number can provide.

This is especially difficult when the same measurement appears repeatedly.

A single number can be dismissed.

A number that follows us starts to feel like a characteristic.

My weight appears every morning.

My performance rating follows me through the year.

My job title sits beneath my name.

My bank balance updates.

My ranking moves.

My follower count rises or falls.

Repetition gives the measurement permanence.

It begins to answer a question it was never designed to answer:

**Who am I?**

And this is where we need to be careful.

A measurement can be true without being the truth about you.

That distinction isn't sentimental.

It's simply accurate.

A metric is a compressed representation of some dimension of reality.

We've insisted on that when discussing companies, employees, software, productivity, health, and every other complex system in this book.

A human being doesn't suddenly become an exception.

I can take the measurement seriously.

I can learn from it.

I can change because of it.

I can even admit that it reveals something about me that I would rather not see.

But I should be very careful when the verb changes.

**I have.**

**I did.**

**I received.**

**I weigh.**

Those are statements about things that can be measured.

**I am** is a much bigger claim.

And no dashboard we've invented is remotely qualified to finish that sentence.

## Some Numbers Can Hurt You

There is an easy piece of advice I could give at this point.

Don't let the numbers define you.

Your performance rating doesn't determine your worth.

Your ranking doesn't determine your worth.

Your salary doesn't determine your worth.

Beautiful.

Put it on a mug.

There is only one problem.

Some of these numbers can hurt you.

A performance rating may not determine my worth as a human being, but it can determine my raise.

It can affect my bonus.

It can influence whether I get promoted.

It can change which opportunities I'm offered.

It can shape how future managers perceive me.

In some organizations, it can influence whether I continue to have a job.

That's not imaginary.

I can repeat **I am not my performance rating** while driving home from work, and I think that's a healthy thing to remember.

But if that rating affects whether I can pay the mortgage, the number has acquired a certain amount of legitimate psychological significance.

This is where corporate metrics become different from many of the personal metrics we've been discussing.

If I miss my step goal, my watch cannot fire me.

It may vibrate disapprovingly, but its enforcement powers are limited.

Work is different.

There is authority attached to the measurement.

And authority changes the experience.

We've seen this throughout the book.

A manager may look at a metric and see information.

An employee may look at exactly the same metric and see consequences.

That's not because the employee is overly sensitive.

It's because consequences exist.

Suppose a dashboard says my productivity is below target.

From one perspective, that's simply a data point.

Maybe management wants to understand what's happening.

Maybe there is a perfectly reasonable explanation.

Maybe nobody has drawn any conclusion at all.

But my mind is capable of traveling considerably faster than the investigation.

**My productivity is down.**

Becomes:

**My manager is going to notice.**

Then:

**My manager is going to think I'm underperforming.**

Then:

**This will affect my rating.**

Then:

**What if I lose my job?**

We have gone from a dashboard to unemployment in approximately eight seconds.

That may be catastrophizing.

It may also reflect the fact that jobs matter.

For most people, employment is not merely a scorekeeping system.

It's how we live.

The paycheck becomes housing.

Food.

Healthcare.

Transportation.

Education.

Retirement savings.

Security for people we love.

A performance metric can therefore touch parts of life that seem very far removed from the thing being measured.

A number on a dashboard can find its way into the kitchen.

That's worth understanding.

It helps explain why people react so strongly to measurements that, from the measurer's perspective, may look relatively harmless.

Management might say:

**We're just trying to understand performance.**

And they may mean exactly that.

The employee hears:

**Someone is trying to understand whether I'm worth keeping.**

Those are very different experiences of the same measurement system.

This doesn't mean every employee concern is justified.

Not every red metric is the beginning of a termination process.

Not every question from a manager is an accusation.

Not every performance discussion is a threat.

Sometimes a manager really is just asking what happened.

But power creates ambiguity.

When the person asking the question also influences my compensation, opportunities, reputation, and continued employment, I cannot experience the question exactly as I would if it came from a coworker over coffee.

**Authority gives numbers weight.**

It also gives questions weight.

This is one reason corporate measurement can become so stressful.

A metric does not need to explicitly threaten us in order to feel threatening.

The consequences may sit several steps downstream.

Imagine two engineers have the same bug metric.

One looks at it and thinks:

**Interesting. My average closure time increased this quarter.**

The other thinks:

**Management has been emphasizing closure time lately. Mine is getting worse. I need to fix this before someone notices.**

Same number.

Different interpretation.

Now suppose the second engineer starts avoiding complicated bugs.

That makes sense.

Not necessarily because the engineer is dishonest.

Not because they don't care about customers.

Not because they have suddenly lost all professional integrity.

The measurement system has made complicated work personally expensive.

Take the difficult bug and you might spend three weeks producing one closure.

Take six easy bugs and the dashboard looks considerably better.

We have already discussed how metrics can make strange behavior rational.

Here we encounter the emotional mechanism underneath it.

People protect themselves.

That's not a particularly surprising discovery.

If a number affects my future, I will pay attention to the number.

If a number affects my livelihood, I will care about how I look through it.

And if I believe the system cannot see the full complexity of my work, I may become anxious about the parts it does see.

This is where invisible work becomes personally dangerous.

Maybe I spend hours mentoring another engineer.

Maybe I prevent a bad architectural decision.

Maybe I take the complicated bug nobody else wants.

Maybe I calm down a difficult situation.

Maybe I quietly make the team better.

I know those things happened.

My coworkers may know.

My manager may even know.

But if the formal measurement system primarily sees commits, bugs, schedules, or whatever else we've chosen to count, there can be an uncomfortable gap between my contribution and my measurable contribution.

Then performance review season arrives.

Suddenly that gap matters.

This is why telling employees to simply detach their identity from work metrics can sound hollow.

They may be trying.

But the organization itself is telling them that the metric matters.

It is discussed.

Tracked.

Compared.

Reviewed.

Sometimes rewarded.

Sometimes punished.

The employee did not invent its authority.

So we need to hold two truths at the same time.

**The metric does not determine my worth.**

And:

**The metric may have real consequences for my life.**

Neither truth cancels the other.

Pretending the second isn't true turns wisdom into denial.

Pretending the first isn't true gives the organization far more authority than it deserves.

The challenge is learning to take a number seriously without allowing it to become total.

That's hard.

Especially when the stakes are high.

And before we put all the blame for this tension on management, we need to look at the other side of the dashboard.

Because organizations have a problem too.

They have limited resources.

They have decisions to make.

They have people to evaluate.

They have customers, competitors, budgets, investors, and goals.

They need some way to decide what happens next.

The employee experiences a life.

The organization has to run a business.

And sometimes those two realities collide.
