# 8 - When the Number Becomes Me

### The Grammar Changes

There is a small linguistic shift that can happen when we live with a number for long enough. At first, the number describes something about us, but eventually, almost without our noticing, it begins to sound as though it describes us. Weight is an obvious example. I can step onto a scale and say that I weigh 247 pounds, which is a fairly modest claim: a device measured one aspect of my body at one moment and gave me a number. But *I weigh 247 pounds* can become *I’m overweight*, which can become *I’m fat*, and before long the statement has gathered meanings about discipline, health, attractiveness, weakness, and self-control that the scale itself never measured.

The scale did not accuse me of anything. It gave me a number, and I supplied most of the commentary afterward.

Work has its own version of this. An employee receives a disappointing performance rating and begins with a factual statement: *I received a low rating this year.* After a while that can become *I’m not performing well*, and from there it is surprisingly easy to arrive at *Maybe I’m not very good at this*. The movement feels reasonable because each statement is related to the one before it. A performance rating really is supposed to tell us something about performance, just as weight really does tell us something about the body and sales figures really do tell an author how many people bought a book.

The trouble is that the claim keeps getting larger as it travels. Suppose a performance system gives me a three out of five. Maybe the rating is fair; perhaps I missed commitments, communicated badly, or simply did not perform as well as I believed I had. One reason to have an evaluation system at all is that my own impression of my work should not be the only source of truth. Even so, that rating still refers to something fairly specific: my performance in a particular role, over a particular period, under a particular set of expectations, interpreted through whatever process my organization uses.

The number removes most of that context because removing context is partly what makes the number useful. A manager cannot carry an entire year of my working life into a calibration meeting, just as an executive cannot carry the full detail of every project into a quarterly review. Organizations need compression. A dashboard turns a complicated business into a handful of indicators, a project status turns months of work into green, yellow, or red, and a performance system turns hundreds of decisions and interactions into a rating that can be discussed and compared.

That compression is not inherently a mistake. We have been relying on it throughout the book because modern organizations could barely function without it. The mistake comes later, when we forget how much disappeared in order to make the representation manageable.

Human beings are particularly awkward things to compress. I have written good code and bad code, handled situations wisely and foolishly, worked hard and procrastinated, been generous and selfish, confident and afraid. Depending on the week, I can produce evidence for several contradictory theories about the sort of person I am. A metric cuts through that mess by selecting one dimension and making it visible, which can be extremely helpful if the dimension happens to be one I need to understand.

If my performance is declining, I would rather know. If my weight is increasing in a direction I do not want, I would rather not protect myself from that fact. If a book I wrote is selling poorly, that is information worth having. The danger is not that the measurement contains no truth, but that a true statement about one dimension can borrow enough authority to begin making claims about the whole person.

Precision encourages this. A scale saying 247.6 feels more authoritative than the vague thought that I have been undisciplined lately. A performance rating of 2.8 looks firmer than the suspicion that perhaps I have been struggling at work. The measured part may indeed be precise, but the meaning we attach to it usually is not. I can know exactly what I weigh and still be uncertain about why my weight changed, just as I can know exactly how many books I sold and remain uncertain about what the book meant to the people who read it.

This is where the grammar becomes useful to notice. There is a difference between *I weigh*, *I received*, *I sold*, *I earned*, and *I am*. The first group describes conditions, events, or outcomes and leaves room for time, context, behavior, and change. The second begins making a much broader claim about identity, often on the basis of information that was never designed to support it.

That broader claim can sometimes point toward something worth examining. If I repeatedly miss commitments, perhaps I do need to become more reliable. If my habits have been harming my health, perhaps discipline really is part of what needs attention. But I do not have to turn a behavior into a permanent identity before I am allowed to work on it. If I handled several commitments poorly, I can ask how to manage them differently; if the problem becomes *I am an unreliable person*, the task has suddenly expanded from changing a behavior to repairing a self.

That is a much heavier project than the original metric ever asked for.

### When the Number Has Consequences

It would be comforting to solve this by saying that we should simply stop letting numbers define us. Your performance rating does not determine your worth, nor does your salary, your weight, your job title, your bank account, your sales figures, or the number of people who follow you online. I think that is broadly true, but it can sound a little thin when one of those numbers has just affected your raise.

A performance rating may not determine my value as a human being, but it can affect my compensation, my chances of promotion, the projects I am trusted with, and sometimes whether I continue to have a job. If I receive a poor rating, I cannot simply float above the system reminding myself that my true worth exists somewhere beyond the reach of corporate measurement. There are practical consequences, and pretending otherwise would make the reassurance feel more like denial than wisdom.

Work also reaches much farther into life than the office. A salary becomes housing, groceries, health insurance, retirement savings, college expenses, vacations, and the ability to help people I care about. A number inside a performance-management system can therefore travel a very long way once it leaves the spreadsheet. This is one reason employees sometimes react more strongly to metrics than the people designing them expect.

Imagine a manager saying, “I noticed your bug-closure time has been high lately. What’s going on?” The manager may genuinely be curious, with no conclusion formed at all. Perhaps the engineer is blocked, perhaps the bugs were unusually difficult, or perhaps the metric is misleading. From the manager’s point of view, this may simply be an attempt to understand the work.

The employee may hear the question differently because the person asking it has authority. *My number is bad. My manager noticed. Is this going to affect my review?* That interpretation can become exaggerated, certainly; one question is not necessarily the first stage of unemployment. But the employee has not invented the stakes out of nowhere, because the same manager may influence compensation, opportunities, reputation, and continued employment.

This is why people often optimize not only for performance but also for safety. If difficult bugs tend to stay open longer and long-running bugs attract unpleasant attention, then a straightforward bug becomes attractive for reasons that have nothing to do with customer impact or technical importance. The engineer is not merely choosing between two work items; they may also be choosing between two possible future stories about their performance.

The same tension appears in work that matters greatly but travels badly through the measurement system. An experienced engineer may spend hours mentoring a younger one, prevent an architectural mistake before it becomes visible, take the complicated problem everyone else avoids, or quietly keep a difficult team situation from becoming worse. Those things may matter to the organization, and a good manager may know they happened, but if the formal system primarily sees bugs, commits, schedules, or some other easily counted output, there can be an uncomfortable gap between contribution and measurable contribution.

That gap feels much less theoretical when decisions about careers are being made. It also explains why telling employees simply to detach their identity from work metrics can ring hollow. The organization itself has told them that the metric matters by tracking it, discussing it, comparing it, and sometimes attaching real consequences to it. The employee did not invent all of that authority.

So the challenge is not to pretend that the number is harmless. It is to take the consequences seriously without allowing the number to become a complete description of the person living with them.

### The Organization Cannot Avoid Comparison

There is another side to this problem, and I think it deserves more sympathy than it usually gets. Organizations really do have to make decisions, and those decisions often involve scarce things. There may be five people ready for promotion and two available positions, ten worthwhile projects and enough budget to fund six, or a compensation pool that cannot simply expand because everyone contributed something valuable.

Someone eventually has to choose, and once an organization reaches any meaningful scale, some form of comparison becomes unavoidable. This is why I resist the easy story in which managers are the people reducing human beings to spreadsheets while employees occupy the morally superior world of full-resolution reality. Managers are also trying to navigate systems that cannot preserve every detail of every person in every decision.

Suppose a manager has ten employees. Over the course of a year, each one works on different projects, solves different problems, collaborates with different people, and operates under different constraints. Some work is highly visible, some is not, and some problems are much harder than the final result will ever reveal. Then the performance cycle arrives and the system asks for a rating.

The manager may know that the rating is inadequate. They may know that one employee inherited a troubled project, another spent much of the year mentoring less-experienced engineers, and a third produced fewer visible results because they took on the hardest technical work. Eventually, though, the box still needs something in it because the organization is trying to make decisions at a scale where “every case is unique” cannot be the entire operating model.

The alternative to formal measurement is not necessarily a warmer and more humane system. If we eliminate performance metrics, structured goals, and explicit attempts to compare outcomes, managers still have to decide who is doing well. They may simply rely more heavily on memory, confidence, visibility, personal rapport, who spoke most persuasively in meetings, or who happened to work on the project receiving executive attention.

An informal system has metrics too; they are just harder to inspect.

Formal measurement can therefore protect people from some kinds of bias. It can make expectations more explicit, give employees something more concrete than the vague sense that the boss seems pleased, and provide evidence that challenges a manager’s intuition. Numbers do not make a system fair by themselves, but removing them does not make the system fair either.

Engineers sometimes forget this because we usually experience measurement from the receiving side. We can become highly sophisticated critics of organizational metrics, especially when the metric implies that something about our work should change. Sometimes we are completely right, because some metrics really are bad. At other times management is trying to solve a problem that we personally do not have to solve: how to make choices across hundreds or thousands of people when direct experience is no longer possible.

That is part of what management is for. Businesses also have customers, payroll, competitors, expenses, and people who expect leadership to use resources responsibly. Growth, profit, productivity, and efficiency are not embarrassing concepts, and “number go up” is not, by itself, a ridiculous objective. If revenue never increases, costs continually rise, productivity steadily falls, and none of this matters because everyone is having rich and meaningful experiences, the organization may eventually discover that it has become something other than a durable business.

The tension is not that companies need numbers while human beings need meaning. Companies need both, and managers are often standing directly in the middle of that conflict. A manager can know everything that disappeared during the compression and still be required to compress. They can understand that one employee took the ugly project nobody wanted and another quietly held a team together, then still have to participate in a system that asks for ratings and comparisons.

From the organization’s point of view, this may be a resource-allocation problem. From the employee’s point of view, it is a year of their life being represented by a small number of fields. Both views are real, and neither becomes more real by pretending the other does not exist.

The practical question, then, is not whether organizations can avoid compression altogether. They cannot. The better question is whether there remain pathways back to context when the compressed representation is no longer sufficient. Can a manager see an unfavorable number and ask what happened before deciding what it means? Can the system recognize that a result needs improvement without immediately treating the person associated with the result as the problem?

That is a harder way to manage than simply trusting the dashboard, but it is much closer to the reality the dashboard was built to represent.

### Comparing Whole People With Partial Data

Metrics make comparison possible, which is one of their greatest strengths and also one of the easiest ways for them to become psychologically dangerous. Comparing two complete human beings would be absurdly difficult. Each person brings a history, temperament, opportunities, limitations, relationships, luck, choices, and circumstances that shaped what happened next, which is far too much information for any practical decision.

So we choose a dimension. Employee A received a higher performance rating than Employee B. Author A sold more books than Author B. Person A earns more. Person B weighs less. Suddenly the comparison is manageable because almost everything except the dimension we care about has been removed.

Sometimes that is exactly what we need. If I want to know which product earned more revenue, revenue is an appropriate dimension. If I want to know which salesperson sold more, sales numbers are relevant. The difficulty begins when the comparison escapes the question it was designed to answer.

This happens easily in personal comparisons because the information on the two sides is so uneven. I know my own life from the inside: the mistakes, doubts, abandoned plans, failures, awkward conversations, unfinished work, and decisions I wish I could revisit. Then I look at someone else and see a promotion, a salary, a book ranking, or a body.

I am comparing my territory with their map.

The other person often looks cleaner because almost all of their complexity has disappeared. I do not see the bad week, the missed opportunity, the insecurity, the fortunate timing, or the part of their life that is going poorly. I see the selected result, while I continue to know everything about my own circumstances that the equivalent result would leave out.

That does not mean comparison is useless. If another engineer consistently produces better work, I may have something to learn. If another author reaches many more readers, perhaps they understand something about craft, audience, or marketing that I do not. The problem arises when the measured difference expands into a judgment the data cannot support.

A lower rating can tell me that someone was rated higher. It cannot tell me that they are more valuable as a person. Lower sales can tell me that another book sold better, but they cannot tell me that writing mine was pointless. A higher weight can tell me something real about my body without settling questions about discipline, attractiveness, character, or whether I am doing well in life.

The number remains useful when it stays connected to the question that produced it. Once we start asking it to settle questions about worth or adequacy, we have moved well beyond the instrument’s design.

### The Appeal of a Verdict

I suspect one reason we let metrics become so personal is that they offer something psychologically attractive: certainty. Questions like *Am I doing well at work? Am I healthy? Am I successful? Am I making good use of my life?* are difficult because the answers depend on time, context, values, tradeoffs, and the inconvenient possibility that two reasonable people might disagree.

A number simplifies the problem. If my rating is high, apparently work is going well. If my weight is down, apparently health is improving. If sales are rising, apparently the book is succeeding. The relief comes from the fact that the number allows us to stop holding several conflicting pieces of information in our heads at once.

But the clean answer is often clean because most of the reality has been removed.

Health illustrates this nicely. Weight matters, but so do blood pressure, strength, fitness, sleep, mobility, how I feel, and what my body allows me to do. If I choose weight as the answer to the much larger question *How healthy am I?*, I gain clarity by discarding information. That may be useful for a particular purpose, but it should not be mistaken for having solved the larger problem.

Work is similar. A performance rating can help answer how the organization assessed my contribution during a particular period. It is much less capable of answering whether my career is meaningful, whether I am growing, whether my coworkers trust me, whether I am doing work that fits my abilities, or whether this way of spending my days fits the life I actually want.

Those questions remain irritatingly human, and no dashboard relieves us of them.

In fact, the desire for a verdict may be part of what gives the metric too much authority. If I already feel uncertain about myself, the number arrives looking objective and decisive. A disappointing result can then do more than report a problem; it can confirm a fear that was already waiting for evidence.

This does not mean we should become emotionally indifferent to measurement. A bad performance rating should get my attention, and a worsening health trend should matter. But attention is not the same thing as handing the entire interpretation over to the number. I can ask what the measurement is telling me without assuming it has answered every other question at the same time.

### Put the Number Back Inside the Life

After all of this, I still want the information. If I am performing poorly, I want to know. If my health is moving in the wrong direction, I want the measurement. If something I create is not reaching people, I would rather know that too than protect myself with a story that feels good but happens not to be true.

The answer is not to soften every uncomfortable result until it means nothing. It is to put the result back into the larger life from which it was extracted.

A performance rating exists inside a career, and a career exists inside a life. Weight exists inside health, while health exists inside a life that also contains relationships, work, pleasure, rest, responsibilities, and things that matter for reasons no scale can see. Book sales exist inside the experience of writing, publishing, sharing ideas, hearing from readers, learning, and deciding whether the work was worth doing.

Once the measurement is returned to that larger context, it can actually become easier to take seriously. If a performance rating no longer has to prove that I am a worthwhile person, I can look more directly at what it says about my work. If my weight does not have to summarize my character, I can examine the trend without immediately turning the number into shame.

I no longer have to defend my whole identity every time a metric moves.

That does not eliminate discomfort, and I am not sure it should. Some information ought to be uncomfortable. If I handled something badly, growth may begin by admitting that I handled it badly. What matters is the proportion of the claim. *I handled this badly* points toward something specific that can be understood and perhaps changed; *I am bad* makes a much larger claim with much weaker evidence.

Metrics are not especially good at maintaining that proportion because their usefulness comes from isolation. We ask a measurement to pull one dimension out of reality so we can see it clearly. It would be odd to complain afterward that the number failed to carry the rest of the life along with it.

Remembering the rest is our job.

The map-and-territory idea has followed us through this book because the same pattern keeps appearing in different places. A dashboard is a map of an organization, a process metric is a map of behavior, and a scale, performance rating, salary, or sales figure is a map of some part of a human life. They are useful because they simplify, and incomplete for exactly the same reason.

When a number looks good, I can enjoy what it tells me without asking it to certify that I am good. When it looks bad, I can learn from what it reveals without allowing it to become a complete account of who I am. In both cases, the measurement can remain serious without becoming absolute.

That seems like enough responsibility for one number.
