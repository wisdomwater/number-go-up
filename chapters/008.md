# 8 — Is This It?

For much of adulthood, there is usually something next. Finish school, find a job, become competent, earn more responsibility, build a home, care for a family, save money, stay healthy, prepare for whatever comes after work. The details vary enormously from one life to another, but the pattern is familiar: there is a future state we are trying to reach, and today's decisions help move us toward it. Having something next gives shape to time. It tells us what to do on Monday morning.

This can continue for decades without feeling particularly strange. The early goals give way to larger ones, and the problems we once hoped to have someday become the problems we complain about now. The entry-level employee wants responsibility, the experienced employee wants influence, and the manager who once wanted influence may eventually wonder why every decision now requires three meetings. Progress has a way of turning yesterday's aspirations into today's calendar.

Then something can change in middle age. It is not necessarily a crisis, and it does not require buying a sports car, quitting a job, or making a dramatic announcement to bewildered family members. Sometimes it is quieter than that. You simply notice that you already know what the next achievement is going to feel like.

There are still goals available. There will always be another promotion, another financial milestone, another project, another improvement to the house, another place to travel, another level of fitness, or another skill to learn. Some of them may be worth pursuing, and some may bring genuine pleasure. But the assumption that the next one will answer something important begins to weaken.

This is a strange place to arrive because nothing is obviously wrong. In fact, the unsettling part may be how much went right. The system worked.

## The Next Thing

When we are young, many of life's objectives are supplied for us. There is another grade, another semester, another test, another graduation, and eventually the question of what we are going to do for work. The path may be difficult or uncertain, but it contains visible markers. We know what progress is supposed to look like.

Work quickly creates another sequence. Learn the job, become reliable, take on more difficult assignments, earn the next level, perhaps lead a project or manage people. Each stage introduces problems that the previous version of us would not have known how to solve. That can be frustrating while it is happening, but it also creates a powerful sense of movement.

Meanwhile, the rest of life develops its own milestones. There may be relationships, children, a home, financial responsibilities, health concerns, aging parents, and the slow construction of a future that once seemed impossibly distant. A person can spend thirty years with legitimate reasons to think ahead. There is always something that needs building, protecting, fixing, or preparing.

These goals are not merely products of a shallow achievement culture. A mortgage represents a place to live, savings can protect people we love, and professional competence can make us useful to others as well as ourselves. Raising children, caring for a family, and becoming someone other people can depend upon are hardly empty accomplishments. The mistake would be treating every form of striving as evidence that we failed to understand what matters.

Still, goals create a particular orientation toward time. The present becomes the place where we perform the actions that will create the future. We tolerate difficult seasons because they are going somewhere, delay gratification because it will matter later, and make sacrifices whose value depends partly on what they make possible. This is one of adulthood's necessary disciplines.

Eventually, though, later begins arriving. The person who once wondered whether they would ever become competent now has decades of experience. The retirement account that began with almost nothing has become a serious number, and the children who once required constant attention may have lives increasingly their own. Some of the things we were preparing for are no longer future events.

The strange part is that arrival rarely feels like arriving. There is no music, no credits rolling, and no authoritative voice announcing that the building phase of adulthood has been successfully completed. Tuesday continues to be Tuesday. So we do what mastery has taught us to do and look for the next thing.

## The Strange Disappointment of Success

We have many stories for what happens when people fail. Someone worked hard but never got the opportunity, made a serious mistake, lost a job, experienced illness, or watched circumstances dismantle a future they expected to have. These experiences can force questions about meaning because the old story no longer works. When the plan collapses, we have little choice but to ask what remains.

Success can produce a quieter version of the same question. Suppose you receive the promotion you wanted. There may be congratulations, a salary increase, a celebratory dinner, and a genuine feeling that years of work have been recognized. Then you wake up a few mornings later and discover that the promotion has become your job.

The same thing happens with money. There may have been a number that once represented security to you, a balance so large that reaching it seemed as though it would permanently alter your relationship with the future. Years pass, contributions accumulate, markets do whatever markets do, and one day the account crosses the line. You notice, perhaps tell your spouse, feel pleased for a while, and make coffee.

This is not evidence that the achievement was meaningless. The money may create real security, and the promotion may provide opportunities you value. A goal can deliver almost exactly what it promised and still fail to produce the larger feeling we quietly attached to it. The problem was not necessarily the goal; it may have been the amount of existential work we expected the goal to perform.

There is a particular kind of disappointment in getting what you wanted and discovering that it feels normal surprisingly quickly. Psychologists have language for this, but most of us do not need technical terminology to recognize the experience. The new house becomes the house, the new car becomes the car, and the new title eventually becomes the line beneath our name in an email signature. Human beings are remarkably talented at turning miracles into furniture.

That adaptation is useful. We could not function if every achievement produced permanent emotional intensity, and nobody wants to spend twenty years walking through the kitchen in astonishment that they own a refrigerator. Familiarity allows us to move forward. But it also means that achievement cannot permanently sustain the feeling of arrival we sometimes expect from it.

This is where the middle-aged achiever can become confused. We did what we were supposed to do, and much of it worked. We learned the system, developed competence, accepted responsibility, made plans, solved problems, and built something resembling the life we once hoped to have. Why, then, does the question remain?

Perhaps because the tools that help us build a life cannot tell us what the life is for. A hammer can help construct a house without knowing what makes the house a home. A retirement calculator can help prepare for the future without knowing what we hope to find when we get there. Mastery can bring us remarkably far without answering the question of what counts as arrival.

## When More Stops Answering the Question

Numbers have a useful property that becomes troublesome around this point: they continue. There is always a number larger than the one we have. If the investment account reaches one hundred thousand dollars, two hundred thousand becomes imaginable; after that comes five hundred thousand, then a million, then two. Arithmetic has no natural place to stop.

The same is true in less obviously numerical parts of life. There is always another level of professional responsibility, another improvement in fitness, another accomplishment, another credential, another experience worth having. Even goals that are not expressed numerically can participate in the logic of more. Whatever we have done, there is something we have not done yet.

This does not mean we should stop growing. Curiosity, ambition, and improvement can remain sources of energy throughout a long life. The question is whether more is still serving something we understand or has become the default answer because we no longer know what else to ask. A direction can continue long after we have forgotten the destination.

Money makes the problem unusually clear. “How much do I have?” is a question measurement can answer with remarkable precision. “How much is enough?” sounds similar but belongs to an entirely different category. The second question cannot be answered until we add two more words: **Enough for what?**

Enough to retire at a certain age requires assumptions about spending, longevity, investment returns, taxes, health care, and the life we expect to live. Enough to feel safe may have much less to do with arithmetic. Enough to impress other people could become an extraordinarily expensive project with no obvious completion date. The number cannot decide which version of enough we mean.

Health has the same hidden question. I may want to lose weight because I want greater mobility, lower health risks, more energy, or a body that supports the life I hope to live. Those purposes can guide sensible goals. But if the number itself becomes the purpose, every lower number invites the possibility of lower still.

The question *How much?* belongs naturally to measurement. The question *Enough for what?* requires meaning. For the first time in our journey, improving the measurement cannot solve the problem because the problem exists one level above it.

That can be uncomfortable for people who are accustomed to making progress by gathering better information. We want to calculate the answer, establish the target, and get moving again. If meaning would simply provide a specification, we could probably build a very respectable plan around it. Instead, meaning has an irritating tendency to ask us what we actually care about.

## The Story We Spent Our Lives Creating

Perhaps the first part of adulthood is necessarily devoted to creating a story. We establish an identity, learn what we can do, form relationships, develop commitments, and gradually become recognizable to ourselves and other people. I am an engineer, a spouse, a parent, a friend, a leader, a musician, a person who believes certain things and tries to live in certain ways. The story gives coherence to a life that might otherwise feel like a collection of unrelated days.

We need that story. A young person cannot spend forever contemplating the infinite possibilities of existence; eventually they have to choose something, even knowing the choice will close other doors. Careers require specialization, relationships require commitment, and communities depend upon people becoming sufficiently stable that others can know what to expect from them. A life acquires shape partly because we stop trying to be every possible person.

For years, much of our energy goes into creating that shape. We build the career, establish the household, learn the responsibilities, develop routines, accumulate history, and become the person whose name now belongs to all of those things. The story is not invented out of nothing. It emerges from choices interacting with circumstances, other people, opportunities, accidents, and all the causes we encountered in the previous chapter.

Then, somewhere along the way, we begin commenting on the story we created. Why did this accomplishment matter so much to me when I was thirty? Why did that failure change the direction of my life? Why do I remember an unplanned afternoon with someone I loved more vividly than a project that consumed two years?

These are different questions from the ones that built the story. Earlier we asked what career to pursue, whether to take the job, how to solve the problem, where to live, and what goal should come next. Now we begin asking what all those choices have meant. We move from constructing a life toward interpreting one.

Meaning often appears differently when we look backward. The events that seemed most important while they were happening do not always become the memories that matter most. A promotion may blur together with several others, while a conversation in a parking lot remains vivid twenty years later. The spreadsheet and the memory seem to have used different criteria for significance.

This does not mean the memory is correct and the spreadsheet was wrong. The promotion may have changed income, responsibility, and the direction of a career in measurable ways. The conversation may have changed something harder to describe. Both belong to the story, but they reveal that significance is not distributed according to a single scale.

Middle age gives us enough story to begin seeing patterns we could not see while we were busy creating it. We notice what endured, what faded, what cost more than expected, and what became valuable almost by accident. The life behind us begins asking questions of the life ahead. The next thing is no longer automatically the answer.

## The Empty Space

The competent response to this realization is, naturally, to develop a plan. If work no longer provides enough meaning, perhaps I need a hobby, a second career, a volunteer role, a consulting practice, more travel, or a spiritual discipline. These may all be excellent ideas. Give an experienced professional an existential crisis and there is a decent chance they will have milestones established by Friday.

Even our attempts to become less achievement-oriented can retain the structure of achievement. We can meditate twenty minutes a day, complete a list of important books, schedule meaningful experiences, and set goals for spending quality time with people we love. Soon we are making excellent progress toward becoming the sort of person who no longer needs to make excellent progress. Mastery is remarkably adaptable.

I recognize the impulse because doing something feels better than not knowing. Problems are comforting in a peculiar way because they imply solutions. If I am lonely, dissatisfied, restless, or uncertain, I want to identify the root cause and begin corrective action. The empty space becomes another defect waiting for triage.

But perhaps not knowing what comes next is not always a defect. There may be periods of life when the old organizing story has reached its boundary and the next one has not yet taken shape. The discomfort comes partly from our unfamiliarity with standing somewhere that does not contain a visible milestone. We have spent decades moving toward things.

This does not mean we should romanticize confusion or remain passive when change is needed. A bad job may need to be left, a damaged relationship may need attention, and depression does not become wisdom simply because it produces existential questions. Sometimes there really is a problem, and competent action is exactly what the situation requires. Not every empty feeling contains a hidden spiritual invitation.

Still, there are questions that become distorted when we demand immediate resolution. *What do I want the rest of my life to be about?* may deserve more than a weekend planning session. *What have I learned from the life I built?* is not improved by converting it immediately into three objectives and twelve key results. Some questions may need to accompany us for a while before they begin revealing what they are asking.

For the achiever, this can feel almost irresponsible. We are accustomed to treating uncertainty as something to reduce and ambiguity as something that delays execution. Yet the space between one story and another may require exactly what our training taught us to avoid: remaining somewhere without knowing what it will produce. We may have to resist filling the silence simply because silence does not look productive.

Perhaps that is what this stage of life is asking from us. Not the abandonment of everything we built, and not a dramatic rejection of the person we became. Something quieter is happening. The tools that helped us construct the story are no longer sufficient to interpret it.

## Outward and Upward

For most of my adult life, when I did not know what to do, I looked for the next thing. There was usually something available: another project, another problem, another goal, another number that could be moved in the right direction. That habit served me remarkably well. It helped me build a career, care for people I love, solve difficult problems, and create a life for which I am deeply grateful.

But the posture of constant improvement does something to attention. We spend a great deal of time looking toward what is missing: the work not finished, the money not yet saved, the weight not yet lost, the problem not yet solved. This is useful because gaps tell us where action is required. It can also make the life already present strangely difficult to see.

Sometimes what interrupts that posture is very ordinary. You are having breakfast with someone you have known for decades and realize there is nowhere else you need to be for the next hour. A piece of music catches you unexpectedly, or light comes through the trees in a way that makes you stop walking for a moment. Nothing has improved, yet something has happened.

These moments are difficult to fit into the system we have built throughout this book. They do not necessarily advance a goal, provide useful feedback, or leave us better prepared for the next milestone. Often their value seems connected to the fact that we were present enough to notice them. They ask for attention rather than optimization.

I do not think this means the answer to midlife is to become more mindful, as though presence were another competency we could acquire and add to the annual development plan. That would simply give us another number to move. The possibility is more unsettling and, I think, more interesting. Perhaps some parts of life are encountered most fully when we stop requiring them to take us somewhere else.

This is not how most of us learned to become adults. We learned to act upon the world, make choices, accept consequences, develop competence, and build a future. Those abilities remain part of us, and nothing ahead requires us to surrender them. But perhaps adulthood has another lesson after mastery.

For years, I assumed that if I kept improving the parts, eventually they would assemble themselves into an answer. Better work, better health, more security, more wisdom, better habits, and enough progress might eventually produce the feeling that I had arrived. I am less certain now that arrival works that way. Perhaps the question is not what I should accomplish next.

I had spent years learning how to act upon the world, and perhaps there was something else to learn about simply being in it. Not instead of competence or responsibility, and not as a retreat from adulthood. Something on the other side of them. I did not yet have a metric for it, and I was not even sure I had a name for it.

Maybe that was the point.
