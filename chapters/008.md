# Chapter 8

## When the Number Becomes Me

### The Grammar Changes

Something happens to a number when we repeat it often enough.

At first, the language is straightforward. I weigh 247 pounds. A scale collected some information about my body at a particular moment and reported it back to me.

Then the grammar changes.

I am fat.

Something important just happened. The number disappeared. So did the scale. A measurement became a description, and the description became an identity.

We do this remarkably easily. I received a poor performance rating becomes *I'm a poor performer*. My book didn't sell very well becomes *I'm not a very good writer*. The movement is subtle enough that we may not notice it, but those are very different claims.

We've seen this kind of collapse throughout the book. A company measures software defects, and defects begin to stand for quality. It measures AI usage, and usage becomes adoption, which eventually becomes progress. Each step compresses reality a little further until the proxy and the thing it represents become almost indistinguishable in our language.

Now we're doing the same thing with a human being.

Except the territory is me.

That's why this particular collapse matters so much. If we shouldn't expect a single metric to completely describe the health of an organization, why would we expect one to describe a person?

People are considerably messier than dashboards. I have written good code and bad code. I've made wise decisions and foolish ones. I've been disciplined and undisciplined, courageous and afraid, focused and distracted, insightful and completely wrong.

Sometimes before lunch.

Which one is me?

All of it, apparently.

A metric solves this complexity by compressing it. That's what metrics do, and it's why they're useful. A performance rating takes an enormous amount of work and reduces it to something an organization can manage. A weight measurement reduces one physical property of my body to a number. A sales ranking compresses the commercial performance of a book.

The usefulness comes partly from what gets left out.

The danger begins when I forget the leaving-out part.

Suppose I receive a disappointing performance rating at work. Maybe the rating is fair. That's important to acknowledge. Maybe I didn't perform as well as I thought. Maybe I missed commitments, exercised poor judgment, or have something I genuinely need to improve.

Even if the rating is completely fair, though, it remains an evaluation of my performance within a particular role, during a particular period, according to particular expectations, interpreted through a particular system.

That's already a lot of qualifiers.

The human mind is wonderfully efficient at removing them.

I got a low rating. That becomes *I'm not doing well*. After a little more processing, it becomes *I'm not very good at this*. Then, if we're having a particularly productive evening, we can arrive at *maybe I'm not very good at anything*.

That escalated quickly.

Nothing in the performance system actually calculated the final statement. There was no field labeled:

*Overall Value of Michael as a Human Being: Below Expectations.*

I added that myself.

This is part of what gives metrics such psychological power. Numbers feel authoritative. They are precise, visible, and comparable. They arrive with the quiet confidence of arithmetic, so when a number says something uncomfortable about me, it can feel more trustworthy than my broader understanding of myself.

The number is objective. I am obviously biased in favor of me.

Case closed.

Except the number isn't making most of the claims I'm attributing to it.

The scale says I weigh 247 pounds. It does not say I am lazy. A performance rating says something about how my work was evaluated. It does not say I am unintelligent. A sales figure says how many copies of a book sold. It does not say whether the book was worth writing.

We add those meanings. Because the meanings feel connected to the measurement, they borrow some of its authority.

That doesn't mean the meanings are always false. Maybe my habits really do need more discipline. Maybe my work needs improvement. Maybe readers genuinely aren't connecting with something I've written.

The point isn't to protect myself from every uncomfortable conclusion. It's to notice when I've crossed from measurement into meaning, because meaning requires more evidence than a number can provide.

This becomes particularly difficult when a measurement follows us around. My weight appears every morning. My performance rating follows me through the year. My job title sits beneath my name. Repetition gives these measurements a kind of permanence, and eventually they can begin answering a question they were never designed to answer.

Who am I?

**A measurement can be true without being the truth about you.**

That isn't sentimental. It's simply an accurate description of what measurement does. A metric is a compressed representation of some dimension of reality, and a human being doesn't suddenly become an exception to that rule.

I can take a measurement seriously. I can learn from it, change because of it, and even admit that it reveals something about me I would rather not see.

But I should be careful when the verb changes.

I weigh. I did. I received. I have.

Those are statements about things that can be measured.

*I am* is a much bigger claim, and no dashboard we've invented is remotely qualified to finish that sentence.

### Some Numbers Can Hurt You

There is an easy piece of advice I could give at this point.

Don't let the numbers define you. Your performance rating doesn't determine your worth. Your salary doesn't determine your worth. Your ranking doesn't determine your worth.

Beautiful.

Put it on a mug.

There is only one problem: some of these numbers can hurt you.

A performance rating may not determine my worth as a human being, but it can affect my raise or bonus. It can influence whether I get promoted, which opportunities I'm offered, and how future managers perceive me. In some organizations, it can influence whether I continue to have a job.

That's not imaginary.

I can remind myself that I am not my performance rating while driving home from work, and I think that's healthy. But if that rating affects whether I can pay the mortgage, the number has acquired a certain amount of legitimate psychological significance.

This is where corporate metrics differ from many of the personal metrics we've been discussing. If I miss my step goal, my watch cannot fire me. It may vibrate disapprovingly, but its enforcement powers are limited.

Work is different because authority is attached to the measurement.

A manager may look at a productivity number and see a data point worth investigating. An employee can look at the same number and see the beginning of a chain of consequences.

My productivity is down. My manager will notice. Maybe they'll think I'm underperforming. Maybe that affects my rating. What if I lose my job?

We've gone from a dashboard to unemployment in approximately eight seconds.

That may be catastrophizing. It may also reflect the fact that jobs matter.

For most people, employment isn't merely a scorekeeping system. A paycheck becomes housing, food, healthcare, transportation, savings, and security for people we love. A performance metric can therefore reach parts of life that seem very far removed from the thing being measured.

A number on a dashboard can find its way into the kitchen.

Understanding that helps explain why employees sometimes react strongly to measurements that look relatively harmless from the other side. Management may genuinely be saying, "We're trying to understand performance." The employee may hear, "Someone is trying to understand whether I'm worth keeping."

Those aren't the same experience.

This doesn't mean every question from a manager is an accusation or every red metric is the beginning of a termination process. Sometimes a manager really is just asking what happened.

But authority creates ambiguity. When the person asking the question also influences my compensation, opportunities, reputation, and continued employment, I cannot hear it exactly as I would if a coworker asked the same thing over coffee.

That helps explain something else we've already observed. People don't merely optimize for rewards.

**People optimize for safety.**

Suppose an engineer knows that bug-closure time is receiving increased attention. Taking a complicated bug might mean spending three weeks producing one closure. Taking several straightforward bugs will make the dashboard look considerably better.

We've already discussed why the measurement can make that behavior rational. What matters here is the emotional mechanism underneath it.

The engineer is protecting themselves.

That doesn't automatically make the choice admirable. People remain responsible for their decisions. But if a number affects my future, I will pay attention to how I look through that number. If I believe the system cannot see the full complexity of my work, I may become particularly anxious about the parts it can see.

This is why telling employees to simply detach their identity from performance metrics can sound hollow. The organization itself is telling them that the metric matters. It tracks the number, discusses it, compares it, and sometimes attaches rewards or consequences to it.

The employee did not invent that authority.

So we have to hold two truths together. The metric does not determine my worth, and the metric may have real consequences for my life. Pretending the second isn't true turns wisdom into denial. Pretending the first isn't true gives the organization far more authority than it deserves.

The challenge is to take the number seriously without allowing it to become total.

And before we put all the blame for that tension on management, we need to walk around to the other side of the dashboard.

Organizations have a problem too.

### The Organization Has a Problem Too

Businesses have limited resources.

There may be five employees ready for promotion and two available positions. Ten worthwhile projects may be competing for enough funding to support six. A hundred people may be doing valuable work inside a compensation budget that cannot reward all of them equally.

Someone has to decide.

"Everyone is wonderful" turns out to be a poor resource-allocation strategy.

Organizations therefore need ways to distinguish among projects, investments, teams, and employees. Which projects are producing results? Which products are growing? Where are costs increasing? Which employees are performing well? Where should the next dollar or person go?

Those are legitimate questions. In a large organization, they're unavoidable.

Measurement helps answer them.

In fact, the alternative to measurement isn't necessarily a kinder, more human workplace. Imagine eliminating performance metrics entirely. No documented goals, no structured evaluation, no attempt to compare outcomes. Managers simply decide who seems to be doing well.

Based on what?

Memory and impression, presumably. Who spoke most confidently in the meeting? Who happens to be working on the project receiving executive attention? Who has the best relationship with the manager? Who sends email at 11:47 p.m. so everyone knows how committed they are?

That system has metrics too.

They're just invisible.

Measurement can provide evidence against favoritism, charisma, recency bias, and office politics. It can make expectations clearer and give employees something more concrete than whether the boss seems pleased.

Numbers don't automatically make a system fair. Removing numbers doesn't automatically make it fair either.

That's worth remembering because it's easy to criticize corporate measurement from the receiving end. I've certainly done it. Engineers can be remarkably sophisticated critics of management metrics, particularly when those metrics suggest we should change something we'd prefer not to change.

Sometimes we're right. Sometimes the metric really is terrible.

And sometimes management is trying to solve a legitimate problem that we have the luxury of not having to solve.

Someone has to allocate resources. Someone has to decide whether what we're doing is working. Someone has to look beyond my individual experience and try to understand the larger system.

That's part of what management is for.

Businesses also have to survive. They have customers, costs, payroll, competitors, and people who have invested money with the expectation that leadership will use it responsibly. Growth, profit, efficiency, and productivity aren't embarrassing concepts that we need to apologize for.

Number go up, as it turns out, is not an entirely ridiculous objective.

If revenue never rises, costs continually increase, productivity steadily falls, and nobody particularly cares because the employees are having meaningful experiences, we may have accidentally created a retreat center.

Possibly a very nice one.

But not a particularly durable business.

The problem throughout this book has never been that organizations want things to improve. The problem appears when the thing we can optimize becomes a substitute for the thing we actually want to improve.

Managers live inside that tension too.

It's tempting to imagine the manager on the other side of the dashboard gleefully reducing complicated human beings to cells in a spreadsheet. Most of the managers I've known are considerably more aware of the compression than that.

A manager may know that one employee took the ugly project nobody wanted. They know another spent much of the year mentoring younger engineers. They know someone else inherited a disaster and somehow kept it from becoming a catastrophe. Two employees may have similar visible numbers and very different years.

Then the performance system asks:

Rating?

Eventually the box needs a number.

The manager may understand everything that disappeared in order to produce it. They still have to fill in the box.

That's the managerial version of the problem we've been discussing throughout this book. Scale requires compression even when the person doing the compressing understands its limitations.

**The organization experiences a resource-allocation problem. The employee experiences a life.**

Neither perspective makes the other disappear.

The organization needs some way to make decisions at scale. The employee experiences the consequences of those decisions at full resolution. A manager may need the map while understanding perfectly well that the employee lives in the territory.

This is why I don't think the answer is to demand that organizations somehow preserve the full complexity of every human being in every decision. They can't. A large organization that attempted to understand every decision at full resolution would eventually stop making decisions.

The better question is what we do after compression occurs.

Do we remember what was lost?

Can context still enter the conversation?

Can a manager look at an unfavorable number and ask what happened before deciding what it means?

Most importantly, can we examine a bad number without immediately turning it into a bad person?

Accountability doesn't require that collapse.

"This result needs to improve" is not the same statement as "you are the problem." A healthy measurement system needs enough room for the first sentence without quietly teaching the second.

And a healthy relationship with measurement needs the same room inside us.

### When the Number Becomes a Verdict

One of the reasons metrics become so personal is that they make comparison easy.

That's another thing metrics are good at.

If I want to compare two complicated human beings in their entirety, I have a problem. Each has a history, abilities, relationships, circumstances, strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, fears, and several decades of decisions.

Good luck putting that in a bar chart.

So we compress.

Employee A has a higher performance rating than Employee B. Author A sold more books than Author B. Person A weighs less than Person B.

Now we can compare.

**Comparison requires compression.**

That isn't necessarily bad. Sometimes the dimension we're comparing is exactly the dimension that matters for the decision. If I'm trying to determine which salesperson sold more, sales numbers seem relevant.

The problem comes when the comparison starts making claims beyond the dimension that survived the compression.

This becomes especially dangerous when I compare myself with other people, because I have radically different amounts of information about the two sides.

I know my own territory.

I know the project that went badly, the conversation I mishandled, the work I haven't finished, the compromises I made, the opportunities I missed, and the things I'm still trying to figure out.

Then I look at someone else and see the promotion.

Or the book ranking.

Or the body.

Or the salary.

I compare my territory with their map.

Of course the map looks cleaner.

The same problem appears when we rank ourselves indefinitely. There is almost always another comparison available. Sell ten thousand books and someone sold a hundred thousand. Earn more money and someone earns more. Get promoted and someone younger already has the next title.

The finish line moves because the comparison set can always expand.

This is one reason a measurement can move so easily from information to verdict. It doesn't merely tell me where I am. It tells me where I am relative to someone else, and then I supply the meaning.

Behind.

Ahead.

Successful.

Failing.

Enough.

Not enough.

But those conclusions are larger than the comparison itself.

This is where I want to be careful not to escape into denial. If someone else consistently performs better than I do at work, that may be worth understanding. If another author reaches more readers, perhaps there is something I can learn. If my health measurements are poor, pretending otherwise isn't self-compassion.

The number can be right.

It just doesn't have to become a verdict on me.

Suppose I receive a disappointing performance rating and, after the defensiveness settles, conclude that it's fair. I missed commitments. I could have communicated better. There are things I need to change.

Fine.

Now I have useful information.

I don't need to convert that information into *I am a failure* before I'm allowed to improve. In fact, turning the measurement into identity may make improvement harder because the problem becomes much larger than the behavior.

There's a difference between saying, "I need to become more reliable about commitments" and "I'm unreliable."

The first gives me somewhere to act.

The second starts describing who I am.

The same is true with weight. If my current weight creates health risks or keeps me from living the way I want, I can take that seriously. I can change what I eat, move more, and work toward a healthier body without making contempt for my current body the fuel.

Wanting something to change doesn't require hating the thing I'm trying to change.

This is where the grammar from the beginning of the chapter becomes useful again.

I received.

I weigh.

I did.

I have.

Those statements leave room for movement.

*I am* has a way of closing the door.

No metric is qualified to answer the question, *Am I enough?* Not because every number will eventually turn out favorably, but because the question is larger than the instrument.

The performance system can tell me something about my work.

The scale can tell me something about my body.

Sales can tell me something about the commercial response to a book.

Let them.

They may know something I need to hear.

Just don't ask them to know more than they know.

### Hold the Number With Open Hands

So what do we do with a number that matters?

Not throw it away.

Not pretend it can't hurt.

Not dismiss it because context exists.

And not grip it so tightly that it becomes an identity.

We hold it with open hands.

A performance rating may tell me I need to improve. I can listen.

A scale may tell me that the long-term trend is moving in the wrong direction. I can respond.

Book sales may tell me that something I wrote didn't connect with many readers. I can learn from that too.

The number gets to speak.

It just doesn't get to speak alone.

My performance rating belongs inside the larger story of my career. My career belongs inside the larger story of my life. My weight belongs inside the larger reality of my health, body, relationships, habits, and experience. Book sales belong inside the experience of writing something, sharing it, learning from it, and deciding whether the work was worth doing.

The order matters.

When I reverse it, the life starts fitting itself inside the measurement. A career becomes a rating. Health becomes weight. Writing becomes sales. The territory starts shrinking until it fits neatly inside the map.

That's backwards.

**Put the measurement back inside the life instead of putting the life inside the measurement.**

This allows me to take the number more seriously, not less. I don't have to spend all my energy defending myself from it. I can say, "That's true," without adding, "and therefore this is all that is true."

That distinction creates room for honesty.

My performance was poor this year.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

My weight is higher than I want it to be.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

This book didn't reach as many people as I hoped.

That's true.

And it's not all that's true.

The second sentence doesn't erase the first. It puts it back in proportion.

Metrics are particularly bad at proportion because their usefulness comes from isolation. They take one dimension of reality and make it visible enough to examine. That's what we asked them to do.

We shouldn't be surprised when they fail to bring the rest of the life along.

That's our job.

I think this is one of the reasons the map-and-territory distinction has kept returning throughout this book. At first it was a way to think about organizations. A dashboard is a map of a complicated business. It leaves things out so a manager can see patterns and make decisions.

Then the metaphor became personal.

My scale is a map.

My performance rating is a map.

My salary, job title, sales figures, and all the other numbers that accumulate around a modern life are maps too. They contain real information about real parts of the territory.

I need some of them.

I would be foolish to insist that because a map is incomplete, it therefore tells me nothing.

But I would be equally foolish to forget what the map is.

When the number looks good, I can enjoy it without needing it to prove that I'm good.

When the number looks bad, I can learn from it without requiring it to prove that I'm bad.

Either way, the territory remains larger.

And in this case, I am the territory.
