# 5 — I Became My Own Manager

The workday ends, but the measurements do not. I close the laptop, stand up from the desk, and glance at my watch to see how many steps I have taken. Later I might log dinner, check my investment accounts, notice how much time I spent on my phone, and think about whether I exercised enough that day. Tomorrow morning there will be another number waiting for me on the bathroom scale. Somewhere along the way, the dashboard followed me home.

None of this feels particularly strange because measurement has become part of ordinary life. Watches count our steps, phones report our screen time, apps track calories, scales estimate body composition, and financial sites can tell us our net worth to the dollar. We can measure sleep, exercise, reading, meditation, spending, productivity, and an impressive collection of bodily functions that previous generations managed to experience without receiving a weekly trend report. Much of this information is genuinely useful. I have voluntarily collected quite a bit of it myself.

The appeal should be familiar by now. Measurement gives us feedback, and feedback helps us make better decisions. If I want to become healthier, knowing what I eat and how much I move can expose the difference between what I imagine I am doing and what I actually do. If I want financial security, knowing where my money goes is considerably more useful than hoping things work out. The same feedback loop that helps an organization improve can help an individual change.

There is one important difference, though. At work, the person examining the dashboard and the person represented by the dashboard are usually different people. At home, I occupy both positions. I set the goals, collect the measurements, review the results, determine whether performance is acceptable, and decide what corrective action should be taken.

I became my own manager.

## The Dashboard Comes Home

The transition happened gradually enough that I never really noticed it. I wanted to lose weight, so I bought a scale. I wanted to understand what I was eating, so I logged food. I wanted to move more, so I wore a device that counted my steps. Each decision made sense by itself, and together they created a surprisingly comprehensive performance-management system for Michael.

Money followed a similar path. For much of adult life, financial progress is naturally expressed through numbers because money is already numerical. We track income, spending, mortgage balances, investment returns, savings rates, and eventually the amount we believe we will need for retirement. A financial dashboard can tell us in seconds what once would have required gathering statements from several institutions and spending an evening with a calculator.

The technology is remarkable. My watch knows that I went for a walk, my phone knows how long I slept, my food app knows what I ate, and my brokerage account knows whether the market has been kind to me today. Each system provides a small window into some aspect of my life. Put enough windows together and it begins to feel as though I ought to be able to see the whole thing.

There are good reasons for wanting that view. Bodies are complicated, money accumulates slowly, and habits are notoriously difficult to perceive from inside the habit itself. A person can sincerely believe they exercise regularly while discovering that “regularly” means twice last month. The dashboard can challenge the stories we tell ourselves just as effectively at home as it does at work.

The language changes, but the underlying method is almost identical. Establish the desired outcome, identify the behaviors likely to produce it, measure the relevant indicators, and adjust when progress stalls. If this sounds suspiciously like something that might appear in a corporate planning document, that is because it is. We have taken one of the most successful technologies of adulthood and pointed it inward.

## What Gets Measured Gets Done

I don't want to dismiss the benefits of doing this because I have experienced them. There is a substantial difference between believing I am eating reasonably well and recording everything I eat for a week. The handful grabbed while standing in the kitchen suddenly exists, as does the snack that barely registered because I ate it while doing something else. Measurement can make behavior visible before I have had time to edit it into a more flattering story.

That visibility can be liberating rather than punitive. If I know what is actually happening, I can make a decision based on reality instead of guilt or vague impressions. Maybe breakfast is fine and the problem is what happens after dinner, or perhaps the restaurant meals I considered occasional are happening four times a week. The data does not have to accuse me of anything. It can simply help me locate the problem.

Exercise works similarly. A goal to “move more” is easy to admire and difficult to evaluate, while a record of actual activity gives the intention some structure. Putting workouts on a calendar increases the chance that they will happen, and tracking them can reveal whether an ambitious plan bears any resemblance to the life I actually have. Sometimes the most useful thing a number can do is make a vague promise concrete.

Financial measurement may be an even clearer example. Most of us would not recommend that someone prepare for retirement by developing a deep intuitive relationship with their checking account. We need to know how much we have, how much we are saving, what we spend, and whether the trajectory is remotely compatible with the future we imagine. A number can reveal a problem decades before direct experience would make the problem painfully obvious.

There is discipline in this kind of attention. Measurement can help us resist the tendency to confuse wanting something with doing what is required to pursue it. The scale, calendar, bank statement, and food log can all provide feedback that our preferences alone cannot. Reality gets a vote at home too.

This is why abandoning self-measurement would be a strange conclusion to draw from its limitations. I am still interested in my health, finances, habits, and use of time, and I want accurate information about them. The question is not whether we should know things about ourselves. It is what happens when knowing becomes managing, and managing becomes the primary way we experience the life being measured.

## When the Walk Becomes Steps

Suppose I go for a walk on a pleasant morning. The air is cool, the neighborhood is quiet, and I notice someone working in a garden as I pass. A dog investigates me from behind a fence, sunlight moves through the trees, and for half an hour I am simply outside moving through the world. Then I arrive home and look at my watch: 3,842 steps.

The number is true. Those steps contribute to my physical activity, and if I am trying to become healthier, knowing how much I move can be useful. The watch has not ruined the walk by counting it. Nothing about the trees becomes less beautiful because an accelerometer was operating on my wrist.

But attention is a limited resource. Once I decide that steps matter, I begin noticing the walk differently. I may choose a longer route because I am behind for the day, circle the block once more because I am close to a goal, or pace around the kitchen in the evening because the little ring on my watch remains stubbornly incomplete. The measurement that originally described my movement has begun influencing it.

Again, that may be exactly what I wanted. If the goal was to become less sedentary, walking around the kitchen instead of sitting on the couch is probably a successful intervention. The oddness appears when an unrecorded walk starts to feel less real, or when I finish a beautiful afternoon outside and experience the primary accomplishment as closing a ring. The representation has begun competing with the experience for my attention.

We do this in surprisingly many areas of life. A book becomes one of the fifty books we intend to finish this year, sleep becomes a score we check before deciding how rested we feel, and a meal becomes a collection of calories and macronutrients before it becomes dinner. None of these descriptions is false. The question is how much authority we give them over the experience they describe.

There is a subtle shift from using measurement to support an activity toward doing the activity for the measurement. I start walking because I want better health, then discover that I am walking because I need another twelve hundred steps. The proxy is easier to see than the purpose, and therefore easier to pursue. The same transformation we watched happen inside organizations can happen inside a single person.

## The Annual Review Happens Every Morning

Few personal measurements demonstrate this better than the bathroom scale. I am trying to lose a substantial amount of weight, which makes body weight a relevant piece of information. If the long-term trend never moves downward, something about my approach probably needs to change. Pretending the number does not matter would not make me healthier.

The trouble is that the scale provides its feedback daily while the outcome I care about unfolds over months and years. I can eat well, exercise, make every choice I intended to make, step on the scale the next morning, and discover that I weigh more. I can also eat poorly, do very little, and receive a lower number the next day. The body contains enough water, food, glycogen, inflammation, and ordinary biological variation to make short-term moral accounting a particularly bad use of a bathroom appliance.

Yet it is remarkably easy to conduct the accounting anyway. The number goes down and I feel successful. The number goes up and I begin searching yesterday for the mistake, even when yesterday may have very little to do with what appeared on the display this morning. A measurement of body mass quietly becomes an evaluation of performance.

If I managed another person this way, I hope someone would eventually intervene. Imagine giving an employee clear expectations, watching them meet every one of them, and then announcing that they had failed because an outcome influenced by dozens of other variables moved the wrong direction overnight. The next day they ignore the expectations, the outcome happens to improve, and I congratulate them on excellent execution. This would not be considered an especially sophisticated management system.

Still, I can do something very similar to myself before breakfast. The absurdity is harder to see because I am simultaneously the manager who chose the metric and the employee whose mood is affected by it. I know intellectually that the number is noisy, yet the emotional response can arrive before the statistical analysis. The dashboard has acquired an authority I never explicitly gave it.

This is one of the peculiar features of self-measurement. There is no manager to blame for choosing the wrong metric because I chose it. There is no employee to reassure that the number is only information because I am the employee too. I can resent a performance-management system that I personally designed, purchased, and placed on the bathroom floor.

The scale is not the villain in this story. It is doing exactly what I asked it to do and reporting the result with admirable indifference. The more interesting question is why a useful piece of information can so quickly become a verdict. Somewhere between reading the number and brushing my teeth, “What does this tell me?” can become “What does this say about how I'm doing?”

## Life as a Project

Once you become comfortable managing one part of yourself, there is no obvious reason to stop. Health can be optimized, finances can be optimized, and productivity can certainly be optimized. We can establish reading goals, sleep targets, morning routines, meditation streaks, screen-time limits, relationship habits, and systems for making sure we remember to maintain the systems. There is always another part of life available for improvement.

The self-improvement industry thrives on this possibility because the future self is an endlessly renewable project. There is always a version of me who wakes earlier, weighs less, reads more, saves more, exercises consistently, wastes less time, and handles difficult emotions with impressive serenity. That person remains just far enough ahead to provide direction. I can spend a lifetime pursuing him without ever having to decide when the project is finished.

Even attempts to escape optimization can become optimization projects. I can meditate so that I will become calmer, schedule unstructured time to improve creativity, take a vacation because rest will make me more productive, and practice gratitude because research suggests it improves well-being. The activity may be genuinely beneficial, but the mind that approaches it remains familiar. What will this produce?

There is nothing wrong with wanting to grow. Some things in my life need to change, and accepting myself does not require pretending every habit is equally healthy or every pattern equally worth preserving. The capacity to examine our lives and deliberately alter them is part of the mastery we spent the first half of this book learning to appreciate. I am grateful that I am not limited to whatever version of myself happened to emerge by accident.

But improvement creates a strange relationship with the present. If the purpose of today's walk is tomorrow's health, today's meal is next month's weight, today's savings are retirement, and today's work is the next promotion, then much of the present becomes raw material for constructing a future. The future matters, of course, but it is worth noticing how easily the life I am living becomes subordinate to the life I am trying to produce.

This can become especially pronounced in middle age. By then, many of us have spent decades developing the ability to identify gaps between current state and desired state. We know how to make plans, break goals into steps, track progress, and adjust when the trend line refuses to cooperate. Then we look at ourselves and discover the largest continuous-improvement project we have ever encountered.

There will always be something to work on. The body ages, finances change, careers evolve, relationships require attention, and habits we thought we had mastered have an irritating ability to return. If I wait until the project of myself is complete before I feel permitted to inhabit my life, I may be waiting for an event that never arrives. The finish line keeps moving because the person approaching it keeps changing.

This does not mean the goals are wrong. I still want to become healthier, use my time well, care for the people I love, make good financial decisions, and continue learning. The question beginning to trouble me is whether managing those things is the same as living them. Perhaps a life can be well managed and still experienced mostly as something that has not yet become what it should be.

For years, mastery taught me to ask a useful question whenever reality differed from what I wanted: *What can I change?* That question helped me solve problems, build a career, plan for the future, and take responsibility for myself. It remains a good question, and I expect to keep asking it.

But eventually life presents us with outcomes that refuse to appear simply because we followed the plan.

And then we have to ask a different question: **What is actually mine to control?**
