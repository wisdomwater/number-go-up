# 5 - Every Metric Is a Message

### We’re Just Tracking It

Most metrics begin innocently enough. Someone wants to know what is happening, perhaps because a new process has been introduced, a tool has been rolled out, or some part of the organization has become difficult to see from a distance. A manager asks for visibility, a team collects some data, and before long there is a chart that tells everyone whether the thing appears to be happening more or less than it did before.

There is nothing suspicious about this. In fact, it is one of the main reasons measurement is useful. Instead of relying on impressions, we can find out whether adoption really increased, whether quality actually improved, or whether a process change had the effect we hoped it would have. A number gives us something firmer than “it feels like people are using this more,” and that can be a genuine improvement in how an organization understands itself.

I think it is important to keep that starting point in mind, because conversations about metrics can become cartoonish very quickly. Management creates a dashboard, management becomes obsessed with the dashboard, employees suffer, and the moral of the story is that managers should have trusted people instead. Sometimes organizations really do behave that badly, but most managers I have known are dealing with a much less theatrical problem: they are responsible for more reality than they can directly observe.

They have projects to coordinate, people to develop, commitments to manage, risks to anticipate, and decisions to make with information that is always incomplete. It would be strange if they did not want data. The complication is that the information a manager sees as useful visibility can feel quite different to the person whose work is being represented by it.

Suppose I notice that AI usage declined this month and mention it to a coworker over lunch. I might say, “Did you notice AI usage was down?” and the conversation could go almost anywhere. Maybe we speculate about whether the tools were less useful for the current work, maybe my coworker shrugs, and maybe we move on to whatever is happening with the cafeteria.

Now imagine that person's manager asks exactly the same question during a one-on-one. The wording has not changed, but the surrounding reality has. The manager may genuinely be curious and may not have any target or expectation in mind, yet the employee still has to wonder whether the question carries more weight than it appears to.

Was I supposed to be using AI more? Is my team behind? Is this going to come up again? Should I have an explanation next time?

Nothing threatening has to be said for those questions to appear. There can be no quota, no mandate, no warning, and still the fact remains that a person with authority has chosen to ask about a particular number. That changes the experience of the conversation.

This is one of the stranger parts of becoming a manager. You can still feel like the same person you were before the promotion while other people begin hearing you differently. A casual suggestion can sound like a request, curiosity can sound like concern, and a question meant as exploration can arrive on the other side of the table as an indication that something needs to be fixed.

That does not mean managers should stop asking questions. If the answer to this problem is for managers to notice something potentially important and then remain silent for six months because they are afraid of sending the wrong signal, we have not improved much. Managers need to ask because part of their job is to understand what is happening.

But in a hierarchy, asking is not neutral. People pay attention not only to the question but to the fact that the question was asked.

At first, leadership may sincerely say, “We’re just tracking this,” and mean exactly that. There may be no performance consequence attached to the number at all. Then it appears in a monthly review, somebody asks why it changed, and the next month the same number appears again.

Perhaps one team is mentioned because its result looks especially good, while another team is asked for context because its result looks unusually low. Nobody has been punished or formally rewarded, but the people in the room are still learning something about what receives attention. Over time, those small moments begin to accumulate into an understanding of what the organization cares enough about to revisit.

This is one of the ways culture forms. It rarely changes because someone announces that a particular metric is now deeply important. More often, people notice what keeps returning: which questions get asked, which results get discussed, which successes get remembered, and which problems seem to follow a person from one meeting to the next.

A metric begins as information, but inside an organization it also becomes part of the conversation people are learning to interpret.

### Cultural Gravity

Organizations often measure things because the underlying things really do matter. Revenue matters because a company that continually spends more than it earns will eventually have a problem that no philosophical argument about metrics can solve. Quality matters because customers depend on products working, schedules matter because other people make plans around commitments, and productivity matters because organizations have finite time and resources.

So the problem is usually not that companies have chosen to care about entirely foolish things. The more subtle issue is that attention itself is limited. No manager can pay equal attention to everything happening on a team, and an executive responsible for thousands of people cannot possibly understand each decision or conversation in detail.

Metrics help decide where to look. A sudden rise in defects may suggest that something deserves attention, just as a drop in customer satisfaction or a project turning yellow may tell leadership that it is worth asking what changed. Without some mechanism for identifying patterns, every organizational problem would arrive as an anecdote and leadership would have very little way to tell the difference between a broad issue and whatever happened to be described most persuasively in the meeting.

That makes measurement valuable, but it also creates an asymmetry. Things that can be turned into numbers become easier to revisit, compare, and display, while equally real things that do not compress neatly can remain almost invisible unless somebody deliberately brings them back into view.

An experienced engineer might spend two hours helping a younger engineer understand a difficult part of the system. The immediate output of those two hours may be almost nothing. No feature ships, no bug closes, and whatever productivity measure the organization is watching may actually look slightly worse because the experienced engineer did less visible work.

Yet the younger engineer may leave the conversation much more capable than before. A future mistake may never happen, a design decision may improve six months later, or that relationship may eventually help the team solve a problem nobody knows about yet.

The difficulty is not that the dashboard is wrong. The dashboard simply has no obvious place to put that event.

Something similar happens with managers. A manager can spend an hour listening to an employee who is struggling, helping resolve a conflict, or giving someone enough support that they decide not to leave the team. The result may matter greatly to the health of the organization while producing almost no measurable artifact in the short term.

This is where a very natural process begins to shift. We start by measuring what we care about, but because the measured things are the ones that repeatedly appear in front of us, we can gradually find ourselves caring more intensely about what is easiest to measure.

Not because everything else becomes unimportant. It simply becomes less visible.

Imagine an hour-long leadership review. Revenue has a slide, schedule has a slide, quality has a slide, customer satisfaction has a slide, AI adoption has a slide, and staffing has a slide. Each one comes with trend lines, comparisons, targets, and enough visual structure to support several minutes of discussion.

Near the end, somebody says, “We also need to make sure we're encouraging thoughtful risk-taking.”

Everyone agrees, because of course they do. Then the conversation moves on, not because anyone secretly believes risk-taking is unimportant, but because the other subjects arrived with machinery around them. They could be reviewed, compared, and discussed with a level of apparent precision that risk-taking does not naturally provide.

I saw this tension in my own company through two values that made complete sense individually and became much more complicated when they had to coexist.

One was risk-taking. We wanted engineers to challenge assumptions, try unfamiliar approaches, experiment with new technologies, and avoid becoming so afraid of failure that the safest path became the only acceptable one. That matters in a technology company because innovation requires people to act before the outcome is guaranteed.

Another value was flawless execution. This also made sense. Customers generally prefer products that work, projects have commitments, bugs have consequences, and a company cannot sustain itself indefinitely by courageously exploring interesting possibilities without delivering anything.

We needed both values at the same time, but they entered the management system very differently.

Flawless execution produces evidence that travels well. Did we hit the date? How many defects escaped? Did we meet the commitment? Was the milestone green? Those questions can be answered in a way that fits comfortably into a quarterly review.

Risk-taking is more awkward. Suppose an engineer proposes an unconventional technical approach, the reasoning is sound, the team understands the uncertainty, and the potential payoff is significant enough that they decide the experiment is worth doing.

Then it fails.

The failed result does not necessarily mean the decision was poor. That is part of what makes risk-taking difficult to manage; a sensible risk can still produce a bad outcome. If failure automatically proves that the decision was wrong, then the organization is not really asking for risk-taking. It is asking for unconventional success.

But now consider what the failed experiment looks like from the measurable side of the system. Time was consumed, the schedule slipped twelve days, some work had to be discarded, and perhaps another team was delayed. The organization may also have learned something valuable, but that knowledge is much harder to place beside the schedule variance in a dashboard.

The schedule can say twelve days.

The learning requires a story.

This does not mean one is real and the other is not. It means one is easier to operationalize. To decide whether the failed experiment represented good judgment, someone has to know enough context to ask what the team believed at the time, whether the potential benefit justified the uncertainty, whether the risk was understood, and what was learned afterward.

Those are slower questions. They require conversation and sometimes disagreement, which gives the measurable side of the equation a practical advantage even when leadership genuinely values both.

That advantage can shape the organization over time. The things that are easy to count are also easy to target, compare, reward, and discuss repeatedly. The harder-to-measure values may remain sincerely held, but they depend more heavily on judgment to survive moments when the two come into conflict.

### The Values on the Wall

Companies like values because values are an attempt to say what kind of organization they want to become. Integrity, innovation, collaboration, courage, customer focus, and respect appear frequently because most people would prefer to work somewhere that actually possesses those qualities.

I do not assume companies are lying when they choose them. Most organizations contain plenty of people who sincerely want the culture described on the wall. The tension is that some values are much easier to translate into operating systems than others.

Courage, for example, does not aggregate especially well. You can imagine the absurdity of a quarterly slide announcing that courage has risen four percent, which is probably why most companies do not attempt it. Instead, they measure the things they can measure with more confidence and trust that the harder-to-quantify values will continue to influence judgment around those numbers.

Sometimes they do.

The interesting moments occur when the two begin pulling in different directions.

Suppose a team misses its schedule by twelve days because a thoughtful technical experiment failed and required part of the product to be redesigned. The explanation does not make the schedule variance disappear. The team really did miss the date, and pretending otherwise would make the whole idea of measurement rather pointless.

But the schedule variance also does not tell us whether the decision to take the risk was wise.

Both pieces of information belong in the same conversation. If we allow the metric to become the whole story, then context starts functioning like an appeal against a verdict the number has already issued. The team is no longer explaining what happened so everyone can understand the situation; it is defending itself against a representation that has acquired more authority than the underlying decision.

That is a very different relationship with data.

The same thing happens through organizational language. Large companies develop vocabularies that allow complicated realities to be discussed quickly: utilization, adoption, throughput, engagement, productivity, execution. These terms are useful because a large organization cannot retell the full story of the work every time somebody needs to make a decision.

But the abstraction can become so familiar that it starts sounding more concrete than the thing it describes.

“We need to increase adoption to improve utilization while maintaining execution against our productivity targets.”

That sentence can make perfect sense in a meeting.

Somewhere beneath it, actual people are doing actual things.

If AI adoption goes up, for example, the number still leaves us with the most interesting questions. Are engineers using AI because it is genuinely helping them solve problems, or because using AI has become the visible evidence that they are responding to leadership's priority? Are they learning faster, improving quality, or merely generating more activity around the tool?

Those questions require us to move back and forth between organizational language and ordinary experience. We need the abstraction because organizations are too large to operate without it, but we also need to remember how to translate the abstraction back into what people are actually doing.

Otherwise the representation begins replacing the thing it was meant to help us understand.

### What Happens Around Here?

Every organization has a second employee handbook that nobody has to print.

You learn it by staying long enough.

Over time, people figure out which deadlines are truly immovable and which ones become flexible when the right circumstances appear. They learn whether disagreeing with a manager is safe, which processes matter deeply and which survive mostly because nobody has gotten around to removing them, and what happens when someone makes a visible mistake.

They also learn what gets remembered.

A company may say that it values both schedule and quality, but employees eventually discover what happens when the two conflict. They may hear that collaboration matters and then notice whether helping a coworker carries any weight when individual performance is evaluated. They may hear that thoughtful risk-taking is encouraged and then watch what happens to the first team whose thoughtful risk fails.

Those moments are more instructive than the values statement because they reveal what the organization does when values become expensive.

Culture grows out of these accumulated experiences. It is not simply what leadership says, and it is not reducible to a policy document or a set of principles. People observe what actually happens and gradually build a practical model of the environment.

If a manager repeatedly says, “Bring me bad news early,” and then reacts badly whenever someone does, the team will eventually stop paying much attention to the original invitation. If leadership says that failure is part of innovation but every failed experiment produces a painful review, people will learn something very different about risk.

No one has to explain the contradiction.

Repeated experience is enough.

This is where the metric begins doing more than influencing a single decision. Suppose leadership starts tracking bug-closure time because it wants to understand how quickly problems are being resolved. From the leader's perspective, the metric may remain a useful signal among many.

The engineer experiences an additional layer. Difficult bugs tend to stay open longer, so choosing one creates a greater chance of producing a bad-looking number. If those numbers repeatedly attract uncomfortable questions, employees begin to understand that easy, fast closure is safer.

At first, that may be conscious. An engineer thinks about which bug to take and notices how each choice is likely to look.

Later, it becomes part of the environment.

People stop calculating the incentive every time because they already understand how things work around here. A pattern that began as a measurement has become part of the culture's practical definition of good performance.

That is why asking what behavior a metric encourages is useful but incomplete. We also have to think about what people are learning from the repeated experience of living under it.

Are they learning that an unusual number invites curiosity, or that it demands a defense? Are they learning that context matters even when the dashboard looks bad, or only when the context supports the dashboard? Are they learning that difficult work is valued despite the fact that difficult work may produce less attractive numbers?

Over time, these become assumptions rather than explicit beliefs. Nobody needs to announce that fast bug closure matters more than difficult problem solving if the structure of the work has been teaching that lesson for years.

### Yesterday Is Still in the Room

This also helps explain why organizations can change a metric much faster than they can change the culture built around it.

Suppose a company spends three years emphasizing schedule predictability. Teams that hit their dates receive praise, missed commitments trigger difficult conversations, managers learn to protect their plans, and employees become increasingly cautious about work whose outcome is hard to predict.

Eventually leadership notices that people have become too conservative. The organization needs more experimentation, more innovation, and more willingness to try ideas that may not work.

So the message changes.

At an all-hands meeting, the CEO says that thoughtful failure is acceptable and that teams should take more risks.

The statement may be completely sincere.

Yet the behavior does not change very quickly, because the people listening have three years of evidence about what happened when schedules slipped. A new message has to compete with a much larger body of lived experience.

That is not necessarily cynicism. It may simply be learning.

If leadership wants people to believe that thoughtful failure is genuinely safe, eventually someone will have to take a sensible risk, fail, and discover that the organization still treats the decision as sensible. Then another person will need to see the same thing happen.

The same is true of bad news. If leaders want employees to surface problems early, someone has to do it and discover that early honesty is actually rewarded rather than punished. If managers want people to use judgment instead of blindly optimizing the metric, employees need to see a situation where someone made the number look worse for a good reason and the organization recognized the difference.

Those events matter because values become credible when following them has a cost.

It is easy to support collaboration when collaboration does not interfere with anyone's individual output. It is easy to value quality when quality and schedule point in the same direction, and easy to celebrate risk-taking when the risk succeeds.

The harder case is the one that tells people what the organization really means.

What happens when collaboration slows down someone's visible work? What happens when quality requires delaying a release? What happens when a thoughtful risk fails and turns the project red?

Those moments become part of the organization's memory.

This is why I think of a measurement system not only as an information system but as part of a larger teaching system. The things we measure attract attention, the things we revisit become expectations, and the way we respond when numbers move in the wrong direction tells people how much room actually exists for context and judgment.

None of this means organizations should become timid about measurement. Managers still need visibility, targets still have value, and numbers remain one of the best defenses we have against comfortable stories that are not true.

It simply means that measurement has consequences beyond the information it produces. People notice which numbers matter, what happens when they miss them, and whether the values the organization talks about survive when those values come into conflict with the dashboard.

The metric itself may eventually disappear. Priorities change, leaders change, and dashboards are rebuilt all the time.

What people learned from living under it can remain much longer.

That is why cultural change so often feels slower than management expects. The new message may be perfectly clear, but the old experience is still part of the room.
