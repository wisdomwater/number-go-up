# 5 — I Became My Own Manager

The workday ends, but the measurements do not. I close the laptop, stand up from the desk, and glance at my watch to see how many steps I have taken. Later I might log dinner, check my investment accounts, notice how much time I spent on my phone, and think about whether I exercised enough that day. Tomorrow morning there will be another number waiting for me on the bathroom scale.

Somewhere along the way, the dashboard followed me home.

None of this feels particularly strange because measurement has become part of ordinary life. Watches count our steps, phones report our screen time, apps track calories, scales estimate body composition, and financial sites can tell us our net worth to the dollar. We can measure sleep, exercise, reading, meditation, spending, productivity, and an impressive collection of bodily functions that previous generations managed to experience without receiving a weekly trend report.

Much of this information is genuinely useful. I have voluntarily collected quite a bit of it myself, and I have no intention of spending the rest of this book pretending otherwise. If I want to become healthier, knowing what I eat and how much I move can expose the difference between what I imagine I am doing and what I actually do. If I want financial security, knowing where my money goes is considerably more useful than hoping things work out.

The method should be familiar by now. Decide what matters, find a way to observe it, compare what is happening with what you intended, and adjust. It is the same basic approach that helped us become competent at work, and there is no obvious reason it should stop being useful when we leave the office.

There is one important difference, though. At work, the person examining the dashboard and the person represented by the dashboard are usually different people. At home, I occupy both positions.

I set the goals, choose the measures, review the results, determine whether performance is acceptable, and decide what corrective action should be taken.

I became my own manager.

### The Dashboard Comes Home

The transition happened gradually enough that I never really noticed it. I wanted to lose weight, so I bought a scale. I wanted to understand what I was eating, so I logged food. I wanted to move more, so I wore a device that counted my steps. Each decision made sense by itself, and together they created a surprisingly comprehensive performance-management system for Michael.

Money followed a similar path. For much of adult life, financial progress is naturally expressed through numbers because money is already numerical. We track income, spending, mortgage balances, investment returns, savings rates, and eventually the amount we believe we will need for retirement. A financial dashboard can tell us in seconds what once would have required gathering statements from several institutions and spending an evening with a calculator.

The technology is remarkable when you think about it. My watch knows that I went for a walk, my phone has opinions about how long I slept, my food app knows what I ate, and my brokerage account can tell me whether the market has been kind to me today. Each system provides a small window into some aspect of my life. Put enough windows together and it begins to feel as though I ought to be able to see the whole thing.

There are good reasons for wanting that view. Bodies are complicated, money accumulates slowly, and habits are notoriously difficult to perceive from inside the habit itself. I can sincerely believe I exercise regularly and then discover that “regularly” means twice last month. I can believe restaurant meals are occasional until the credit-card statement suggests that *occasional* has developed a rather generous definition.

Measurement can be especially useful when memory and intention have quietly formed an alliance. I may genuinely intend to eat differently, exercise more, spend less, or put my phone down earlier, and after a few weeks my recollection of what I actually did begins to resemble what I meant to do. A record interrupts that process. It doesn't have to shame me; it can simply remind me what happened.

That distinction matters. If the data says most of my eating is fine until eight o'clock at night, that is useful information. I don't need to conclude that I lack discipline, redesign my entire diet, or begin Monday with a twelve-point personal transformation initiative. Perhaps I need to understand what happens at eight o'clock.

At its best, self-measurement does exactly this. It makes something visible enough that I can become curious about it.

### When the Walk Becomes Steps

Suppose I go for a walk on a pleasant morning. The air is cool, the neighborhood is quiet, and I notice someone working in a garden as I pass. A dog investigates me from behind a fence, sunlight moves through the trees, and for half an hour I am simply outside moving through the world.

Then I arrive home and look at my watch.

3,842 steps.

The number is true. Those steps contribute to my physical activity, and if I am trying to become healthier, knowing how much I move can be useful. The watch has not ruined the walk by counting it, and nothing about the trees becomes less beautiful because an accelerometer was operating on my wrist.

But once steps matter, I begin noticing the walk differently. I may choose a longer route because I am behind for the day, circle the block once more because I am close to a goal, or pace around the kitchen in the evening because the little ring on my watch remains stubbornly incomplete. The measurement that originally described my movement has begun influencing it.

Again, that may be exactly what I wanted. If my goal was to become less sedentary, walking around the kitchen instead of sitting on the couch is probably a successful intervention. The strange part begins when an unrecorded walk feels somehow less valuable, or when I return from a beautiful afternoon outside and experience the primary accomplishment as closing a ring.

Nothing dramatic has happened. I haven't forgotten that walking can be enjoyable or decided that trees exist primarily to provide scenery during cardiovascular exercise. My attention has simply acquired another object. There is the walk itself, and there is the representation of the walk waiting on my wrist.

The distinction becomes easier to see when the technology fails. Anyone who has exercised while forgetting their watch knows the peculiar disappointment of completing something that apparently will not be entered into the permanent record. Rationally, I know my body received exactly the same benefit. Emotionally, there is still a faint sense that the universe has failed to give me proper credit.

That feeling is worth noticing because it reveals how quickly measurement can change the experience it was meant to support. I started counting steps because I wanted walking to become part of my life. Somewhere along the way, it became possible for walking to feel like a way of producing steps.

We do this in other places too. A book can become one of the books we intend to finish this year, sleep can become a score we check before deciding how rested we feel, and a meal can become calories and macronutrients before it becomes dinner. None of those descriptions is false, and sometimes they are exactly the information we need. The question is whether the measurement remains in service to the experience or begins competing with it for our attention.

### My Morning Performance Review

Few personal measurements make this relationship clearer for me than the bathroom scale. I am trying to lose a substantial amount of weight, so body weight is relevant information. If the long-term trend never moves downward, something about what I am doing probably needs to change. Pretending the number doesn't matter would not make me healthier.

The problem is that the scale is extremely willing to give me feedback every morning, while the change I care about unfolds over a much longer period. I can eat the way I intended, exercise, and make choices I feel good about, then step on the scale the next morning and discover that I weigh more. On another day I can eat poorly, barely move, and be rewarded with a lower number.

There are perfectly ordinary biological reasons for this, and intellectually I know them. My body contains water, food, glycogen, inflammation, and enough other variables to make short-term moral accounting a particularly bad use of a bathroom appliance. Unfortunately, my emotional response does not always wait for the statistical briefing.

The number goes down and something in me relaxes. The number goes up and I can feel the temptation to review the previous day as though I am investigating a performance failure. What did I eat? Did I move enough? Was dinner too large? Somewhere between stepping on the scale and brushing my teeth, a measurement can begin to feel like a meeting.

If I managed another person this way, I hope someone would eventually intervene. Imagine giving an employee clear expectations, watching them meet those expectations, and then telling them the next morning that they had failed because an outcome influenced by dozens of variables moved in the wrong direction overnight. The following day they ignore the expectations, the outcome happens to improve, and I congratulate them on excellent execution. This would not be considered a particularly sophisticated management philosophy.

Still, I can do something remarkably similar to myself before breakfast. The absurdity is harder to see because I am simultaneously the manager who selected the metric and the employee standing there waiting to learn how we did. There is no unreasonable boss to complain about. I built the system.

In fact, I purchased it.

I brought it home.

I put it on the bathroom floor.

The scale isn't doing anything wrong. It is reporting the information I asked it to report with admirable indifference, much like the compiler and stopwatch from earlier chapters. The interesting thing is what happens on my side of the display.

For now, I only want to notice that transition. We will have more to say later about why a number can begin to feel like a verdict and what it can reasonably tell me about myself. Here, the simpler observation is enough: a tool I purchased to help manage one aspect of my life can change the way I experience that aspect of my life.

I am no longer merely collecting information about myself.

I am managing myself with it.

### The Self as a Project

Once you become comfortable managing one part of yourself, there is no obvious reason to stop. Health can be improved, finances can be organized, productivity can be increased, sleep can be optimized, and time can certainly be used more effectively. We can establish reading goals, morning routines, meditation streaks, screen-time limits, relationship habits, and systems for making sure we remember to maintain the systems.

There is always another part of life available for improvement.

I understand the appeal because I feel it. There is a future version of me who weighs less, exercises consistently, reads more, wastes less time, has his finances neatly organized, maintains a reasonable sleep schedule, and handles difficult emotions with a degree of serenity that current management considers aspirational. He is an impressive fellow. I have been trying to hire him for years.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to become healthier or wiser. Some things in my life genuinely need to change, and accepting myself does not require pretending every habit is equally healthy or every pattern equally worth preserving. The ability to examine our lives and deliberately alter them is part of the mastery we've spent the first part of this book appreciating. I am grateful that I am not limited to whatever version of myself happened to emerge by accident.

The strange part is how easily improvement becomes a posture toward nearly everything. I can meditate so I will become calmer, schedule unstructured time to increase creativity, take a vacation because rest will make me more productive, and practice gratitude because I have read that grateful people experience better well-being. None of those motives invalidates the activity. But the managerial question keeps appearing: *What will this produce?*

Even rest can acquire deliverables.

After enough years thinking this way, the present begins to look suspiciously like raw material. Today's walk contributes to future health, today's savings contribute to retirement, today's reading contributes to knowledge, and today's work contributes to whatever comes next in a career. The future matters, and much of adulthood consists of caring for a person we haven't become yet. Still, there is something peculiar about spending so much of the life I actually have preparing a better version of it.

Middle age makes this especially visible. By then, many of us have spent decades learning how to identify gaps between current state and desired state. We know how to make plans, break goals into steps, monitor progress, diagnose problems, and adjust when the trend line refuses to cooperate. Then we turn all that accumulated competence toward the largest continuous-improvement project we have ever encountered.

Ourselves.

The project has an unusual property: it never ships.

There will always be something to improve because the target changes while we approach it. Bodies age, careers evolve, relationships enter new seasons, finances change, and habits we thought we had mastered have an irritating ability to return. Even success creates new possibilities for improvement. The better-managed life does not eventually reach a state where management is no longer required.

This doesn't mean we should stop planning or growing. It means there may be a difference between **managing a life well and experiencing a life fully**. I can be responsible with my health, money, work, and time while still asking whether every part of myself needs to stand beneath the fluorescent lighting of a quarterly review.

That question would have seemed strange to me earlier in life. Mastery taught me to respond to the gap between reality and desire by asking, *What can I change?* It is an excellent question, one that helped me build a career, prepare for the future, solve problems, and take responsibility for myself.

I still believe in it.

But eventually we encounter situations where we can do the right things and the desired result still refuses to arrive on schedule.

When that happens, another question becomes necessary.

**What is actually mine to control?**
