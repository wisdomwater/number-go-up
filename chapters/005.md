# Chapter 5 — Every Metric Is a Message

## We’re Just Tracking It

Most metrics start innocently enough.

Someone wants to know what's happening.

A manager wants to understand whether a new process is working. Leadership wants visibility into a part of the organization that's difficult to see. A team has invested in a new tool and wants to know whether anyone is actually using it.

So they measure something.

That's reasonable.

In fact, it's exactly what we've spent much of this book defending. Measurement gives us information. It allows reality to push back against intuition. Instead of sitting in a meeting debating whether people seem to be using the new tool more often, we can look.

**Usage increased 23 percent.**

Great. Now we know something.

Maybe that's all the manager wants to know.

I think it's important to give managers the benefit of the doubt here, because it's easy to tell this story with a villain.

Management creates dashboard. Management watches dashboard. Management demands number go up. Employees suffer.

Sometimes that happens.

But most managers I've known are trying to do something considerably more difficult and considerably less sinister. They're trying to understand a complicated human system with limited time and incomplete information.

They have teams to coordinate, commitments to manage, problems to solve, people to develop, information to communicate upward, information to communicate downward, and decisions to make with consequences they cannot fully predict.

Of course they want information.

The problem is that managers don't experience information in quite the same way employees do.

Imagine that I notice a number on a dashboard.

AI usage declined this month.

Interesting.

I mention it to a coworker over lunch.

**“Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?”**

Maybe we talk about it. Maybe we speculate about why. Maybe my coworker shrugs and takes another bite of sandwich.

Now imagine that person's manager asks exactly the same question in a one-on-one.

**“Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?”**

Same words.

Different sentence.

The manager may genuinely be curious.

The employee hears the question through the context of authority.

Does my manager think this is a problem?

Was I supposed to be using AI more?

Is our team behind?

Is this going to come up again?

Should I have an explanation?

Do I need to do something differently?

Nothing threatening had to be said.

The manager didn't need to pound the table.

There was no mandate.

There wasn't even a target.

There was simply a person with authority asking about a number.

Authority gives words weight.

This is one of the strange things about becoming a manager. You can continue feeling like the same person you were before the promotion, while everyone else starts hearing you differently.

A suggestion can sound like a request.

A request can sound like an expectation.

Curiosity can sound like concern.

And concern can sound remarkably similar to **you should probably fix this**.

The manager may not intend any of that.

But intention isn't the only thing communicating.

Position is communicating too.

That's why a metric that begins as purely informational doesn't necessarily stay that way.

At first, leadership may genuinely say:

**We're just tracking this.**

And they may mean it.

There is no target. No quota. No performance consequence. Nobody is being evaluated on it.

Then the number appears in a meeting.

Someone asks about it.

The next month, it appears again.

A team with a high number gets mentioned.

A team with a low number gets asked what's happening.

Nobody is punished.

Nobody is rewarded.

But people are learning.

They are learning that this number receives attention.

And attention from people with authority is rarely experienced as neutral.

This doesn't mean managers should stop asking questions.

That would create its own absurdity.

**“I noticed something potentially important on the dashboard, but I didn't want anyone to infer that I cared, so I stared silently at it for six months.”**

Probably not the management breakthrough we're looking for.

Managers need to ask questions.

They need to notice patterns.

They need to challenge teams.

They need to investigate problems.

They need information precisely because they carry responsibility for things larger than their own individual work.

The point is simply to recognize that measurement inside a hierarchy has more than one effect.

The manager sees information.

The employee also sees information.

But the employee sees something else:

**My manager is paying attention to this.**

That is already a message.

It may be a very quiet message.

It may not mean **make this number go up**.

It may not mean **your performance will be judged by this**.

It may mean nothing more than **this is worth understanding**.

But even that communicates importance.

And repetition turns up the volume.

Ask about something once and people know you're curious.

Ask about it every week and people know you care.

Put it on the dashboard and people know the organization cares.

Review it with senior leadership and people know senior leadership cares.

Soon nobody remembers exactly when the metric crossed the line from something we were observing to something that mattered.

There may not have been a line.

That's the important part.

Culture rarely changes because someone sends an email announcing:

**Effective immediately, we will begin caring about this number.**

It changes through accumulated signals.

Questions.

Meetings.

Dashboards.

Follow-ups.

Praise.

Concern.

The things leaders repeatedly turn their attention toward.

Each signal may be perfectly reasonable on its own.

Together, they tell a story.

This is why every metric is also a message.

Not because measurement is manipulative.

Not because managers are secretly trying to control people.

Not because employees are paranoid.

Because human beings pay attention to what other human beings pay attention to, especially when those other human beings have authority.

The metric begins as information.

Then someone asks about it.

And everyone else begins learning what the asking means.

## What We Ask About Matters

We measure what we care about.

That's usually how this starts.

A company cares about revenue, so it measures revenue. It cares about quality, so it measures defects. It cares about customers, so it measures satisfaction. It cares about delivery, so it measures schedules and commitments.

There is something real behind all of those numbers.

That's worth emphasizing because it would be easy to dismiss corporate metrics as arbitrary inventions created by people who enjoy spreadsheets a little too much.

Usually they aren't.

Revenue matters. If a company consistently spends more money than it brings in, eventually the philosophical discussion about metrics will be interrupted by bankruptcy.

Quality matters.

Schedules matter.

Customers matter.

Costs matter.

Productivity matters.

The things organizations measure are often things they genuinely should care about.

And because they care about them, they pay attention to them.

The problem is that attention is limited.

A manager cannot pay equal attention to everything happening across a team. An executive responsible for thousands of employees certainly cannot. There are too many projects, customers, decisions, risks, technologies, relationships, and problems competing for awareness.

Some things have to receive more attention than others.

That's what priority means.

If everything is a priority, we have simply discovered a more exhausting synonym for **nothing is a priority**.

Metrics help establish those priorities.

A dashboard says:

**Look here.**

This number is worth watching.

This trend deserves attention.

This change might require a decision.

That's incredibly useful.

Imagine running a large organization without any mechanism for deciding where to look. Every problem would arrive as an anecdote. Every success would be a story. Leadership would have no reliable way to distinguish a systemic issue from the unusually persuasive person who happened to get invited to the meeting.

Measurement helps direct scarce attention toward places where it may be needed.

Defects suddenly rise.

Look here.

Customer satisfaction falls.

Look here.

Costs jump.

Look here.

A project starts slipping.

Look here.

The metric acts almost like a flashlight. It illuminates a small part of a much larger landscape and says:

**Something is happening over here.**

That's good.

But a flashlight has another property.

Whatever it isn't pointed at remains darker.

That doesn't mean nothing is there.

It means we're not looking at it.

This creates an unavoidable tension inside organizations. There is always more reality than there is attention available to understand it.

Some of that reality gets represented in metrics.

Some doesn't.

The things that get represented have an advantage.

They can appear on dashboards.

They can be reviewed.

They can be compared.

They can trend upward or downward.

They can turn red.

They can trigger an alert.

They can show up in a presentation to senior leadership.

Other things may be just as real and just as important, but they don't arrive with a number attached.

An experienced engineer spends two hours helping a younger engineer understand a difficult part of the system.

Something happened.

Knowledge moved.

A relationship strengthened.

Future mistakes may have been prevented.

The younger engineer may become more confident and independent.

The team may be better because of those two hours.

Where does that appear on the dashboard?

Probably nowhere.

Meanwhile, the experienced engineer shipped two fewer units of whatever the organization happens to count.

Both realities exist.

One is legible to the measurement system.

The other may not be.

Or consider a manager who notices that someone on the team is struggling and spends time listening.

No project milestone moves.

No defect gets closed.

No revenue appears.

There may be no measurable output at all.

But perhaps that conversation keeps a good employee from leaving.

Perhaps it helps someone recover from a bad month.

Perhaps it resolves a conflict before it damages the team.

Something real happened.

The absence of a metric does not make it less real.

This is where organizational attention becomes culturally important.

We tend to begin with:

**We measure what we care about.**

That's reasonable.

But over time, another process can begin:

**We care about what we measure.**

Not exclusively.

Not intentionally.

But practically.

The things with numbers keep returning.

They appear in weekly meetings.

They appear in monthly reports.

They appear in quarterly reviews.

They demand explanations.

They invite comparisons.

They create questions.

The unmeasured things depend on someone remembering to bring them up.

That creates an asymmetry.

Imagine a leadership review with an hour available.

There are twelve slides.

Revenue gets a slide.

Schedule gets a slide.

Quality gets a slide.

Customer satisfaction gets a slide.

AI adoption gets a slide.

Staffing gets a slide.

Each one has numbers, trends, colors, and comparisons.

Then somewhere near the end someone says:

**We also need to make sure we're encouraging thoughtful risk-taking.**

Everyone agrees.

Of course we do.

Risk-taking matters.

Then the meeting moves on.

Nothing dishonest happened.

Nobody secretly decided risk-taking was unimportant.

The measurable things simply arrived at the meeting better prepared.

They had charts.

This is one of the quiet ways priorities form.

Not necessarily through declarations.

Through attention.

What gets discussed?

What gets reviewed?

What gets followed up on?

What gets an explanation when it changes?

What gets remembered because the dashboard remembers it for us?

People notice these patterns.

If leadership says something matters but rarely asks about it, that communicates something.

If leadership never mentions something in a values statement but asks about it every Thursday, that communicates something too.

Again, this doesn't mean leaders should avoid asking about metrics for fear of sending the wrong message.

Organizations need priorities.

Scarce attention has to go somewhere.

But we should recognize that directing attention is itself an act of prioritization.

The dashboard isn't merely describing the organization.

It is helping decide where the organization looks.

And where an organization looks, people tend to look too.

This is why the distinction between measured reality and unmeasured reality matters so much.

The measured thing is not necessarily more real.

It is not necessarily more important.

It is simply more visible.

And visibility has power.

Once something becomes visible enough, regular enough, and prominent enough, it can begin acquiring importance beyond the reason we originally measured it.

We measured it because we cared about it.

Then we kept looking at it because we measured it.

And eventually it becomes difficult to tell where one ended and the other began.

This is how a reasonable decision about what information to collect can gradually become a statement about priority.

We only have so much attention.

Metrics help us decide where to spend it.

Which means every time we decide what deserves a number, we should also remember to ask:

**What important part of reality might not have one?**
