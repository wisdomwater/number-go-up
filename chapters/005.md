# Chapter 5 — Every Metric Is a Message

## We’re Just Tracking It

Most metrics start innocently enough.

Someone wants to know what's happening.

A manager wants to understand whether a new process is working. Leadership wants visibility into a part of the organization that's difficult to see. A team has invested in a new tool and wants to know whether anyone is actually using it.

So they measure something.

That's reasonable.

In fact, it's exactly what we've spent much of this book defending. Measurement gives us information. It allows reality to push back against intuition. Instead of sitting in a meeting debating whether people seem to be using the new tool more often, we can look.

**Usage increased 23 percent.**

Great. Now we know something.

Maybe that's all the manager wants to know.

I think it's important to give managers the benefit of the doubt here, because it's easy to tell this story with a villain.

Management creates dashboard. Management watches dashboard. Management demands number go up. Employees suffer.

Sometimes that happens.

But most managers I've known are trying to do something considerably more difficult and considerably less sinister. They're trying to understand a complicated human system with limited time and incomplete information.

They have teams to coordinate, commitments to manage, problems to solve, people to develop, information to communicate upward, information to communicate downward, and decisions to make with consequences they cannot fully predict.

Of course they want information.

The problem is that managers don't experience information in quite the same way employees do.

Imagine that I notice a number on a dashboard.

AI usage declined this month.

Interesting.

I mention it to a coworker over lunch.

**“Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?”**

Maybe we talk about it. Maybe we speculate about why. Maybe my coworker shrugs and takes another bite of sandwich.

Now imagine that person's manager asks exactly the same question in a one-on-one.

**“Hey, did you notice AI usage was down this month?”**

Same words.

Different sentence.

The manager may genuinely be curious.

The employee hears the question through the context of authority.

Does my manager think this is a problem?

Was I supposed to be using AI more?

Is our team behind?

Is this going to come up again?

Should I have an explanation?

Do I need to do something differently?

Nothing threatening had to be said.

The manager didn't need to pound the table.

There was no mandate.

There wasn't even a target.

There was simply a person with authority asking about a number.

Authority gives words weight.

This is one of the strange things about becoming a manager. You can continue feeling like the same person you were before the promotion, while everyone else starts hearing you differently.

A suggestion can sound like a request.

A request can sound like an expectation.

Curiosity can sound like concern.

And concern can sound remarkably similar to **you should probably fix this**.

The manager may not intend any of that.

But intention isn't the only thing communicating.

Position is communicating too.

That's why a metric that begins as purely informational doesn't necessarily stay that way.

At first, leadership may genuinely say:

**We're just tracking this.**

And they may mean it.

There is no target. No quota. No performance consequence. Nobody is being evaluated on it.

Then the number appears in a meeting.

Someone asks about it.

The next month, it appears again.

A team with a high number gets mentioned.

A team with a low number gets asked what's happening.

Nobody is punished.

Nobody is rewarded.

But people are learning.

They are learning that this number receives attention.

And attention from people with authority is rarely experienced as neutral.

This doesn't mean managers should stop asking questions.

That would create its own absurdity.

**“I noticed something potentially important on the dashboard, but I didn't want anyone to infer that I cared, so I stared silently at it for six months.”**

Probably not the management breakthrough we're looking for.

Managers need to ask questions.

They need to notice patterns.

They need to challenge teams.

They need to investigate problems.

They need information precisely because they carry responsibility for things larger than their own individual work.

The point is simply to recognize that measurement inside a hierarchy has more than one effect.

The manager sees information.

The employee also sees information.

But the employee sees something else:

**My manager is paying attention to this.**

That is already a message.

It may be a very quiet message.

It may not mean **make this number go up**.

It may not mean **your performance will be judged by this**.

It may mean nothing more than **this is worth understanding**.

But even that communicates importance.

And repetition turns up the volume.

Ask about something once and people know you're curious.

Ask about it every week and people know you care.

Put it on the dashboard and people know the organization cares.

Review it with senior leadership and people know senior leadership cares.

Soon nobody remembers exactly when the metric crossed the line from something we were observing to something that mattered.

There may not have been a line.

That's the important part.

Culture rarely changes because someone sends an email announcing:

**Effective immediately, we will begin caring about this number.**

It changes through accumulated signals.

Questions.

Meetings.

Dashboards.

Follow-ups.

Praise.

Concern.

The things leaders repeatedly turn their attention toward.

Each signal may be perfectly reasonable on its own.

Together, they tell a story.

This is why every metric is also a message.

Not because measurement is manipulative.

Not because managers are secretly trying to control people.

Not because employees are paranoid.

Because human beings pay attention to what other human beings pay attention to, especially when those other human beings have authority.

The metric begins as information.

Then someone asks about it.

And everyone else begins learning what the asking means.

## What We Ask About Matters

We measure what we care about.

That's usually how this starts.

A company cares about revenue, so it measures revenue. It cares about quality, so it measures defects. It cares about customers, so it measures satisfaction. It cares about delivery, so it measures schedules and commitments.

There is something real behind all of those numbers.

That's worth emphasizing because it would be easy to dismiss corporate metrics as arbitrary inventions created by people who enjoy spreadsheets a little too much.

Usually they aren't.

Revenue matters. If a company consistently spends more money than it brings in, eventually the philosophical discussion about metrics will be interrupted by bankruptcy.

Quality matters.

Schedules matter.

Customers matter.

Costs matter.

Productivity matters.

The things organizations measure are often things they genuinely should care about.

And because they care about them, they pay attention to them.

The problem is that attention is limited.

A manager cannot pay equal attention to everything happening across a team. An executive responsible for thousands of employees certainly cannot. There are too many projects, customers, decisions, risks, technologies, relationships, and problems competing for awareness.

Some things have to receive more attention than others.

That's what priority means.

If everything is a priority, we have simply discovered a more exhausting synonym for **nothing is a priority**.

Metrics help establish those priorities.

A dashboard says:

**Look here.**

This number is worth watching.

This trend deserves attention.

This change might require a decision.

That's incredibly useful.

Imagine running a large organization without any mechanism for deciding where to look. Every problem would arrive as an anecdote. Every success would be a story. Leadership would have no reliable way to distinguish a systemic issue from the unusually persuasive person who happened to get invited to the meeting.

Measurement helps direct scarce attention toward places where it may be needed.

Defects suddenly rise.

Look here.

Customer satisfaction falls.

Look here.

Costs jump.

Look here.

A project starts slipping.

Look here.

The metric acts almost like a flashlight. It illuminates a small part of a much larger landscape and says:

**Something is happening over here.**

That's good.

But a flashlight has another property.

Whatever it isn't pointed at remains darker.

That doesn't mean nothing is there.

It means we're not looking at it.

This creates an unavoidable tension inside organizations. There is always more reality than there is attention available to understand it.

Some of that reality gets represented in metrics.

Some doesn't.

The things that get represented have an advantage.

They can appear on dashboards.

They can be reviewed.

They can be compared.

They can trend upward or downward.

They can turn red.

They can trigger an alert.

They can show up in a presentation to senior leadership.

Other things may be just as real and just as important, but they don't arrive with a number attached.

An experienced engineer spends two hours helping a younger engineer understand a difficult part of the system.

Something happened.

Knowledge moved.

A relationship strengthened.

Future mistakes may have been prevented.

The younger engineer may become more confident and independent.

The team may be better because of those two hours.

Where does that appear on the dashboard?

Probably nowhere.

Meanwhile, the experienced engineer shipped two fewer units of whatever the organization happens to count.

Both realities exist.

One is legible to the measurement system.

The other may not be.

Or consider a manager who notices that someone on the team is struggling and spends time listening.

No project milestone moves.

No defect gets closed.

No revenue appears.

There may be no measurable output at all.

But perhaps that conversation keeps a good employee from leaving.

Perhaps it helps someone recover from a bad month.

Perhaps it resolves a conflict before it damages the team.

Something real happened.

The absence of a metric does not make it less real.

This is where organizational attention becomes culturally important.

We tend to begin with:

**We measure what we care about.**

That's reasonable.

But over time, another process can begin:

**We care about what we measure.**

Not exclusively.

Not intentionally.

But practically.

The things with numbers keep returning.

They appear in weekly meetings.

They appear in monthly reports.

They appear in quarterly reviews.

They demand explanations.

They invite comparisons.

They create questions.

The unmeasured things depend on someone remembering to bring them up.

That creates an asymmetry.

Imagine a leadership review with an hour available.

There are twelve slides.

Revenue gets a slide.

Schedule gets a slide.

Quality gets a slide.

Customer satisfaction gets a slide.

AI adoption gets a slide.

Staffing gets a slide.

Each one has numbers, trends, colors, and comparisons.

Then somewhere near the end someone says:

**We also need to make sure we're encouraging thoughtful risk-taking.**

Everyone agrees.

Of course we do.

Risk-taking matters.

Then the meeting moves on.

Nothing dishonest happened.

Nobody secretly decided risk-taking was unimportant.

The measurable things simply arrived at the meeting better prepared.

They had charts.

This is one of the quiet ways priorities form.

Not necessarily through declarations.

Through attention.

What gets discussed?

What gets reviewed?

What gets followed up on?

What gets an explanation when it changes?

What gets remembered because the dashboard remembers it for us?

People notice these patterns.

If leadership says something matters but rarely asks about it, that communicates something.

If leadership never mentions something in a values statement but asks about it every Thursday, that communicates something too.

Again, this doesn't mean leaders should avoid asking about metrics for fear of sending the wrong message.

Organizations need priorities.

Scarce attention has to go somewhere.

But we should recognize that directing attention is itself an act of prioritization.

The dashboard isn't merely describing the organization.

It is helping decide where the organization looks.

And where an organization looks, people tend to look too.

This is why the distinction between measured reality and unmeasured reality matters so much.

The measured thing is not necessarily more real.

It is not necessarily more important.

It is simply more visible.

And visibility has power.

Once something becomes visible enough, regular enough, and prominent enough, it can begin acquiring importance beyond the reason we originally measured it.

We measured it because we cared about it.

Then we kept looking at it because we measured it.

And eventually it becomes difficult to tell where one ended and the other began.

This is how a reasonable decision about what information to collect can gradually become a statement about priority.

We only have so much attention.

Metrics help us decide where to spend it.

Which means every time we decide what deserves a number, we should also remember to ask:

**What important part of reality might not have one?**

## Measurability Creates Cultural Gravity

Focused attention is powerful.

It's one of the reasons metrics work.

If quality is slipping and we begin measuring defects, people pay more attention to defects. If customers are waiting too long and we begin measuring response times, people pay more attention to delays. If costs are rising and we start reviewing expenses more carefully, people start noticing where money is going.

Good.

Sometimes an organization needs to turn its head.

A metric can do that.

It takes something that was previously vague and makes it visible enough that thousands of people can focus on it together.

That's an extraordinary organizational tool.

The problem begins when the things that can focus our attention most easily start competing with things that require judgment.

At one point, my company had two values that I found particularly interesting.

One was risk-taking.

We wanted people to try things. Challenge assumptions. Explore new technologies. Pursue ideas that might not work. Don't become so afraid of failure that we only do things whose outcomes we already know.

This makes sense, particularly in a technology company.

Innovation requires uncertainty.

If you already know exactly how something is going to turn out, there's a decent chance you aren't taking much of a risk.

Another value was flawless execution.

That also makes sense.

Customers generally prefer products that work.

Projects have commitments. Products have schedules. Bugs have consequences. A company cannot spend all of its time courageously exploring fascinating possibilities while forgetting to actually ship anything.

We needed both.

**Take risks.**

**Execute flawlessly.**

Now guess which one was easier to measure.

Flawless execution practically walks into the quarterly review carrying its own PowerPoint.

Did we hit the date?

Did we meet the commitment?

How many defects escaped?

Did the milestone turn green?

Was the project delivered according to plan?

How accurate was the forecast?

Numbers everywhere.

Risk-taking is harder.

Did we take enough intelligent risks this quarter?

Seven?

Was six cowardice?

Should we target eight next quarter?

And what exactly counts as a good risk?

Suppose an engineer pursues an unconventional technical approach. The reasoning is sound. The potential payoff is significant. The team understands the uncertainty and decides the experiment is worth attempting.

Then it fails.

Was that good risk-taking?

Possibly.

In fact, it may have been excellent risk-taking.

The decision can be good even when the outcome is bad.

That's what makes risk a risk.

But now look at what happened to the measurable side of the equation.

The experiment consumed time.

The schedule slipped.

A commitment was missed.

Perhaps some work had to be discarded.

Maybe another team was delayed.

The organization may have gained valuable knowledge.

Unfortunately, **valuable knowledge gained from a thoughtful failed experiment** is a difficult field to add to a dashboard.

**Schedule variance** is much easier.

This creates what I think of as **cultural gravity**.

The things that are easiest to measure naturally pull organizational attention toward themselves.

Not because they are necessarily more important.

Because they are easier to see.

They have numbers.

Numbers can be reviewed.

Numbers can be compared.

Numbers can have targets.

Numbers can turn red.

And once again, red has a remarkable ability to focus a meeting.

The harder-to-measure value doesn't disappear.

Leadership may sincerely continue saying:

**We want people to take risks.**

But employees experience both messages.

One says:

**Take thoughtful risks.**

The other says:

**You missed the schedule. Explain.**

Guess which one has more gravity.

This is not necessarily hypocrisy.

That's important.

It would be easy to tell this story as though executives secretly cared about execution and merely decorated the corporate website with risk-taking because it sounded innovative.

I don't think that's generally what happens.

Leaders can genuinely value both.

The problem is that one value is easier to operationalize.

Execution leaves evidence that fits naturally into the systems organizations use to manage themselves.

Risk-taking requires interpretation.

Was the risk thoughtful?

Was the potential benefit worth it?

Was the uncertainty understood?

Did we learn something useful?

Would a reasonable person have made the same decision with the information available at the time?

Those are judgment questions.

They don't fit neatly into a cell.

And judgment is expensive.

It requires time.

Context.

Conversation.

Knowledge of what happened.

Sometimes disagreement.

Two intelligent people can examine the same failed experiment and reach different conclusions about whether it was worth attempting.

A metric is considerably more cooperative.

**Schedule missed by twelve days.**

There.

Now we know.

Except, of course, we don't.

We know the schedule was missed by twelve days.

That's real information.

We don't yet know what it means.

But because the number is precise, visible, and easy to compare, it can begin carrying more authority than the messier judgment surrounding it.

This is how measurement can subtly displace judgment.

Not all at once.

Nobody announces:

**From now on, please stop thinking. The dashboard will handle it.**

Instead, the organization gradually becomes accustomed to answering complicated questions with increasingly standardized numbers.

Is this team performing well?

Check the scorecard.

Is this project healthy?

Check the dashboard.

Is this employee productive?

Check the metrics.

Are we innovative?

Check adoption.

Are customers happy?

Check the score.

Each number may contain useful information.

The danger is the accumulation.

Eventually people begin managing the representation instead of continually returning to the reality it represents.

This can make organizations strangely robotic.

The dashboard says green.

Good.

The dashboard says red.

Bad.

Number up.

Celebrate.

Number down.

Explain.

At that point, judgment has not disappeared completely.

But it has been demoted.

Instead of using judgment to interpret the metric, we start using judgment to explain deviations from what the metric has already declared.

That's a subtle but important reversal.

The number has become the default truth.

The human being gets to file an appeal.

And once an organization reaches that point, intelligent people can begin behaving like robotic number chasers.

Not because they've suddenly lost their intelligence.

Because the system has taught them where intelligence is rewarded.

If every conversation eventually comes back to the score, then making the score better becomes an extremely sensible use of attention.

This is where the cultural gravity of metrics becomes self-reinforcing.

The more attention a number receives, the more important it feels.

The more important it feels, the more people optimize around it.

The more people optimize around it, the more leadership watches it.

The number becomes part of the rhythm of the organization.

Meanwhile, the things requiring judgment have to fight for airtime.

Was that a wise risk?

Did that engineer make the team better?

Did we solve the customer's actual problem?

Did someone prevent a problem that never became visible?

Are people thinking deeply enough?

Are we becoming better at what we do, or merely better at producing the evidence that says we are?

Those questions are harder.

That's precisely why we need them.

Metrics focus attention by reducing complexity.

Judgment brings some of that complexity back when we need it.

Healthy organizations need both.

We need the metric to tell us where to look.

Then we need human beings to look.

The danger is not that we measure what matters.

We should.

The danger is assuming that what matters most will always be what measures best.

It won't.

Some values arrive with numbers attached.

Others require us to recognize them.

And if we're not careful, the things that are easiest to count will slowly acquire more authority than the things that require us to think.

## The Values on the Wall and the Values in the Dashboard

Most companies have values.

They're usually good ones.

Integrity. Innovation. Collaboration. Customer focus. Excellence. Courage. Respect. Risk-taking.

Nobody puts **Protect the Quarterly Metrics at All Costs** on the wall.

And I don't think that's because companies are being dishonest.

Most organizations genuinely want the things they say they want. Leaders really do want employees to collaborate. They really do want innovation. They really do want people to take thoughtful risks, develop others, exercise good judgment, and put customers first.

The problem is that some values are much easier to turn into numbers than others.

And numbers travel well.

They fit into dashboards.

They fit into spreadsheets.

They fit into quarterly reviews.

They can be aggregated from a team to a department to a division to an entire company.

They can be compared against last quarter.

They can be compared against a target.

They can be red, yellow, or green.

Try doing that with courage.

**Courage: 73 percent.**

Up four points quarter over quarter.

Strong work, everyone.

We laugh because the idea is obviously ridiculous. Courage isn't unreal. It just doesn't submit easily to that kind of representation.

Neither does wisdom.

Neither does good judgment.

Neither does trust.

Neither does thoughtful risk-taking.

Neither does the willingness to tell your manager something they don't want to hear.

These things can be observed. They can be discussed. Sometimes they can even be evaluated reasonably well.

But they require context.

And context doesn't aggregate nearly as nicely.

This creates an imbalance between the values we **say** and the values we can **see**.

Return to risk-taking and flawless execution.

Both can appear on the company values page.

They look equal there.

Same font.

Same size.

Maybe each gets a nice little icon.

But once they enter the operating system of the organization, they stop being equal.

Flawless execution has dates.

Risk-taking has stories.

Flawless execution has defect counts.

Risk-taking has judgment.

Flawless execution can tell me that a project missed its commitment by twelve days.

Risk-taking requires someone to understand why.

One value comes with evidence that can be automatically collected.

The other needs a conversation.

Guess which one is easier to manage at scale.

This is where spoken values can begin losing power.

Not because people stop believing in them.

Because the measurable values keep showing up.

Every week.

Every month.

Every quarter.

The spoken value appears at the all-hands meeting.

The measurable value appears every Tuesday.

That difference matters.

Imagine an employee deciding whether to pursue an uncertain technical idea.

The idea could produce a significant improvement.

It could also fail.

The company has told the employee:

**Take risks.**

The employee believes them.

But the employee also knows that the project has a committed date.

The date appears on a dashboard.

The dashboard is reviewed by management.

A missed date requires an explanation.

The manager's manager may ask about it.

The team may appear red in the quarterly review.

There is no corresponding cell on the dashboard for:

**Demonstrated exactly the kind of thoughtful risk-taking we keep saying we want.**

So the employee receives two messages.

One is a value.

The other is a value with infrastructure.

That's a very different thing.

The value with infrastructure gets measured.

It gets reviewed.

It gets remembered.

It carries consequences.

The spoken value has to survive on conviction.

This doesn't mean the employee will never take the risk.

People do courageous things inside imperfect systems all the time.

It means the organization has unintentionally made one value more expensive than the other.

If I execute flawlessly, the system recognizes it.

If I take a thoughtful risk and succeed, everyone may celebrate.

If I take a thoughtful risk and fail, now we discover whether risk-taking was actually a value or merely a preference for successful innovation.

That's an important test.

It's easy to celebrate risk-taking after it works.

At that point we usually call it vision.

The real test comes when the risk produces exactly what risks sometimes produce.

Failure.

What happens then?

Do we examine the quality of the decision?

Do we ask what information was available at the time?

Do we consider the potential upside?

Do we ask what was learned?

Or do we point to the missed schedule?

The answer teaches people more about the organization's values than the poster ever could.

This pattern appears everywhere.

A company says:

**We value collaboration.**

But performance reviews emphasize individual accomplishments.

People collaborate, of course.

But they also learn to make sure their own contribution is visible.

A company says:

**Quality comes first.**

But every major review focuses on delivery dates.

People care about quality.

They also know which number they'll be asked to explain.

A company says:

**Put the customer first.**

But support agents are measured on call time.

They want to help customers.

They also know that a thirty-minute conversation has a number attached to it.

A company says:

**Think long term.**

But managers are evaluated every quarter.

They may sincerely care about what happens three years from now.

They are still having a performance conversation in three months.

Again, none of this requires hypocrisy.

That's what makes the problem interesting.

The organization can sincerely believe both messages.

The manager can sincerely believe both messages.

The employee can sincerely believe both messages.

But when the values come into conflict, the measurable one often has an advantage.

It has evidence.

It has visibility.

It has repetition.

And eventually, it may have consequences.

This is how organizations can slowly become different from what they say they are without anyone consciously deciding to change the values.

The values on the wall remain exactly where they were.

Maybe the posters are still hanging.

But employees are learning another set of values through experience.

They learn what gets discussed.

They learn what gets rewarded.

They learn what requires an explanation.

They learn what can safely fail.

They learn what the organization is willing to sacrifice when two good things come into conflict.

That's the real test of a value.

Values are easy when they agree.

Of course we want quality when quality doesn't affect the schedule.

Of course we want collaboration when collaboration doesn't slow individual output.

Of course we want customer care when the customer only needs seven minutes.

Of course we want risk-taking when the risk succeeds.

Values become meaningful when they cost something.

That's when the organization has to choose.

And if the measurable value wins every time, people notice.

Eventually they stop needing the poster to tell them what matters.

They already know.

This doesn't mean we need to invent metrics for everything.

In fact, that may make the problem worse.

The answer to **we aren't measuring courage** is probably not a Courage Dashboard.

Some things need to remain in the world of human judgment.

They need stories.

Conversation.

Observation.

Context.

Leaders who know enough about the work to recognize something valuable even when it doesn't arrive as a number.

That's harder.

It doesn't scale as cleanly.

It may even require us to tolerate disagreement.

But if we only give institutional weight to the values we can quantify, we shouldn't be surprised when the culture gradually organizes itself around the quantifiable.

The things we measure don't automatically become our values.

But they have an enormous advantage.

They have a voice in every meeting.

And unless we're intentional about listening for the things without numbers, that voice can eventually become the loudest one in the room.

## Every Target Is Louder

Numbers have a credibility problem.

They have too much of it.

Put two people in a room and ask whether a project went well, and you'll probably get a conversation.

Someone thinks the team moved too slowly.

Someone else points out that the requirements changed halfway through.

Another person thinks the team handled the changes remarkably well.

Someone mentions a technical problem nobody could have anticipated.

Someone else wonders whether they should have anticipated it anyway.

This is judgment.

It's messy.

Then someone puts a number on the screen.

**Schedule adherence: 82 percent.**

Ah.

Finally.

Reality has arrived.

There is something about a number that feels fundamentally different from a conversation.

The conversation sounds subjective.

The number sounds rigorous.

The conversation contains opinions.

The number contains data.

The conversation might be influenced by personalities, relationships, incomplete information, organizational politics, or someone's mood after a particularly bad cup of coffee.

The number appears to have none of these problems.

It simply sits there.

Eighty-two percent.

No emotion.

No agenda.

No favorite employee.

No bad day.

Just math.

And to be clear, this is one of the great strengths of measurement.

Numbers can challenge our biases.

A manager may believe a team is doing poorly and discover that its results are actually strong. An organization may believe customers are happy and discover otherwise. An engineer may be certain a performance optimization helped, only to run the benchmark and discover that the software is now slower.

Good measurement can correct human perception.

That's why science measures things.

That's why businesses measure things.

That's why I step on a scale even when I am quite certain my pants have become more persuasive evidence.

We need reality to push back.

But numbers are not free from judgment simply because they contain arithmetic.

Before the number ever appeared on the dashboard, someone made choices.

What should we measure?

How should we define it?

What counts?

What doesn't?

What period should we examine?

Should every case carry equal weight?

What should the target be?

Which number deserves a slide in the quarterly review?

Which equally real part of the organization will receive no number at all?

Those are judgment calls.

The calculation may be impartial.

The choice of what to calculate is not.

This is why numbers can be precise without being neutral.

Suppose our schedule adherence really is 82 percent.

Maybe the calculation is flawless.

Every project is accounted for. Every date is correct. The formula has been checked. The spreadsheet is magnificent.

We still haven't answered the most important question.

**What does 82 percent mean?**

Were the missed commitments caused by poor planning?

Changing requirements?

Thoughtful risks?

Bad execution?

Unexpected technical problems?

Dependencies outside the team's control?

Did teams make commitments they never had a realistic chance of meeting?

Did they discover problems that were worth stopping to solve?

The number cannot tell us.

It knows 82 percent.

That's what it knows.

The conversation supplies the rest.

Yet organizations can subtly reverse those roles.

The number becomes the evidence.

The conversation becomes the excuse.

You can hear it in the language.

**The data says we're behind.**

Then someone explains the circumstances.

Now it can sound as though the human being is arguing against reality.

But they may not be arguing against the data at all.

They may agree completely.

Yes, we missed the date.

The conversation is answering a different question:

**Why?**

That's not an attempt to escape the facts.

It's an attempt to understand them.

Judgment has to defend itself.

Numbers often arrive already carrying authority.

And then we make them louder.

We establish a target.

Now the number isn't merely telling us what happened.

It has a reference point.

**Schedule adherence: 82 percent.**

**Target: 95 percent.**

Suddenly 82 has acquired a personality.

It is disappointing.

Put it in red and we've practically given it an annual performance review.

The target may be perfectly reasonable.

Organizations need goals. Targets can create focus. They can tell us what good performance generally looks like. They can help thousands of people coordinate around shared expectations.

But the message has changed.

Tracking something says:

**We think this is worth understanding.**

Setting a target says:

**We expect this to move in a particular direction.**

Then we compare teams.

Team A: 97 percent.

Team B: 94 percent.

Team C: 81 percent.

Now we've added another message:

**We can see how you compare with everyone else.**

Maybe leadership isn't ranking the teams.

It doesn't have to.

The teams can read.

Then consequences arrive.

The metric appears in performance discussions.

Managers have to explain misses.

Organizations are evaluated against it.

Bonuses depend partly on it.

Promotions favor people who consistently deliver it.

Now the message is considerably harder to misunderstand.

This is the progression:

**We measure it.**

**We target it.**

**We compare it.**

**We attach consequences to it.**

At each step, the number gets louder.

And because numbers already carry the appearance of impartiality, this progression can feel almost inevitable.

We're not making a subjective judgment.

**We're just following the data.**

That sentence should make us at least a little nervous.

Data is enormously useful.

But data does not decide what matters.

Human beings decided what to measure.

Human beings decided what success looked like.

Human beings decided where to set the target.

Human beings decided which consequences to attach.

Then, after making all of those decisions, we can point at the resulting number as though nobody made any decisions at all.

**The data says.**

No.

The data tells us something.

We decide what it means.

That distinction is especially important when metrics collide with values that are harder to quantify.

The schedule has a number.

Risk-taking has a conversation.

Individual output has a number.

Collaboration may have a conversation.

Call time has a number.

Compassion for a frustrated customer may have a conversation.

The measurable side already begins with an advantage because it appears more rigorous.

Add a target and the advantage grows.

Add comparison and it grows again.

Add consequences and eventually the conversation barely stands a chance.

This doesn't mean we should distrust numbers.

It means we should stop pretending that numerical and human information belong to two categories called **objective** and **subjective**, with objective information automatically winning.

The number and the conversation may be describing different parts of the same reality.

One provides consistency.

The other provides context.

One allows comparison.

The other allows interpretation.

One can reveal patterns across thousands of cases.

The other can tell us why this particular case was different.

We need both.

A healthy organization should be able to say:

**The number is accurate, and the number is incomplete.**

There is no contradiction in that sentence.

In fact, it's probably true of nearly every metric we've ever created.

The danger comes when we forget the second half.

Because every metric is a message.

Every target is louder.

Every comparison turns up the volume.

And every consequence adds an exclamation point.

Eventually the number can become so loud that we stop hearing the parts of reality that never learned how to count.

## What We Don’t Measure

Dashboards are very good at showing us things.

That's their job.

They take an enormous amount of information and make a small part of it visible.

Revenue.

Costs.

Defects.

Schedule.

Customer satisfaction.

Headcount.

AI adoption.

Whatever the organization has decided it needs to watch.

There it is.

One screen.

Maybe two if someone got ambitious.

This is incredibly useful.

But every dashboard has an invisible companion.

Everything that isn't on it.

Some things are absent because they don't matter.

Good.

We don't need a quarterly metric for the number of staples consumed by the engineering organization.

At least I hope we don't.

Other things are absent for a very different reason.

They're difficult to quantify.

How much mentoring happened this quarter?

How much trust was built?

How many bad ideas were quietly prevented by someone asking the right question at the right moment?

How many employees helped a colleague without receiving any credit for it?

How much thoughtful disagreement prevented a team from making a mistake?

How much institutional knowledge passed from one person to another?

How many managers created an environment where someone felt safe enough to say:

**I think we're making the wrong decision.**

These things are real.

They affect organizations.

They can affect them enormously.

They're just difficult to put on a dashboard.

And if something doesn't appear on the dashboard, it begins with a visibility problem.

Imagine a senior engineer spends an afternoon helping three other engineers work through a difficult technical problem.

The engineer could have spent those hours producing individual output.

Instead, three other people become more capable.

Maybe one of them avoids a serious mistake.

Maybe another learns something that makes them faster for the next five years.

Maybe the third eventually teaches someone else.

The effect spreads through the organization in ways that would be almost impossible to calculate.

At the end of the quarter, what do we see?

The senior engineer produced less.

The other effects may be nowhere.

This doesn't mean the organization doesn't value mentoring.

It may value it deeply.

The manager may recognize exactly what happened.

The other engineers certainly do.

But the dashboard doesn't.

The dashboard isn't being unfair.

It simply has no field for that.

That's the distinction we need to keep making.

**Invisible to the measurement system does not mean nonexistent in reality.**

But invisibility has consequences.

What appears on the dashboard arrives automatically.

Nobody has to remember to mention revenue.

Revenue has a slide.

Nobody has to tell leadership that the schedule slipped.

The red box took care of that.

Nobody needs to make a passionate argument that defects increased.

There's a graph.

Qualitative information doesn't have that luxury.

Someone has to carry it into the room.

Someone has to say:

**There's something happening on this team that I don't think the numbers are showing.**

And now we're back in the world of conversation.

The world we just established has less automatic authority.

Maybe the manager says:

**This engineer's individual output looks lower, but you need to understand how much they're doing for everyone around them.**

That's judgment.

Maybe someone says:

**We're hitting our schedule, but the team is exhausted and I'm worried we're borrowing against the future.**

Judgment.

**Customer satisfaction looks fine, but I'm hearing the same frustration repeatedly in customer conversations.**

Judgment.

**Our AI adoption numbers look excellent, but I'm not convinced we're actually getting much value from some of this usage.**

Judgment.

None of those observations fit as neatly into a dashboard cell.

That doesn't make them less important.

It makes them easier to lose.

This is where the organizational preference for quantitative information can become dangerous.

We begin with a reasonable principle:

**Let's make decisions based on data.**

Absolutely.

Then, without noticing, we can quietly change the sentence:

**If we don't have data, we shouldn't make a decision.**

Then:

**If we can't measure it, we can't really know it.**

And eventually:

**If it isn't measured, it isn't real enough to matter.**

Nobody has to say those sentences out loud.

The information systems can teach them for us.

This creates a strange hierarchy of reality.

Things that can be quantified become official.

Things that require observation and judgment remain anecdotal.

The measurable world has charts.

The qualitative world has stories.

And in many professional environments, calling something **anecdotal** is a remarkably efficient way of making it disappear.

Sometimes that's appropriate.

One angry customer is not necessarily evidence of a systemic problem.

One employee's experience does not tell us how ten thousand employees feel.

A compelling story can distort our judgment just as easily as a compelling number can.

Qualitative information has limitations.

But so does quantitative information.

The answer isn't to choose one form of blindness over another.

It's to recognize what each can see.

Quantitative information is extraordinarily good at revealing patterns.

It can tell us that customer satisfaction fell across six regions.

It can tell us that defects increased after a release.

It can tell us that turnover is rising.

It can tell us that one team consistently misses commitments.

Then qualitative information can help us understand why.

Talk to the customers.

Talk to the engineers.

Talk to the people leaving.

Talk to the team.

Go down to the ground.

Reality is still there.

This is why good management can't happen entirely from a dashboard.

Eventually someone has to encounter the work.

The dashboard can tell you where to look.

It cannot do the looking for you.

And some things may never generate a signal strong enough to appear there at all.

Trust rarely turns red on Tuesday.

Team cohesion doesn't send an alert.

Wisdom doesn't produce a quarterly trend line.

A culture can become less psychologically safe for months before any measurable consequence appears.

An experienced employee can quietly stop caring long before they resign and become part of the turnover statistics.

A team can stop taking risks long before innovation slows enough to show up in the numbers.

By the time some realities become measurable, they have already been real for a very long time.

That means organizations need ways to keep the qualitative world visible.

Not necessarily by forcing it into another metric.

That's an important temptation to resist.

We notice that mentoring is invisible, so we create a mentoring score.

Now everyone must log their mentoring hours.

Six months later, someone is scheduling a fifteen-minute mentoring conversation because they are two hours short of their quarterly mentoring target.

We have learned nothing.

Sometimes the answer to **this isn't on the dashboard** is not **put it on the dashboard**.

Sometimes the answer is:

**Look away from the dashboard.**

Talk to people.

Listen.

Observe.

Ask what isn't showing up.

Make room in meetings for information that doesn't arrive with a percentage sign.

Allow experienced people to say, **Something feels wrong here**, and treat that not as proof, but as information worth investigating.

Ask what the numbers might be missing.

Because dashboards highlight.

They focus.

They make parts of reality extraordinarily visible.

That's their strength.

But the light they cast can make us forget that anything exists outside the beam.

The unmeasured parts of an organization do not disappear.

They simply become easier not to see.

And sometimes the things easiest not to see are the things holding everything else together.

## The Dashboard Learns to Speak

Every profession develops its own language.

Organizations do too.

Spend enough time inside a company and you'll learn an impressive collection of abbreviations, acronyms, ratios, scores, and phrases that would be nearly incomprehensible to anyone walking in from the outside.

This is useful.

Language compresses.

If everyone knows what CSAT means, we don't need to say **customer satisfaction score** every time we discuss customers.

If everyone understands the defect rate, schedule variance, conversion rate, utilization rate, or whatever else the organization tracks, we can communicate complicated information quickly.

The metric becomes shorthand.

But shorthand has an interesting habit.

Eventually we forget what it was short for.

We begin with a question:

**Are our customers getting good help?**

That's a big question.

It includes whether the customer's problem was solved, whether they were treated with respect, whether the process was frustrating, whether the answer was correct, whether they had to call three times, and probably a dozen other things.

That's difficult to discuss at scale.

So we develop a metric.

Maybe it's CSAT.

Now we can ask:

**What's our CSAT?**

Much easier.

And for a while, everyone remembers the relationship.

CSAT is one piece of evidence helping us understand whether customers are having good experiences.

But language compresses thought as well as communication.

Over time:

**Are customers getting good help?**

becomes:

**How's CSAT?**

Then:

**We need to improve CSAT.**

Notice what happened.

The customer disappeared from the sentence.

We didn't mean to remove them.

We just developed a more efficient vocabulary.

This happens everywhere.

**Are we building reliable software?**

becomes:

**What's the defect rate?**

Then:

**We need to get defects below target.**

**Are teams making commitments they can reasonably deliver?**

becomes:

**What's schedule predictability?**

Then:

**We need predictability above 90 percent.**

**Are employees discovering useful ways to use AI?**

becomes:

**What's adoption?**

Then:

**We need adoption up ten points this quarter.**

The original question is still somewhere underneath.

But it gets farther away.

The metric becomes the noun.

The target becomes the verb.

And the organization starts speaking the language of its dashboard.

This matters because language doesn't merely describe what we're thinking about.

It helps determine what is easy to think about.

A phrase like **customer experience** invites a broad conversation.

A phrase like **CSAT** invites us to look at a number.

A question like **Are we taking the right risks?** invites judgment.

A question like **Are we on schedule?** can be answered with a color.

Neither form is inherently better.

Sometimes we need the color.

If a hundred projects are underway, leadership cannot begin every meeting with a forty-five-minute philosophical exploration of the nature of successful execution.

Green, yellow, red is useful.

The problem is when the shorthand stops being shorthand.

The representation begins standing in for the thing itself.

We stop saying:

**The metric suggests customers may be less satisfied.**

We say:

**Customer satisfaction is down.**

Those sentences sound almost identical.

They're not.

One preserves the distinction between the measurement and the reality.

The other collapses them.

Sometimes that collapse is harmless.

If a thermometer says it's 102 degrees outside, I don't feel a strong need to say:

**The temperature measurement produced by this particular instrument suggests that the ambient air may be unusually warm.**

It's hot.

I'm going inside.

Human systems are different.

Our measurements of things like productivity, satisfaction, innovation, engagement, quality, collaboration, and performance are rarely the thing itself.

They're representations.

Often very useful ones.

But the language can make us forget the gap.

This is particularly powerful when a metric acquires a name that sounds like the underlying reality.

Consider **productivity**.

Suppose an organization creates a productivity score from several measurable activities.

Maybe it counts output, completion rates, utilization, or some combination of things someone has thoughtfully researched.

The score may be useful.

But then someone says:

**Productivity increased 12 percent.**

Maybe.

What we know for certain is that the **productivity score** increased 12 percent.

Whether actual productivity increased depends on how well the score represents the much larger thing we decided to call productivity.

That extra word matters.

It reminds us that we're looking at a measurement.

Drop it often enough and the proxy starts sounding like the reality itself.

This is how dashboards gradually teach organizations to speak in abstractions.

Revenue.

Utilization.

Engagement.

Adoption.

Velocity.

Performance.

Efficiency.

These words aren't bad.

They help us communicate across enormous organizations.

But every abstraction leaves something behind.

And the more fluent we become in the abstraction, the easier it is to forget what was left out.

Eventually an organization can have entire conversations in which almost nothing tangible appears.

**We need to increase adoption to improve utilization while maintaining execution against our productivity targets.**

Everyone nods.

Somewhere, presumably, a human being is doing something.

This is why returning to ordinary language can be surprisingly powerful.

**What does that mean?**

Are people actually getting their work done faster?

Are customers happier?

Is the software better?

Are employees spending less time on tedious work?

Are we solving problems we couldn't solve before?

What is happening in the real world that we expect this number to represent?

Ordinary language forces the abstraction to cash itself out.

It makes us reconnect the metric with the thing that gave it meaning.

This doesn't require banning metric language.

That would be ridiculous.

Organizations need shorthand.

Engineers need technical vocabulary.

Executives need abstractions.

Dashboards need labels considerably shorter than **An Imperfect Numerical Representation of a Complicated Human Reality That Should Be Interpreted With Appropriate Context**.

Though I admit I would enjoy seeing that column heading.

The goal is simply to remain bilingual.

We need to speak the language of the dashboard.

And we need to speak the language of the world behind it.

We should be able to move from:

**CSAT declined three points**

to:

**What are customers experiencing?**

From:

**AI adoption increased**

to:

**What are people doing differently, and is it helping?**

From:

**Schedule predictability declined**

to:

**What happened in the work?**

From the abstraction back to reality.

Because once a metric enters the vocabulary, it becomes remarkably easy for the organization to forget that it was ever a proxy at all.

The dashboard doesn't merely show us what to look at.

Eventually, it gives us words for what we see.

And if we use those words long enough, they can begin shaping what we're capable of seeing.

## People Learn What Matters Around Here

Nobody needs to hand you the real employee handbook.

You figure it out.

Spend enough time in an organization and you learn which meetings matter.

You learn which deadlines are actually deadlines.

You learn which processes are mandatory and which ones are more like strongly worded suggestions.

You learn when it's safe to disagree.

You learn which problems need to be escalated immediately and which ones everyone quietly works around.

And you learn what matters.

Not because someone tells you.

Because you watch.

A new employee may begin with the official values.

**We value innovation.**

**We put customers first.**

**We collaborate.**

**We take thoughtful risks.**

**We hold ourselves accountable.**

Good.

Then the employee starts experiencing the organization.

They watch what happens when a project misses its schedule.

They watch what happens when someone takes a risk and fails.

They watch who gets promoted.

They watch who gets praised in the all-hands meeting.

They watch which slides get twenty minutes of discussion and which values get mentioned for thirty seconds at the end.

They watch which numbers require explanations.

They watch what managers ask about.

And they learn.

This is culture.

Culture is partly what an organization says it believes.

But it's also the accumulated answer to a much more practical question:

**What happens around here?**

What happens when quality conflicts with schedule?

What happens when helping someone else reduces my own visible output?

What happens when the right decision makes the dashboard look worse?

What happens when I disagree with someone more powerful than me?

What happens when a thoughtful risk fails?

Those moments teach culture much more effectively than a slide deck.

Because human beings are excellent pattern-recognition machines.

We notice consequences.

If employees repeatedly see people rewarded for shipping quickly, they learn that speed matters.

If people who stop and raise concerns are treated as obstacles, they learn something about risk.

If managers say collaboration matters but promotions consistently go to the people with the most visible individual accomplishments, employees learn how collaboration fits into the hierarchy.

It may matter.

It just doesn't matter as much.

Nobody has to say that.

The system says it.

This is why culture can drift away from intention without anyone consciously deciding to change it.

A leader may sincerely say:

**I want you to use your judgment.**

Then every time someone exercises judgment and produces an unexpected result, the leader asks for an explanation.

Again, asking for an explanation is not unreasonable.

Managers need to understand what happened.

But if following the metric requires no explanation while deviating from it requires a defense, employees learn which path is safer.

The metric becomes the default.

Judgment becomes the exception.

This is particularly powerful because most employees aren't trying to conduct an anthropological study of corporate culture.

They're trying to do their jobs.

They have projects to finish.

They have managers to satisfy.

They have coworkers depending on them.

They have performance reviews.

They have mortgages.

They would generally prefer not to become the subject of an executive meeting.

So they adapt.

This isn't cowardice.

It's learning.

Imagine two employees.

One follows the process exactly, hits the metric, and produces a mediocre outcome.

The other uses judgment, deviates from the process, misses the metric, and produces a better outcome.

If the second employee spends the next three meetings defending the decision while the first employee receives a green box, everyone watching learns something.

They learn that judgment may be valued.

But compliance is safer.

Do that often enough and you don't need to tell people to stop exercising judgment.

They'll figure it out.

The same thing happens with risk-taking.

Suppose leadership repeatedly tells employees:

**We need to take more risks.**

People may genuinely want to.

But then they watch what happens to someone whose risk fails.

Was the decision evaluated based on what was known at the time?

Was the learning recognized?

Was reasonable failure tolerated?

Or did the missed deliverable become the entire story?

People notice.

The next time someone sees a risky opportunity, the organization doesn't have to tell them what to do.

Culture already did.

This is where measurement systems become much more than measurement systems.

They become teaching systems.

Every time a number is reviewed, the organization teaches.

Every time a target is celebrated, the organization teaches.

Every time a red metric requires an explanation, the organization teaches.

Every time someone makes a wise decision that hurts the metric and leadership supports them anyway, the organization teaches.

That's important too.

Metrics don't have to teach bad lessons.

They can teach good ones.

A quality metric can communicate that craftsmanship matters.

A safety metric can communicate that getting people home safely matters more than squeezing out another unit of production.

A customer metric can remind an organization that the people buying its products are not an inconvenient detail.

The question isn't whether metrics shape culture.

They do.

The question is what they're shaping it toward.

And that means leaders need to pay attention not only to the metrics themselves, but to the lessons surrounding them.

Suppose a team misses a target for a good reason.

What happens next matters.

If the leader says:

**I see the number. Walk me through what happened.**

the lesson is:

**The metric matters, and so does context.**

If the explanation reveals a thoughtful decision and the leader says:

**I agree. You made the right call.**

the lesson becomes even stronger:

**The target matters, but the point matters more.**

People remember those moments.

Especially when the number is red.

A leader can give a hundred speeches about empowering employees to use judgment.

One public moment of supporting someone who used good judgment and produced an ugly number may communicate more than all of them.

Because now the value cost something.

The organization had to choose.

And people saw what won.

The reverse is also true.

One executive can spend an hour talking about psychological safety and then humiliate someone for bringing bad news.

Guess which part people remember.

This is why culture is difficult to manage directly.

You can't simply announce it into existence.

Culture forms from thousands of small observations.

What gets attention?

What gets ignored?

What gets rewarded?

What gets punished?

What gets explained?

What gets forgiven?

Who gets trusted?

When does the number win?

When is it allowed to lose?

Eventually people assemble those observations into a working model of the organization.

They may never articulate it.

They don't need to.

They just know.

**Around here, you don't miss a date.**

**Around here, you tell people early when something is going wrong.**

**Around here, the customer really does come first.**

**Around here, you make sure your work is visible.**

**Around here, taking risks is fine as long as they work.**

**Around here, if you have a good reason, your manager will listen.**

Those sentences are culture.

And every metric contributes to them.

This brings us back to the three perspectives we started with.

The measurer sees information.

The measuree experiences an incentive.

And the culture absorbs a value.

None of those perspectives is wrong.

They're simply different views of the same act.

The leader may genuinely be trying to understand the organization.

The employee may genuinely be trying to respond to what leadership cares about.

And the culture quietly records the interaction.

Then it teaches the next person.

That's how a metric becomes more than a number.

It becomes part of the answer to a question every employee eventually learns to ask:

**What really matters around here?**
