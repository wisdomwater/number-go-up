# Chapter 7 — Measure Where You Have Agency

## You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I would like to lose a pound this week.

Excellent.

Let's put it on the calendar.

**Thursday, 2:00–2:30 p.m.: Lose one pound.**

There is only one problem.

I have no idea what to do when the appointment begins.

I know how to go for a walk.

I know how to exercise.

I know how to prepare a meal.

I know how to choose what I'm going to eat.

But I don't know how to perform the activity **lose one pound**.

Weight loss is an outcome.

It's something I want to happen.

It is not something I can directly do.

This distinction seems painfully obvious when stated that way, but we confuse it all the time.

We set goals around outcomes and then hold ourselves accountable as though those outcomes were actions.

Lose twenty pounds.

Get promoted.

Increase my investment portfolio by 10 percent.

Sleep eight hours.

Lower my cholesterol.

Sell ten thousand copies of a book.

Build a successful business.

Have a good marriage.

Raise happy children.

These are all things we might reasonably want.

Some of them are things we can influence enormously.

None of them are completely ours to command.

Take something as simple as sleep.

I can go to bed at ten o'clock.

I can turn off the television.

I can put down my phone.

I can make the room dark.

I can avoid drinking coffee at nine at night, which seems like a reasonable contribution to the project.

What I cannot do is lie down and issue the instruction:

**Sleep for eight hours. Begin.**

My body remains inconveniently involved.

I can create conditions for sleep.

I cannot guarantee the outcome.

Money works the same way.

Suppose I want my investment portfolio to grow by 8 percent this year.

Perfectly reasonable hope.

Perhaps historically informed.

Absolutely terrible personal performance target.

I can decide how much I save.

I can decide how much I invest.

I can choose an investment strategy.

I can control fees to some extent.

I can avoid panic-selling because someone on television used the word *crash* in a sufficiently dramatic tone.

But I do not control the market.

If my portfolio falls during a broad market decline, I haven't necessarily failed.

And if it rises 20 percent during a booming market, I haven't necessarily become Warren Buffett.

The outcome contains my choices.

It also contains the world.

That's the problem with outcomes.

They're mixtures.

My effort is in there.

My judgment is in there.

My habits are in there.

But so are circumstances.

Timing.

Other people.

Biology.

Randomness.

Markets.

Weather.

Opportunity.

Luck.

Things I knew.

Things I didn't know.

Things nobody knew.

The farther we move from an action toward an outcome, the more the rest of reality gets a vote.

This is true at work too.

An engineer can write excellent software and still have a project canceled.

A salesperson can handle an account beautifully and still lose the sale because the customer's budget disappeared.

A manager can lead well and still have someone leave for an opportunity they couldn't possibly match.

A team can make a thoughtful decision that produces a bad result.

And, inconveniently, someone can make a terrible decision and get lucky.

Outcomes matter.

But outcomes are noisy.

This is one reason they can be dangerous as performance judgments.

They combine what I did with what happened.

Then they hand me one number.

My scale does this every morning.

There is a number.

That number contains some information about my recent behavior.

It also contains water.

Salt.

Food.

Digestion.

Hormones.

Exercise.

Timing.

And whatever mysterious biological process causes the scale to wait until the morning after I've done everything right to increase by 1.7 pounds.

The scale doesn't separate the variables.

It just reports the outcome.

Then I have to decide what the outcome means.

The mistake is believing that because I can measure something precisely, I must also control it precisely.

I don't.

There is an important difference between **control** and **influence**.

I influence my weight.

I influence my health.

I influence my finances.

I influence my career.

I influence my relationships.

I influence whether a book succeeds.

But influence is not command.

That matters because accountability works best when it attaches to something we can actually do.

Suppose I decide that my goal this week is to lose two pounds.

Friday arrives.

I lost half a pound.

Did I fail?

Maybe.

Maybe I ignored the plan all week.

Maybe I ate considerably more than I intended and barely moved.

The disappointing outcome might be telling me something important.

Or maybe I followed the plan remarkably well.

I ate thoughtfully.

I exercised.

I walked.

I slept reasonably.

My weight simply didn't move as quickly as I hoped.

Same outcome.

Completely different performance.

That's why outcomes make such poor daily judges.

They tell us where we ended up.

They don't necessarily tell us how we traveled.

And yet we need outcomes.

If I follow the same weight-loss plan for six months and gain twenty pounds, eventually I should probably stop congratulating myself on process adherence.

Something isn't working.

If I save diligently for years but my financial plan keeps moving me farther from my actual goals, I need to reconsider the plan.

If I write every morning but nobody can understand what I've written, the consistency of my writing habit does not settle the question of whether the writing is good.

The destination matters.

But so does the distinction between destination and steering.

Maybe that's the better way to think about it.

When I'm driving somewhere, I care very much about the destination.

I look at the map.

I watch whether I'm getting closer.

If I discover that I've been driving east for two hours and my destination is west, that information deserves my attention.

But I cannot drive by staring at the destination.

I have to steer the car.

Our personal measurement systems often ask us to stare at the destination.

Weight.

Net worth.

Sales.

Rank.

Score.

Performance.

Outcome.

Then we wonder why we feel powerless when the number doesn't move on command.

The better question is closer.

Not:

**Can I control the outcome?**

Usually I can't.

But:

**What can I do that makes the outcome more likely?**

That is where agency begins.

**Outcomes are things we want. Actions are things we do.**

If I'm going to hold myself accountable, I should know which one I'm looking at.

## Outcomes Still Matter

There is a very convenient conclusion I could draw from the last section.

If outcomes are outside my direct control, perhaps I should stop worrying about them.

Problem solved.

Throw away the scale.

Stop checking the investment account.

Don't worry about whether anyone buys the book.

Just focus on the process.

Trust the system.

This is appealing.

It is also nonsense.

Outcomes matter.

A lot.

If I am trying to lose weight, my weight matters.

Not every day.

Not every fluctuation.

Not as a judgment of whether I was a good person yesterday.

But over time, if the goal is weight loss, whether I am actually losing weight seems like relevant information.

The same is true everywhere else.

If I am saving for retirement, the value of my investments matters.

If I own a business, whether the business makes money matters.

If I write books, whether anyone reads them tells me something.

If I exercise because I want to become stronger, whether I become stronger matters.

If a company changes a process to reduce defects, the defect rate matters.

Process without outcomes can become its own form of self-deception.

I followed the plan.

Great.

Did the plan work?

Those are different questions.

And we need both.

**Am I doing what I intended to do?**

**Is what I'm doing actually working?**

The first question is about behavior.

The second is about reality.

This is where outcome metrics earn their place.

They close the feedback loop.

Suppose I decide to lose weight by eating a particular way.

I follow the plan carefully.

Day after day.

Week after week.

My process metrics look fantastic.

I hit my calorie target.

I exercise.

I walk.

I prepare meals.

I sleep reasonably well.

If I stop there, I can give myself an excellent performance review.

But suppose six months later I weigh exactly the same.

That matters.

It doesn't mean the previous six months were a moral failure.

It doesn't mean I lack discipline.

It doesn't even necessarily mean every part of the plan was wrong.

It means the outcome is giving me information.

**Something about this approach is not producing the result I expected.**

Now I need to learn.

Maybe my assumptions were wrong.

Maybe my measurements were inaccurate.

Maybe the target needs adjustment.

Maybe something else is happening that I haven't considered.

Whatever the explanation, reality gets a vote.

This is one of the reasons measurement became so powerful in the first place.

It allows our theories to fail.

Without feedback, I can continue believing in a system indefinitely.

I can say:

**This should be working.**

The outcome can respond:

**That's interesting. It isn't.**

That conversation is valuable.

It is the same conversation a good organization should have with its metrics.

Imagine a software team adopts a new development process.

Everyone follows it perfectly.

All the required meetings happen.

Every template is completed.

Every checklist is checked.

Adoption reaches 100 percent.

Management is delighted.

Then software quality gets worse and delivery gets slower.

At some point, **but everyone followed the process** stops being a defense of the process.

The process exists for the outcome.

Not the other way around.

This is particularly important because process metrics can feel morally superior.

They're closer to effort.

They're closer to our choices.

So they can feel fairer.

I went to the gym.

I hit my calorie target.

I wrote for an hour.

I saved the money.

I did the thing.

That deserves recognition.

But doing the thing doesn't guarantee that it was the right thing.

I can be extraordinarily disciplined in the wrong direction.

There is probably no shortage of people who have followed ineffective plans with admirable consistency.

Discipline is a multiplier.

It does not automatically choose what gets multiplied.

That's why outcomes remain essential.

They help us test the relationship between our actions and our goals.

The key is to give outcomes the right job.

An outcome is excellent at answering:

**Where are we?**

It is less reliable at answering:

**Why are we here?**

And it is often terrible at answering:

**Whose fault is it?**

My weight trend can tell me whether my weight is generally moving up or down.

It cannot, by itself, tell me why.

My investment balance can tell me whether my portfolio grew.

It cannot tell me whether I made wise decisions or happened to own assets during a strong market.

A sales number can tell a company how much it sold.

It cannot, by itself, distinguish brilliant execution from favorable conditions.

The outcome is evidence.

It is not the entire explanation.

This gives us a healthier division of labor.

Actions help me evaluate my behavior.

Outcomes help me evaluate my assumptions.

That's not absolute, but it's a useful starting point.

If the scale jumps tomorrow morning after I followed my plan, I don't need to rewrite the plan.

If the scale trends upward for six months while I follow my plan, I probably do.

Time matters.

Patterns matter.

One data point may be noise.

A persistent outcome deserves curiosity.

This lets us avoid both extremes.

On one side:

**The outcome is everything.**

Number down, success.

Number up, failure.

We already know where that leads.

On the other:

**The outcome doesn't matter. Just trust the process.**

That can become equally absurd.

If the process never produces the thing it exists to produce, perhaps it would appreciate a performance review of its own.

The relationship should be a loop.

I act.

Reality responds.

I observe.

I learn.

I adjust.

Then I act again.

Measurement belongs inside that loop.

Not above it.

The outcome doesn't get to declare my worth.

But it does get to challenge my strategy.

That is a much more useful kind of authority.

So yes, measure the destination.

Know where you are.

Watch the trend.

Ask whether your choices seem to be taking you where you intended to go.

Just don't confuse watching the destination with steering the car.

For steering, we need to move the measurement closer.

Closer to the choices.

Closer to the behavior.

Closer to the place where something is actually mine to do.

Closer to agency.

## Move the Measurement Closer to Agency

So if outcomes matter but make poor daily performance reviews, where should I look?

Move closer.

Closer to the decision.

Closer to the action.

Closer to the place where I can actually do something.

Take weight loss.

I cannot directly control what the scale says tomorrow morning.

But I have considerably more influence over what I eat today.

I can choose breakfast.

I can choose lunch.

I can choose whether I go through the drive-through on the way home.

I can choose whether I exercise.

I can choose whether I go for a walk.

I can choose whether I keep eating after I'm full because there are still fries sitting there and apparently leaving three fries uneaten feels like an unresolved moral issue.

These choices aren't perfectly under my control.

Nothing involving human behavior is.

I'm affected by hunger, stress, sleep, environment, habit, schedule, available food, and the fact that pizza exists.

But these choices are much closer to me than tomorrow morning's weight.

That's the principle:

**Measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency.**

If I want to hold myself accountable, move the measurement toward something I can do.

Not:

**Did I lose two pounds this week?**

But:

**Did I generally eat the way I intended to eat?**

**Did I move my body?**

**Did I exercise?**

**Did I make choices consistent with the goal?**

Now the measurement is closer to behavior.

The scale still matters.

It tells me whether those behaviors appear to be producing the outcome I want over time.

But it no longer has to conduct my daily performance review.

This distinction applies far beyond weight.

Suppose I want to write a successful book.

I would very much like people to buy it.

I might even have a number in mind.

Ten thousand copies.

Sounds nice.

Let's do that.

Unfortunately, **sell ten thousand copies** is another activity I cannot put on Tuesday's calendar.

I can write.

I can revise.

I can publish.

I can tell people the book exists.

I can improve the description.

I can talk with readers.

I can learn from what resonates and what doesn't.

I can do things that increase the likelihood of sales.

Then thousands of other human beings get a vote.

The sales number matters.

It tells me something about how the book is reaching the world.

But if I measure myself only by sales, I have handed my daily sense of success to a large group of strangers who don't know they're participating.

Better to move closer.

**Did I write?**

**Did I make the book better?**

**Did I do the work that was mine to do?**

Then periodically look up and ask whether the work is producing the outcome I hoped for.

Finances work the same way.

I might want to retire with a certain amount of money.

That's an outcome.

Markets will have quite a bit to say about whether I get there.

I cannot control what the S&P 500 does this year.

I can control my spending much more directly.

I can decide how much I save.

I can decide whether I invest consistently.

I can decide whether I abandon a long-term strategy because the market had a frightening week.

The portfolio balance is important.

My behavior is closer to agency.

Career goals get even messier.

Suppose I want a promotion.

I can work hard.

I can develop skills.

I can take responsibility.

I can communicate well.

I can help other people succeed.

I can ask for feedback.

I can make my ambitions known.

I cannot walk into the promotion system and press the **Promote Me** button.

Other people are involved.

Budgets are involved.

Organizational politics may be involved.

Timing is involved.

Opportunities are involved.

Maybe someone else is simply better qualified.

If I make the promotion itself my measure of personal performance, I am assigning myself responsibility for a decision I do not make.

That doesn't mean becoming passive.

It means putting accountability in the right place.

What was mine to do?

Did I do it?

Relationships make the distinction even clearer.

Suppose I want a close relationship with someone I love.

I cannot directly produce closeness.

There is no dashboard setting for **Increase Marriage Quality 12 Percent**.

I can listen.

I can be present.

I can make time.

I can express affection.

I can apologize when I'm wrong.

I can tell the truth.

I can put down my phone.

I can try to understand before trying to be understood.

Those are actions.

Closeness emerges between people.

It cannot be manufactured by one of them.

This is one of the deeper reasons agency matters.

When I measure myself primarily by outcomes, I can accidentally make myself responsible for the entire system.

But I am almost never the entire system.

My body is a system.

A market is a system.

A workplace is a system.

A relationship is a system.

The world is full of other variables.

I participate.

Sometimes powerfully.

But participation and control are not the same thing.

Moving measurement closer to agency helps restore that distinction.

It tells me:

**This part belongs to you.**

That can actually make accountability stronger.

If I judge myself by the outcome, I always have an escape route.

The market was bad.

My body is stubborn.

Nobody buys books anymore.

Management doesn't recognize talent.

My spouse was in a bad mood.

Maybe some of those things are true.

But move closer to agency and the questions become harder to dodge.

Did I save the money?

Did I write?

Did I exercise?

Did I listen?

Did I prepare?

Did I make the choice I said I wanted to make?

Now we're standing somewhere much closer to responsibility.

Not total responsibility for what happened.

Responsibility for my contribution to what happened.

That's a more honest kind of accountability.

It avoids two equally tempting fantasies.

The first is:

**Everything is under my control.**

If the outcome is bad, I failed.

The second is:

**Nothing is under my control.**

If the outcome is bad, there's nothing I could have done.

Usually reality is somewhere in between.

I have agency.

I do not have sovereignty.

That's an important difference.

I can act.

I can choose.

I can influence.

I can learn.

I can change my contribution.

Then reality does whatever reality does with it.

This means a healthy personal measurement system probably needs two different kinds of information.

I need some way of seeing outcomes:

**Is this going where I want it to go?**

And I need some way of seeing behavior:

**Am I doing the things that make that outcome more likely?**

One keeps me connected to reality.

The other keeps me connected to agency.

Together they create a much better conversation than either one alone.

If my behavior is good and the outcome is good, keep going.

If my behavior is poor and the outcome is poor, I have somewhere obvious to look.

If my behavior is poor and the outcome is good, I may be getting lucky.

And if my behavior is good while the outcome remains poor, I may need to reconsider my assumptions.

Now the numbers are helping me think.

They're not simply telling me whether I won.

This is what good measurement should do.

It should help us learn where our choices meet reality.

And perhaps that is the most useful place to hold ourselves accountable:

**as close as possible to the point where choice becomes action.**

## The Serenity Prayer Has Entered the Dashboard

There is an old prayer that has probably appeared on more coffee mugs, recovery-group walls, and refrigerator magnets than almost any other piece of theology:

**God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change,
courage to change the things I can,
and wisdom to know the difference.**

Apparently it also has something to say about dashboards.

Because much of the anxiety created by personal measurement comes from failing to make exactly this distinction.

What can I change?

What can I influence?

What simply happens?

These are not the same category.

The distinction is ancient.

Long before smartwatches and spreadsheets, philosophers were wrestling with the limits of human control.

The Stoics, for example, placed enormous emphasis on distinguishing what belongs to us from what does not. Our choices, judgments, and actions are ours in a way that reputation, fortune, health, and other people's behavior are not.

The Serenity Prayer expresses something similar in language many of us know more intuitively.

There are things I can change.

There are things I cannot.

And then there is the difficult middle category that causes most of the trouble:

Things I can influence but cannot control.

My health lives there.

So does my career.

My financial future.

My relationships.

The success of my work.

Probably most things I care deeply about.

I can influence them enormously.

That makes it tempting to believe I control them.

Then something goes wrong.

The scale doesn't move.

The promotion doesn't happen.

The market falls.

Someone rejects the work.

A relationship changes.

And because I believed the outcome belonged to me, I experience the outcome as a verdict on what I did.

This is where measurement can quietly make the illusion stronger.

Numbers create the appearance of control.

If something can be measured, tracked, charted, and graphed, it begins to feel manageable.

There is a line.

There is a target.

There is a gap between the two.

Surely my job is simply to close the gap.

That's how engineering works surprisingly often.

If a processor is using too much power, we can investigate.

If software is too slow, we can profile it.

If a manufacturing process produces too many defects, we can change the process.

Cause and effect may be complicated, but the assumption that intervention can change the system is often reasonable.

Then we take the same mindset into life.

Target weight: 200.

Current weight: 247.

Gap: 47.

Excellent.

We have identified the bug.

Unfortunately, I am not a bug.

My life is not an engineering problem with every variable available for inspection.

Neither is yours.

Human beings live inside systems we influence without controlling.

That doesn't make us powerless.

It makes us participants.

There is a difference.

Suppose I have an important job interview.

There are many things I can do.

I can prepare.

I can research the company.

I can think carefully about likely questions.

I can show up on time.

I can communicate clearly.

I can be honest.

I can present my experience well.

Those things belong largely to me.

Then the interview ends.

Now other people decide.

Maybe another candidate has exactly the experience they need.

Maybe the position gets eliminated.

Maybe someone has an internal candidate in mind.

Maybe I simply wasn't the best person for the job.

At some point, my agency ends.

That boundary matters.

Before the interview, **I have work to do.**

After the interview, **I have reality to accept.**

Of course, I can learn from the outcome.

I can ask for feedback.

I can prepare differently next time.

Acceptance doesn't mean refusing to learn.

It means recognizing that I cannot reach backward through time and force another person to choose me.

The same principle applies to much smaller things.

I can go to bed.

I cannot command sleep.

I can eat thoughtfully.

I cannot command tomorrow's weight.

I can invest wisely.

I cannot command the market.

I can love someone.

I cannot command love in return.

I can write something as truthfully and carefully as I know how.

I cannot command anyone to care.

There is something liberating about this.

Not because outcomes stop mattering.

They still matter deeply.

But I don't have to carry responsibility for every variable in the universe.

I have to carry mine.

That is enough.

And sometimes carrying mine is hard.

This is where the *courage* part of the Serenity Prayer matters.

“Focus on what you can control” can sound suspiciously like permission to shrug.

**Well, the outcome isn't under my control. Nothing I can do.**

No.

There is plenty I can do.

Agency creates responsibility.

If my health matters to me, I have choices to make.

If my marriage matters to me, I have ways of showing up.

If my work matters to me, I have effort to give.

If my finances matter to me, I have decisions to make.

Acceptance of limits is not passivity.

It is an attempt to spend effort where effort can actually do something.

That's the wisdom part.

And wisdom is considerably harder to put on a dashboard.

The world rarely divides neatly into two columns labeled **CONTROLLABLE** and **NOT CONTROLLABLE**.

Most important things sit somewhere on a spectrum.

I may have little control over an outcome but enormous influence.

Or considerable control over one part and almost none over another.

Even my own behavior isn't perfectly controllable.

Fatigue matters.

Stress matters.

Habit matters.

Environment matters.

Biology matters.

The point isn't to calculate an exact Agency Percentage and add another column to the spreadsheet.

Please don't.

The point is orientation.

When a number bothers me, I can ask:

**What part of this is mine?**

That's a remarkably useful question.

The scale went up.

What part is mine?

The portfolio went down.

What part is mine?

The project failed.

What part is mine?

The relationship is struggling.

What part is mine?

Sometimes the answer will be uncomfortable.

I made choices that contributed to this.

Good.

Now I have somewhere to act.

Sometimes the answer will be:

Very little.

That's useful too.

Maybe there is something to learn but nothing to fix.

Maybe the appropriate response isn't optimization.

Maybe it's acceptance.

This is one of the limits of number-go-up culture.

Optimization assumes there is always another lever.

Another adjustment.

Another intervention.

Another technique.

Another way to improve the result.

Sometimes there is.

Sometimes the wisest thing we can do is pull the right lever.

And sometimes wisdom is recognizing that there is no lever.

Reality is not obligated to provide one.

That doesn't fit particularly well on a dashboard.

But it fits human life remarkably well.

Measure what matters.

Watch the outcomes.

Pay attention to what reality tells you.

Then bring the question back toward yourself:

**What is mine to do?**

Do that.

And let the rest be reality.

## Process Metrics Can Become Tyrants Too

At this point, I may have accidentally created a new problem.

I started with one number.

Weight.

Then I argued that weight is an outcome and that I should move measurement closer to the choices I can actually make.

Excellent.

So now, instead of tracking weight, I can track calories.

And protein.

And carbohydrates.

And fiber.

And steps.

And workouts.

And minutes of exercise.

And hours of sleep.

And water.

And servings of vegetables.

And meal timing.

And resting heart rate.

And heart-rate variability.

And—

Wait.

I think I liked it better when I just had the scale.

This is one of the great temptations of process metrics.

Once we realize that outcomes are noisy and actions are closer to agency, the obvious solution is to measure all the actions.

If one metric was causing trouble, perhaps seventeen better metrics will fix it.

Now I have a comprehensive personal performance-management system.

I can wake up, review my sleep score, weigh myself, check my resting heart rate, log breakfast, monitor my protein intake, count my steps, record my workout, track dinner, and finish the evening by reviewing the day's results.

Then, assuming I have any time left, I can live.

This is not what I mean by measuring closer to agency.

Process metrics have real advantages.

If I'm trying to lose weight, knowing something about how much I eat is often more actionable than staring angrily at the scale.

If I'm trying to become more active, tracking movement can help.

If I'm trying to write a book, measuring whether I'm actually writing gives me information that book sales cannot.

These metrics sit closer to my choices.

But closer does not mean perfect.

A process metric is still a metric.

And every metric leaves something out.

Take calories.

Calories are useful information.

If I consistently consume much more energy than my body needs, that matters for weight loss.

But a calorie number does not completely describe food.

Five hundred calories can represent very different meals.

They can differ in protein, fiber, nutrients, volume, satisfaction, and how I feel two hours later.

The calorie number sees something important.

It does not see everything important.

Steps work the same way.

Ten thousand steps tells me something about movement.

It doesn't tell me whether I lifted weights.

It doesn't tell me whether I spent an hour cycling.

It doesn't tell me how my body feels.

It doesn't tell me whether I need recovery.

It doesn't tell me whether walking 10,000 steps today is actually a good idea.

It's a proxy.

A useful one.

We haven't escaped proxies by moving closer to agency.

We've simply chosen different ones.

And Goodhart is perfectly capable of following us home.

If calories become the target, I can optimize calories.

If steps become the target, I can optimize steps.

If workouts become the target, I can optimize workout count.

If books become the target, I can optimize books read.

The same strange behaviors begin appearing.

Maybe I choose foods because they fit the calorie target even though they leave me hungry.

Maybe I exercise when I should rest because I don't want to break the streak.

Maybe I turn an easy walk into a required march around the neighborhood at 9:45 p.m.

Maybe I choose shorter books because Goodreads has become my performance-management system.

The process metric begins doing exactly what the outcome metric did.

It becomes the point.

There is another danger too.

The more things I measure, the more opportunities I create to fail.

Suppose I have an excellent day.

I eat thoughtfully.

I exercise.

I feel good.

I spend time with people I love.

I get meaningful work done.

Then I review the dashboard.

Protein was low.

Steps: 8,412.

Sleep: 6 hours, 53 minutes.

Water target missed.

Suddenly a perfectly good day has several red cells.

This is the peculiar magic of a sufficiently comprehensive dashboard.

It can find disappointment anywhere.

Organizations do this too.

Give a team enough metrics and something will always be off target.

Schedule is good, but quality slipped.

Quality is good, but productivity is down.

Productivity is good, but adoption is low.

Adoption is good, but utilization is—

At some point, everyone would like to go home.

Personal dashboards can create the same environment.

There is always another dimension to optimize.

And modern technology makes this nearly irresistible because the cost of collecting another number is so low.

The sensor already has the data.

The app already has the chart.

Why wouldn't I look?

Because attention has a cost.

That's something dashboards don't measure particularly well.

Every metric I track occupies a little mental space.

Every target creates another expectation.

Every notification asks for attention.

Every red number presents a tiny problem to solve.

One metric may help me focus.

Twenty metrics may simply give me twenty things to worry about.

This is where optimization culture sneaks back in.

If health matters, surely more health data is better.

If productivity matters, surely more productivity data is better.

If sleep matters, surely more sleep data is better.

But information has diminishing returns.

At some point, another measurement stops helping me make better decisions.

It simply gives me another measurement.

There is an important distinction between **actionable information** and **available information**.

Modern technology gives us enormous amounts of available information.

That doesn't mean I need all of it.

If my watch tells me twelve different things about my sleep but none of them changes what I do, I may not have twelve useful metrics.

I may have one useful metric surrounded by eleven interesting facts.

Or perhaps the information is useful occasionally.

I don't need to review it every morning.

This is another place where corporate dashboards have something to teach us.

A good dashboard isn't the one with the most data.

It's the one that helps someone make better decisions.

Personal dashboards should have the same standard.

**What decision will this number help me make?**

That's a wonderfully clarifying question.

If I track protein, what will I do differently?

If I track steps, what will I do differently?

If I track sleep, what will I do differently?

If I track my weight, what will I do differently?

If the answer is clear, great.

The measurement may be useful.

If the answer is:

**Nothing, but I enjoy knowing it,**

that's fine too.

Curiosity is allowed.

Not every number needs to justify its existence through productivity.

But if the answer is:

**Mostly it gives me another opportunity to feel like I'm behind,**

perhaps the metric isn't earning its place.

This is the danger of replacing outcome obsession with process obsession.

We can move measurement closer to agency and still give the measurement too much authority.

We can become wonderfully disciplined about things that were only ever supposed to help us.

The process exists for the point.

The calorie target exists for health.

The step goal exists for movement.

The writing schedule exists for writing.

The budget exists for financial well-being.

The meditation streak exists for—

Actually, if I'm meditating primarily to preserve the meditation streak, we may need to have a separate conversation.

The hierarchy remains the same.

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment.**

Process metrics don't get an exemption just because they're closer to our choices.

They're still servants.

And sometimes the most sophisticated personal measurement system is not the one that tracks everything.

It's the one that knows what it can safely leave unmeasured.

## Rigor and Intuition Need Each Other

There are two wonderfully convenient ways to avoid reality.

The first is to ignore the numbers.

The second is to hide behind them.

I am capable of both.

Suppose I'm trying to lose weight and I don't want to track anything.

I know my body.

I know when I'm hungry.

I know what reasonable portions look like.

I don't need an app telling me what to eat.

Fair enough.

There is wisdom in learning to pay attention to your body.

But there is also the minor complication that I am the same person who gained the weight I'm now trying to lose.

Perhaps my intuition deserves a seat at the table.

I'm not sure it should chair the meeting.

This is where rigor helps.

Measure the food for a while.

Look at the portions.

Track what you're actually eating.

Compare memory with reality.

Maybe my instincts are excellent.

Maybe I discover that my intuitive tablespoon of peanut butter is approximately the size of a tennis ball.

Now I've learned something.

Measurement can challenge the stories we tell ourselves.

That's one of its greatest strengths.

We say we're eating reasonably.

Measure.

We say we're not spending much.

Measure.

We say we're exercising regularly.

Measure.

We say we're working on the important project.

Look at the calendar.

Data has a wonderful ability to ask:

**Are you sure?**

We need that.

Without some kind of external feedback, intuition can become self-deception with excellent public relations.

But then we can make the opposite mistake.

Once we discover that numbers can correct intuition, we decide numbers are more trustworthy than intuition.

Now the spreadsheet gets the chair.

The number says 1,800 calories.

So I eat 1,800 calories.

I'm starving.

Doesn't matter.

The spreadsheet has spoken.

My watch says I need another workout.

My body feels exhausted.

Doesn't matter.

The ring is still open.

My sleep tracker says I slept poorly.

I feel refreshed.

Apparently I am mistaken about being awake.

This is where rigor becomes its own kind of foolishness.

The number is objective about what it measures.

That does not make it omniscient.

A calorie target does not know how hungry I am.

A fitness tracker does not know exactly how recovered my body is.

A productivity system does not know whether I need to spend an afternoon thinking instead of completing tasks.

A budget does not know that an unexpected opportunity is worth spending money on.

The measurement has information.

So do I.

This creates a tension we cannot solve by choosing one side.

**Rigor without intuition becomes tyranny.**

**Intuition without rigor can become self-deception.**

We need both.

Rigor says:

**Let's look at what actually happened.**

Intuition says:

**Let's understand what it meant.**

Rigor gives us resistance.

It pushes back against convenient stories.

Intuition gives us context.

It notices things the measurement system cannot see.

Each one protects us from the other's weakness.

This is particularly important because some forms of knowledge are difficult to quantify but still very real.

I know what it feels like when I'm exhausted.

I know when a conversation with someone I love needs another hour.

I know when I'm doing shallow work because I want the satisfaction of checking boxes.

I know when a plan is technically working but becoming impossible to sustain.

I know when I'm following the rules while somehow missing the point.

At least sometimes I know.

And sometimes I absolutely don't.

That's why the numbers get to stay.

The goal isn't to decide whether data or experience is more trustworthy.

The better question is:

**What does each one know?**

Suppose I've been following a weight-loss plan for several weeks.

The scale isn't moving.

The data says something.

Pay attention.

But my experience also says something.

Am I hungry all the time?

Do I have energy?

Can I imagine eating this way six months from now?

Am I becoming stronger?

Do my clothes fit differently?

Is the plan making my life better or merely making my food log impressive?

Those questions don't invalidate the scale.

They enlarge the picture.

Likewise, suppose I feel like the plan is going wonderfully.

I'm enjoying the food.

I feel good.

Everything seems sustainable.

But over six months my weight continues climbing.

The experience doesn't invalidate the outcome either.

Reality is giving me information I need to integrate.

This is what judgment does.

It holds different kinds of evidence together.

Not perfectly.

Not mathematically.

Human judgment is messy.

It can be biased.

It can be inconsistent.

It can be wrong.

But removing judgment doesn't remove those problems.

It simply hides judgment inside the measurement system.

Someone still chose what to measure.

Someone chose the target.

Someone decided what counts as good.

Someone decided how much authority the number receives.

When I'm measuring myself, that someone is me.

There is no escape from judgment.

The question is whether I exercise it consciously.

This applies far beyond health.

A writer can track word count.

That's useful.

Some days 2,000 words might represent excellent progress.

Other days deleting 2,000 words might be the best writing decision of the week.

The metric says negative productivity.

The manuscript says thank you.

A manager can count meetings with employees.

Useful perhaps.

But twelve scheduled one-on-ones do not necessarily equal twelve meaningful conversations.

A parent can count hours spent with children.

But presence is not measured in minutes alone.

A person can track meditation.

But forty consecutive days of sitting doesn't tell us what happened in the silence.

The quantitative and qualitative dimensions aren't enemies.

They answer different questions.

One gives structure.

The other gives texture.

One sees patterns.

The other sees meaning.

One can tell us **how much**.

The other often helps us understand **what kind**.

Wisdom requires moving between them.

That means sometimes letting the number challenge the story.

And sometimes letting the story challenge the number.

**I know the metric looks good, but something feels wrong.**

Pay attention.

**I know the metric looks bad, but here's what happened.**

Pay attention.

We wanted managers to create room for those sentences at work.

We should create room for them inside ourselves too.

This is not permission to explain away every uncomfortable result.

It is permission to investigate it.

The difference matters.

The purpose of measurement isn't obedience.

It's learning.

If the number reveals something my intuition missed, learn.

If my experience reveals something the number cannot see, learn.

If they disagree, get curious.

The disagreement itself may be the most useful information available.

Because neither the number nor my intuition gets to be the final authority.

The point does.

What am I actually trying to accomplish?

What kind of life am I trying to build?

What is this measurement supposed to help me understand?

Once those questions are clear, rigor and intuition can stop fighting for control.

They can start doing what they do best.

Helping me see.
