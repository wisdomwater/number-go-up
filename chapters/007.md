# Chapter 7 — Measure Where You Have Agency

## You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I would like to lose a pound this week.

Excellent.

Let's put it on the calendar.

**Thursday, 2:00–2:30 p.m.: Lose one pound.**

There is only one problem.

I have no idea what to do when the appointment begins.

I know how to go for a walk.

I know how to exercise.

I know how to prepare a meal.

I know how to choose what I'm going to eat.

But I don't know how to perform the activity **lose one pound**.

Weight loss is an outcome.

It's something I want to happen.

It is not something I can directly do.

This distinction seems painfully obvious when stated that way, but we confuse it all the time.

We set goals around outcomes and then hold ourselves accountable as though those outcomes were actions.

Lose twenty pounds.

Get promoted.

Increase my investment portfolio by 10 percent.

Sleep eight hours.

Lower my cholesterol.

Sell ten thousand copies of a book.

Build a successful business.

Have a good marriage.

Raise happy children.

These are all things we might reasonably want.

Some of them are things we can influence enormously.

None of them are completely ours to command.

Take something as simple as sleep.

I can go to bed at ten o'clock.

I can turn off the television.

I can put down my phone.

I can make the room dark.

I can avoid drinking coffee at nine at night, which seems like a reasonable contribution to the project.

What I cannot do is lie down and issue the instruction:

**Sleep for eight hours. Begin.**

My body remains inconveniently involved.

I can create conditions for sleep.

I cannot guarantee the outcome.

Money works the same way.

Suppose I want my investment portfolio to grow by 8 percent this year.

Perfectly reasonable hope.

Perhaps historically informed.

Absolutely terrible personal performance target.

I can decide how much I save.

I can decide how much I invest.

I can choose an investment strategy.

I can control fees to some extent.

I can avoid panic-selling because someone on television used the word *crash* in a sufficiently dramatic tone.

But I do not control the market.

If my portfolio falls during a broad market decline, I haven't necessarily failed.

And if it rises 20 percent during a booming market, I haven't necessarily become Warren Buffett.

The outcome contains my choices.

It also contains the world.

That's the problem with outcomes.

They're mixtures.

My effort is in there.

My judgment is in there.

My habits are in there.

But so are circumstances.

Timing.

Other people.

Biology.

Randomness.

Markets.

Weather.

Opportunity.

Luck.

Things I knew.

Things I didn't know.

Things nobody knew.

The farther we move from an action toward an outcome, the more the rest of reality gets a vote.

This is true at work too.

An engineer can write excellent software and still have a project canceled.

A salesperson can handle an account beautifully and still lose the sale because the customer's budget disappeared.

A manager can lead well and still have someone leave for an opportunity they couldn't possibly match.

A team can make a thoughtful decision that produces a bad result.

And, inconveniently, someone can make a terrible decision and get lucky.

Outcomes matter.

But outcomes are noisy.

This is one reason they can be dangerous as performance judgments.

They combine what I did with what happened.

Then they hand me one number.

My scale does this every morning.

There is a number.

That number contains some information about my recent behavior.

It also contains water.

Salt.

Food.

Digestion.

Hormones.

Exercise.

Timing.

And whatever mysterious biological process causes the scale to wait until the morning after I've done everything right to increase by 1.7 pounds.

The scale doesn't separate the variables.

It just reports the outcome.

Then I have to decide what the outcome means.

The mistake is believing that because I can measure something precisely, I must also control it precisely.

I don't.

There is an important difference between **control** and **influence**.

I influence my weight.

I influence my health.

I influence my finances.

I influence my career.

I influence my relationships.

I influence whether a book succeeds.

But influence is not command.

That matters because accountability works best when it attaches to something we can actually do.

Suppose I decide that my goal this week is to lose two pounds.

Friday arrives.

I lost half a pound.

Did I fail?

Maybe.

Maybe I ignored the plan all week.

Maybe I ate considerably more than I intended and barely moved.

The disappointing outcome might be telling me something important.

Or maybe I followed the plan remarkably well.

I ate thoughtfully.

I exercised.

I walked.

I slept reasonably.

My weight simply didn't move as quickly as I hoped.

Same outcome.

Completely different performance.

That's why outcomes make such poor daily judges.

They tell us where we ended up.

They don't necessarily tell us how we traveled.

And yet we need outcomes.

If I follow the same weight-loss plan for six months and gain twenty pounds, eventually I should probably stop congratulating myself on process adherence.

Something isn't working.

If I save diligently for years but my financial plan keeps moving me farther from my actual goals, I need to reconsider the plan.

If I write every morning but nobody can understand what I've written, the consistency of my writing habit does not settle the question of whether the writing is good.

The destination matters.

But so does the distinction between destination and steering.

Maybe that's the better way to think about it.

When I'm driving somewhere, I care very much about the destination.

I look at the map.

I watch whether I'm getting closer.

If I discover that I've been driving east for two hours and my destination is west, that information deserves my attention.

But I cannot drive by staring at the destination.

I have to steer the car.

Our personal measurement systems often ask us to stare at the destination.

Weight.

Net worth.

Sales.

Rank.

Score.

Performance.

Outcome.

Then we wonder why we feel powerless when the number doesn't move on command.

The better question is closer.

Not:

**Can I control the outcome?**

Usually I can't.

But:

**What can I do that makes the outcome more likely?**

That is where agency begins.

**Outcomes are things we want. Actions are things we do.**

If I'm going to hold myself accountable, I should know which one I'm looking at.

## Outcomes Still Matter

There is a very convenient conclusion I could draw from the last section.

If outcomes are outside my direct control, perhaps I should stop worrying about them.

Problem solved.

Throw away the scale.

Stop checking the investment account.

Don't worry about whether anyone buys the book.

Just focus on the process.

Trust the system.

This is appealing.

It is also nonsense.

Outcomes matter.

A lot.

If I am trying to lose weight, my weight matters.

Not every day.

Not every fluctuation.

Not as a judgment of whether I was a good person yesterday.

But over time, if the goal is weight loss, whether I am actually losing weight seems like relevant information.

The same is true everywhere else.

If I am saving for retirement, the value of my investments matters.

If I own a business, whether the business makes money matters.

If I write books, whether anyone reads them tells me something.

If I exercise because I want to become stronger, whether I become stronger matters.

If a company changes a process to reduce defects, the defect rate matters.

Process without outcomes can become its own form of self-deception.

I followed the plan.

Great.

Did the plan work?

Those are different questions.

And we need both.

**Am I doing what I intended to do?**

**Is what I'm doing actually working?**

The first question is about behavior.

The second is about reality.

This is where outcome metrics earn their place.

They close the feedback loop.

Suppose I decide to lose weight by eating a particular way.

I follow the plan carefully.

Day after day.

Week after week.

My process metrics look fantastic.

I hit my calorie target.

I exercise.

I walk.

I prepare meals.

I sleep reasonably well.

If I stop there, I can give myself an excellent performance review.

But suppose six months later I weigh exactly the same.

That matters.

It doesn't mean the previous six months were a moral failure.

It doesn't mean I lack discipline.

It doesn't even necessarily mean every part of the plan was wrong.

It means the outcome is giving me information.

**Something about this approach is not producing the result I expected.**

Now I need to learn.

Maybe my assumptions were wrong.

Maybe my measurements were inaccurate.

Maybe the target needs adjustment.

Maybe something else is happening that I haven't considered.

Whatever the explanation, reality gets a vote.

This is one of the reasons measurement became so powerful in the first place.

It allows our theories to fail.

Without feedback, I can continue believing in a system indefinitely.

I can say:

**This should be working.**

The outcome can respond:

**That's interesting. It isn't.**

That conversation is valuable.

It is the same conversation a good organization should have with its metrics.

Imagine a software team adopts a new development process.

Everyone follows it perfectly.

All the required meetings happen.

Every template is completed.

Every checklist is checked.

Adoption reaches 100 percent.

Management is delighted.

Then software quality gets worse and delivery gets slower.

At some point, **but everyone followed the process** stops being a defense of the process.

The process exists for the outcome.

Not the other way around.

This is particularly important because process metrics can feel morally superior.

They're closer to effort.

They're closer to our choices.

So they can feel fairer.

I went to the gym.

I hit my calorie target.

I wrote for an hour.

I saved the money.

I did the thing.

That deserves recognition.

But doing the thing doesn't guarantee that it was the right thing.

I can be extraordinarily disciplined in the wrong direction.

There is probably no shortage of people who have followed ineffective plans with admirable consistency.

Discipline is a multiplier.

It does not automatically choose what gets multiplied.

That's why outcomes remain essential.

They help us test the relationship between our actions and our goals.

The key is to give outcomes the right job.

An outcome is excellent at answering:

**Where are we?**

It is less reliable at answering:

**Why are we here?**

And it is often terrible at answering:

**Whose fault is it?**

My weight trend can tell me whether my weight is generally moving up or down.

It cannot, by itself, tell me why.

My investment balance can tell me whether my portfolio grew.

It cannot tell me whether I made wise decisions or happened to own assets during a strong market.

A sales number can tell a company how much it sold.

It cannot, by itself, distinguish brilliant execution from favorable conditions.

The outcome is evidence.

It is not the entire explanation.

This gives us a healthier division of labor.

Actions help me evaluate my behavior.

Outcomes help me evaluate my assumptions.

That's not absolute, but it's a useful starting point.

If the scale jumps tomorrow morning after I followed my plan, I don't need to rewrite the plan.

If the scale trends upward for six months while I follow my plan, I probably do.

Time matters.

Patterns matter.

One data point may be noise.

A persistent outcome deserves curiosity.

This lets us avoid both extremes.

On one side:

**The outcome is everything.**

Number down, success.

Number up, failure.

We already know where that leads.

On the other:

**The outcome doesn't matter. Just trust the process.**

That can become equally absurd.

If the process never produces the thing it exists to produce, perhaps it would appreciate a performance review of its own.

The relationship should be a loop.

I act.

Reality responds.

I observe.

I learn.

I adjust.

Then I act again.

Measurement belongs inside that loop.

Not above it.

The outcome doesn't get to declare my worth.

But it does get to challenge my strategy.

That is a much more useful kind of authority.

So yes, measure the destination.

Know where you are.

Watch the trend.

Ask whether your choices seem to be taking you where you intended to go.

Just don't confuse watching the destination with steering the car.

For steering, we need to move the measurement closer.

Closer to the choices.

Closer to the behavior.

Closer to the place where something is actually mine to do.

Closer to agency.
