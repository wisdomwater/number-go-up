# 7 - Measure Where You Have Agency

### You Cannot Schedule an Outcome

I would like to lose a pound this week, which seems like the sort of goal that ought to be manageable with a little planning. So let's put it on the calendar.

Thursday, 2:00–2:30 p.m.: Lose one pound.

The problem becomes apparent when Thursday afternoon arrives and I have no idea what activity I'm supposed to perform. I know how to go for a walk, exercise, prepare a meal, or decide what I'm going to eat. I can do any of those things between two and two-thirty. What I cannot do is sit down and perform the activity *lose one pound*.

Weight loss is an outcome. It is something I want to happen and something my choices can influence enormously, but it isn't something I can directly do. This distinction seems painfully obvious when stated that way, yet we blur it constantly because so many of the goals that matter to us are expressed as outcomes. We want to lose weight, get promoted, grow our investments, sell books, build a successful business, sleep better, or have strong relationships. These are perfectly reasonable things to want, but none of them is completely ours to command.

Investing makes the distinction particularly clear. Suppose I decide that my portfolio should grow by eight percent this year. That may be a reasonable hope, perhaps even one informed by historical returns, but it would make a peculiar personal performance target. I can decide how much I save, choose an investment strategy, keep fees under control, and resist the urge to panic-sell because someone on television has used the word *crash* in a sufficiently dramatic tone. What I cannot do is instruct the market to provide my eight percent.

If the portfolio rises twenty percent during a booming market, I haven't necessarily become Warren Buffett. If it falls during a broad decline, I haven't necessarily failed. My decisions are part of the result, sometimes a very important part, but the result also contains everything that happened outside those decisions.

The same is true of most outcomes. My effort and judgment are in there, but so are circumstances, timing, other people, biology, randomness, opportunity, and things I couldn't possibly have known when I made the decision. As we move farther away from a specific action toward a larger outcome, more of the surrounding world gets a vote.

My scale provides a small demonstration of this every morning. It gives me one number containing some information about my recent behavior along with water, food, digestion, exercise, timing, and whatever mysterious biological process causes the scale to wait until the morning after I've done everything right to increase by 1.7 pounds. The scale doesn't separate any of those variables for me. It simply reports the outcome and leaves me to decide what the outcome means.

This is why outcomes can make poor daily performance reviews. Suppose I want to lose two pounds this week and Friday arrives with the scale showing that I've lost only half a pound. I could have ignored the plan, eaten considerably more than I intended, barely moved, and spent most of the week making choices that were unlikely to produce the result I wanted. In that case, the disappointing outcome may be telling me something useful about my behavior.

Or I could have followed the plan remarkably well. I might have eaten thoughtfully, exercised, walked, and generally done what I intended to do, while my weight simply didn't move as quickly as I hoped. The result on Friday could be exactly the same even though my contribution to that result was very different.

The problem is not that outcomes tell us nothing. It is that they combine what we did with what happened next, and those are not quite the same thing. A precise measurement of the outcome can tempt us into believing we had equally precise control over producing it.

We don't.

There is an important difference between influence and command. I influence my health, career, finances, relationships, and whether a book succeeds, and in some of those areas my influence may be enormous. But accountability becomes much more useful when I attach it to the part of the system where I can actually do something rather than treating myself as though I were responsible for every variable that eventually contributes to the result.

That still leaves us with an important complication, because outcomes matter.

Suppose I follow a weight-loss plan faithfully for six months. I eat the way I intended, exercise regularly, prepare my meals, and generally do all the things I believed would move me toward the goal. Six months later, I weigh exactly the same.

I don't think the right response is to congratulate myself on six months of flawless process adherence and carry on indefinitely. The unchanged weight doesn't mean the previous six months were a moral failure, and it doesn't necessarily mean I lacked discipline. It does mean that reality has supplied some information my theory needs to account for.

Maybe my assumptions were wrong. Maybe my measurements were inaccurate. Maybe something about the plan needs to change, or perhaps there is another factor I haven't considered. Whatever the explanation turns out to be, the approach isn't producing the outcome I expected, and that matters.

This is one of the reasons measurement became so powerful in the first place. It gives our theories a chance to fail. Without feedback, I can continue believing almost indefinitely that something *should* be working, while the result quietly suggests otherwise.

The same problem appears at work. Imagine a software organization adopts a new development process. Everyone follows it perfectly: all the required meetings happen, every template is completed, every checklist is checked, and adoption reaches one hundred percent.

Then software quality gets worse and delivery gets slower.

At some point, “everyone followed the process” stops being a defense of the process. The process was created because we believed it would produce some desirable effect, and the outcome helps us discover whether that belief was justified. We can be extraordinarily disciplined about doing something that turns out not to work.

This is why I don't want to choose between process and outcome. I need to know whether I'm doing what I intended to do, and I also need to know whether what I'm doing is producing the result I expected. The first question tells me something about my behavior; the second lets reality challenge my assumptions.

Time matters enormously here. If the scale jumps tomorrow morning after a day when I followed my plan, I probably don't need to redesign the plan before breakfast. If my weight trends upward for six months while I'm following the same plan, I probably need to learn something.

This gives outcomes a more useful role. They don't have to function as daily verdicts, but neither do they disappear into a comforting philosophy of trusting the process regardless of what happens. I act, reality responds, and over time I use that response to decide whether the action still makes sense.

I still care about the destination.

I simply cannot steer by staring at it.

### Measure Where You Have Agency

If outcomes matter but are too noisy to serve as daily performance reviews, then the obvious question is where to put my attention instead. For me, the answer is to move the measurement closer to the place where I actually make choices.

Take weight loss again. I cannot directly control what the scale says tomorrow morning, but I have considerably more influence over what I eat today. I can choose my meals, decide whether to exercise or go for a walk, and decide whether to keep eating after I'm full because three fries remain on the plate and apparently leaving them there feels like an unresolved moral issue.

Even these choices aren't under perfect control in the mechanical sense. Hunger affects me, as do stress, sleep, habits, environment, the food available around me, and the unfortunate continued existence of pizza. Human behavior is part of a system too, and pretending that every choice occurs in a vacuum is not especially useful.

Still, today's choices are much closer to me than tomorrow morning's weight. If I want accountability to help rather than merely judge me, it makes sense to place more of it there.

**Measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency.**

For weight loss, that means I can ask whether I generally ate the way I intended, moved my body, exercised, and made choices consistent with what I'm trying to accomplish. The scale remains part of the picture, but it has a different job. Over time, it tells me whether those behaviors seem to be producing the result I want instead of being asked each morning to decide whether yesterday was a success.

The distinction travels well beyond health. I would very much like this book to sell a lot of copies, for example. Ten thousand sounds nice. I would be happy to discover that ten thousand people had purchased a book containing several pages devoted to why I should not become overly attached to the number ten thousand.

Unfortunately, *sell ten thousand copies* is another activity I cannot put on Tuesday's calendar.

I can write. I can revise what I've written, publish it, tell people the book exists, listen to readers, improve how I describe it, and learn from what seems to resonate. All of those actions may increase the likelihood of sales.

Then thousands of other human beings get a vote.

The sales number still matters because it tells me something about whether the book is reaching people. If almost nobody buys it, I may eventually want to reconsider how I'm presenting it, whether I'm reaching the right audience, or even whether I've written something people want to read. But if I make sales my primary measure of whether I had a productive day, I've handed my daily sense of accomplishment to a large group of strangers who don't know they're participating.

So I can keep the outcome in view while bringing daily accountability closer to the work itself. Did I write? Did I make the manuscript better? Did I do something useful to help the book reach its audience? Then, periodically, I can look up and ask whether all that work appears to be producing the outcome I hoped for.

Career goals work the same way. Suppose I want a promotion. I can work hard, develop my skills, take responsibility, communicate well, help other people succeed, ask for feedback, and make my ambitions known. Those things may make a promotion much more likely.

I cannot walk into the promotion system and press the *Promote Me* button.

Other people are involved, as are budgets, timing, organizational needs, politics, and the possibility that someone else may simply be better qualified. If I make the promotion itself my measure of personal performance, I am taking responsibility for a decision I do not ultimately make.

That doesn't mean becoming passive about my career. Quite the opposite: identifying what is actually mine to do gives me somewhere concrete to act. I can prepare, improve, communicate, and take opportunities seriously without pretending I control the entire system around me.

Relationships make the distinction even clearer because the other person is not merely an environmental variable. If I want greater closeness with someone I love, there is no dashboard control labeled *Increase Relationship Quality 12 Percent*. I can listen, be present, make time, express affection, apologize when I'm wrong, tell the truth, and occasionally put down my phone.

Those actions matter enormously, but closeness still emerges between people. I participate in creating it without being able to manufacture it by myself.

This is one of the deeper reasons I find agency a useful concept. When I measure myself primarily by outcomes, I can accidentally assume responsibility for the whole system even though I am almost never the whole system. My body is a system, a market is a system, a workplace is a system, and a relationship contains at least one other person, which immediately introduces a troublesome independent variable.

I participate in these systems, and my choices matter. Sometimes they matter enormously. But participating in a system is different from ruling it.

The distinction isn't perfectly binary. Even my own behavior is affected by fatigue, stress, habit, environment, and other conditions I can only partly influence. We could keep drawing increasingly precise circles around what is and isn't under my control until someone eventually produces a spreadsheet containing my Agency Percentage, which I would prefer not to see.

A more practical question is simply: *What part of this is mine?*

Sometimes the answer is uncomfortable because quite a lot of it is mine. Perhaps I didn't prepare, avoided the difficult conversation, or kept making the same choice while telling myself the disappointing outcome was mysterious. Thinking in terms of agency isn't supposed to make every result feel better. It is supposed to help identify where action is actually possible.

At other times, the answer may be that very little remains for me to do. Before an important job interview, I can prepare carefully, learn about the role, think about my answers, and show up ready. Afterward, someone else makes the decision. I can learn from the experience and perhaps follow up, but eventually I reach a point where continued attempts to control the result are mostly attempts to control something that is no longer mine.

That point can be difficult for people who enjoy optimization. When a number looks bad, we want a lever; if that lever doesn't work, we want another one. Accepting that there may be nothing useful to do can feel suspiciously like giving up, even when the action available to us has simply run out.

But acceptance doesn't require passivity. I can take responsibility for my contribution and still acknowledge that the final result belongs to a larger system.

Knowing the difference can save an extraordinary amount of energy.

### Process Metrics Can Become Tyrants Too

Once we decide to move measurement closer to behavior, a new temptation appears. If process metrics are fairer because they sit closer to things I can influence, perhaps the solution is simply to measure more of them.

Instead of weighing myself, I can track calories. Since I'm tracking calories, I might as well track protein, carbohydrates, and fiber. Then steps, workouts, sleep, water, heart rate, and whatever mysterious new number my watch has recently decided is central to human flourishing.

Before long I can wake up each morning and discover that although my weight is fine, sleep is down, protein is low, steps are behind, recovery is questionable, and hydration has apparently become a matter requiring executive attention. There is a peculiar efficiency to a sufficiently comprehensive dashboard: somewhere in all that data, it can usually locate a reason for disappointment.

This is not much of an improvement if all we've done is replace one overpowered outcome metric with twenty-seven process metrics. The original problem was not simply that we chose the wrong number. It was that we allowed measurement to consume more authority and attention than it deserved.

Measuring closer to agency therefore doesn't mean measuring everything near an action. A better test is whether the information will help me make a decision.

If I track steps because noticing a low number in the afternoon reminds me to go for a walk, the measurement has a clear job. If I track my weight because I want to know whether the long-term trend is moving in the direction I intend, that has a job too.

My watch can generate considerably more information than I know what to do with. That doesn't mean I need to promote every available sensor reading onto my personal management team.

We already understand this principle in other systems. A modern car measures an astonishing number of things about itself, most of which remain hidden from the driver. That's appropriate because the driver is trying to get to the grocery store, not operate a mobile telemetry center. The dashboard surfaces the information likely to matter for the decisions being made, and more detailed data becomes available when there is a reason to investigate.

Our personal dashboards could benefit from similar restraint.

Information consumes attention, and attention is part of the system we're trying to manage. Every metric I monitor creates another thing to interpret and another opportunity to decide whether I'm ahead or behind. At some point, adding information can make it harder rather than easier to see what actually deserves attention.

So moving measurement closer to agency needs some restraint built into it. The goal isn't maximum visibility into myself. It's enough useful information to make better decisions without turning ordinary life into a control room.

### Rigor and Intuition

There are at least two convenient ways to avoid reality when trying to change something about ourselves. One is to ignore the numbers whenever they tell us something we don't particularly want to hear. The other is to hide behind the numbers so completely that we no longer have to exercise judgment.

Suppose I decide I don't need to track food because I know my body. I eat intuitively, pay attention to hunger, and can tell when I've eaten enough.

Maybe that's true.

There is only the minor complication that this is the same body whose current condition I'm trying to change. My intuition may be excellent in some areas and surprisingly generous in others, particularly around foods that come with peanut butter.

This is where measurement can be wonderfully clarifying. I can track my food for a while and discover that my intuitive tablespoon of peanut butter has roughly the dimensions of a tennis ball. The number can challenge the story I have been telling myself.

Without some form of external feedback, intuition can quietly become another word for whatever seemed reasonable at the time. I can believe I'm eating less, exercising more, spending carefully, or working effectively while reality accumulates evidence to the contrary. Measurement forces me to look at what actually happened rather than relying entirely on my recollection of what probably happened.

But once I have the numbers, I can make the opposite mistake. A spreadsheet tells me that 1,800 calories is the plan, so I continue eating 1,800 despite being constantly hungry and miserable. The training schedule says today is a workout day, so I exercise even though I'm exhausted. My sleep tracker reports poor sleep, and I begin to distrust the inconvenient fact that I woke up feeling refreshed.

The measurement knows something, but so do I.

A human being is not merely an unreliable sensor waiting to be replaced by a more accurate device. Hunger, fatigue, pain, mood, enjoyment, and the feeling that a plan is becoming impossible to sustain are also forms of information. They can be noisy and biased, which is why measurement is useful, but the measured data is incomplete too.

This is why I don't think rigor and intuition need to compete for authority. I want rigor because it can expose self-deception, and I want intuition because no dashboard contains enough information to tell me what living inside the plan is actually like.

If my weight hasn't moved for six months, the scale gets to say so. I don't get to dismiss the evidence merely because I feel that the plan ought to be working. But the scale also doesn't get to tell me why the plan failed or what I should do next.

Perhaps the calorie target is too high. Perhaps my tracking is inaccurate. Perhaps the plan is theoretically sound but so unpleasant that I cannot sustain it. Perhaps something else entirely is happening. Those possibilities require information that isn't contained in the weight trend itself.

The useful relationship is conversational. Measurement can challenge my experience, while experience supplies context the measurement cannot see. Judgment then has to work with both, which is admittedly less satisfying than letting the dashboard settle the matter.

Judgment carries the irritating possibility that I may be wrong. Sometimes I'll use context to excuse behavior I should change, and other times I'll allow a number to bully me into changing something that was working perfectly well. There is no measurement system sophisticated enough to remove that responsibility from me.

I think this is one reason we are so attracted to numbers in the first place. We would like reality to become easier to judge, and often measurement genuinely helps. But eventually the number reaches the edge of what it knows, and someone still has to decide what the information means.

### Did I Do the Things That Matter?

So tomorrow morning I can step onto the scale and look at the number without asking it to provide a complete evaluation of the previous day. Instead, I can look closer to my own contribution. Did I generally eat the way I intended? Did I move? Did I make choices consistent with the health I'm trying to build?

Those questions give me a fairer place to locate daily accountability because they are closer to what I actually did. But they cannot be the only questions I ask, or I risk congratulating myself indefinitely for faithfully following a plan that isn't working.

Every so often, I also need to look farther out and ask whether the things I'm doing are producing the result I hoped for.

Together, those perspectives create a feedback loop that is more useful than either one alone. My actions tell me something about my behavior, while the outcomes test the assumptions connecting those behaviors to my goals. I can take responsibility for the first without pretending to command the second, and I can learn from the second without turning every fluctuation into a verdict on the first.

That also gives me somewhere more useful to stand when the result disappoints me. I don't have to choose between condemning myself and ignoring the number. I can ask what happened, what part of it was mine, whether I'm seeing noise or a meaningful pattern, and whether anything actually needs to change.

Sometimes the answer will be uncomfortable because I do need to change something. Sometimes I'll discover that I've been telling myself a comforting story and the data isn't supporting it.

Other times, after all that analysis, the conclusion may be to do nothing.

Bodies take time. Investments take time. Careers and relationships take time, and writing a book takes considerably more time than the person writing it usually believes should be necessary. If I demand constant feedback from outcomes that move slowly, I will spend much of my life reacting to noise. A favorable number will tempt me to repeat whatever I happened to do yesterday, while an unfavorable one will tempt me to abandon a plan that may be working perfectly well.

There are moments when patience is part of the feedback loop. I keep doing what appears to be sensible long enough for reality to produce a meaningful pattern, rather than treating every new data point as a request for intervention.

That can feel oddly passive in a culture built around optimization. We like adjustments because they make us feel as though we're responding. There is satisfaction in finding a lever and pulling it, even when leaving the machinery alone for another week would have been wiser.

But the purpose of measurement isn't to maximize how often I intervene in my own life. It is to help me understand what is happening well enough to act wisely when action is useful and to refrain from acting when it isn't.

This is why I don't particularly want a perfect personal measurement system. I don't want to spend my life discovering increasingly sophisticated ways to evaluate whether I'm living correctly. I want the things the measurements were supposed to help me reach in the first place.

I want health, for example, rather than merely excellent health metrics. The distinction seems obvious until the scale is sitting on the bathroom floor every morning with a number precise to one decimal place, while health itself remains larger, slower, and much harder to display. Steps are easy to count; a life in which I can move freely, feel well, and participate in the things I care about is considerably harder to fit onto a screen.

The numbers can help me get there, which is why I intend to keep them. I just don't want the process of measuring my life to become the life I'm measuring.
