# Appendix

## The Map

### An Executive Summary of *Number Go Up*

After spending an entire book explaining that compression removes context, it seems only appropriate to conclude with an appendix that removes most of the context.

What follows is a map of the book. Like any map, it is useful because it leaves things out. The stories, jokes, qualifications, examples, and much of the reasoning have been compressed into a few paragraphs for each chapter. If we've learned anything by now, this should make you at least mildly suspicious.

But maps are useful. Use this one to remember an idea, prepare for a conversation, revisit a chapter, or recover the shape of the argument without rereading the entire book. Just remember that if anything here seems suspiciously clean, the territory is back there in the book.

## Introduction — Number Go Up

We live surrounded by numbers that tell us whether things are getting better. Revenue goes up. Productivity goes up. AI adoption goes up. Weight goes down. Attendance goes up. The line moves in the preferred direction, and we instinctively conclude: **This is good.** Sometimes it is. The trouble begins when we stop asking what the number was supposed to represent.

Metrics are proxies: small numerical windows through which we try to glimpse larger realities. The danger is not measurement itself but the authority we give it. A number can be accurate while our interpretation of it is wrong. It can measure something real without measuring everything that matters.

The central question of the book is therefore wonderfully unsophisticated: **What's the point?** The metric should always remain downstream from the answer.

## Chapter 1 — What Gets Measured Gets Managed

Measurement became powerful because it works. It gives reality a way to disagree with us. Science, engineering, medicine, economics, and modern organizations all depend on our ability to turn observations into information that can be compared, tested, and understood.

But measuring complex human systems requires proxies. Productivity, health, engagement, quality, success, and similar concepts cannot usually be placed directly on a scale. We choose something observable that represents part of the larger reality. In doing so, **we sacrifice completeness in exchange for visibility.**

A metric is therefore a map. Its usefulness depends partly on what it leaves out. The important question is not simply whether the number is accurate, but **good for what?** Precision in measurement is not precision in meaning. Look through the number at the reality it represents, and remember: **the number knows what the number knows. Nothing more.**

## Chapter 2 — When the Measure Becomes the Target

Human beings are different from boards, temperatures, and distances because we notice when we're being measured. Once people know which number matters, the measurement becomes part of the system it is attempting to observe. Attention becomes expectation, expectation becomes incentive, and incentive changes behavior.

This is the territory of Goodhart's Law: **When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.** People optimize what the system rewards. That does not necessarily mean they are dishonest. Often they are behaving rationally within the incentives we created. We made the game, published the rules, displayed the score, and then became annoyed when people started playing.

Whenever a metric becomes important, ask: **How could someone make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing we care about?** Once people begin optimizing a metric, the metric must be interpreted in light of that optimization. Measurement is not passive. When we introduce a metric into a human system, we change the system.

## Chapter 3 — The Dashboard Is Not the World

Organizations need abstraction. Leaders cannot personally inspect every bug, customer interaction, project, transaction, or decision. Dashboards compress enormous amounts of reality into forms that make patterns visible. That compression is not a defect. It is the reason dashboards work.

But the view from thirty thousand feet is different from the view on the ground. Managers can become too distant from the work, trusting clean abstractions over messy reality. They can also respond by descending into every detail and micromanaging. Neither extreme works particularly well. **The ground reveals detail. Altitude reveals pattern.**

A healthy dashboard should create questions rather than end conversations. Ask: **What had to disappear for this number to become this clean?** Use the dashboard to know where to look, then go look. The metric should begin the investigation, not conclude it.

## Chapter 4 — You Get What You Measure

Metrics are often introduced precisely because we want behavior to change. We find something correlated with a desirable outcome, measure it, set a target, and encourage people to improve it. This can work remarkably well.

The problem appears when our ability to optimize the proxy becomes greater than the proxy's ability to represent the thing we actually care about. Teams can improve local metrics while making the larger system worse. Speed can improve while quality declines. Utilization can rise while flexibility disappears. Bugs can close faster while difficult problems are avoided.

Instead of immediately blaming people for responding to an incentive, ask: **What behavior have we made rational?** Sometimes healthy systems produce ugly numbers because something more important required the metric to lose. Companies do not ship dashboards. They ship products. **The number gets a seat at the table. It just doesn't get the chair at the head. Reality does.**

## Chapter 5 — Every Metric Is a Message

A metric does more than collect information. Over time, it communicates what matters. What leaders repeatedly ask about receives attention. What receives targets receives even more. What receives rewards and consequences eventually becomes part of the culture.

This creates cultural gravity around things that are easy to measure. Organizations may say they value mentoring, experimentation, trust, long-term thinking, or thoughtful risk-taking while their measurement systems consistently reward output, predictability, and short-term results. The calculation itself may be impartial, but the choice of what to calculate is not.

**The measurer sees information. The measuree experiences an incentive. The culture absorbs a value.** Measurement systems eventually become teaching systems. When choosing a metric, don't ask only what it will tell you. Ask: **What are we teaching people?**

## Chapter 6 — The Quantified Self

Eventually, we bring the dashboard home. We measure our weight, sleep, steps, heart rate, calories, workouts, screen time, productivity, finances, reading, habits, and countless other parts of ourselves. Much of this is genuinely useful. Measurement can reveal patterns, correct faulty memories, and challenge the stories we tell ourselves.

But something strange happens when the measurer and the measuree are the same person. A number that began as information can become an internal performance review. We become both manager and employee, and neither of us is particularly pleasant to work with.

The danger is not self-measurement but self-rule by measurement. Ask **What happened?** before asking **What's wrong with me?** Personal metrics should help us investigate our lives, not prosecute ourselves. **My life is the reality. The number is information about it. Keep the data. Lose the boss.**

## Chapter 7 — Measure Where You Have Agency

Outcomes matter, but outcomes are not actions. I can control what I eat today more directly than what the scale says tomorrow. I can prepare carefully for an interview but cannot control whether I get the job. I can write a book but cannot make someone buy it.

The farther we move from action toward outcome, the more the rest of reality gets a vote. That leads to a practical principle: **Measure yourself closest to the point where you actually have agency.** Actions help evaluate our behavior. Outcomes help evaluate our assumptions. We are responsible for our contribution without pretending we have sovereignty over the result.

Process metrics can become tyrants too, so the answer is not to replace one dashboard with twenty-seven better ones. Measure what helps you make decisions. **Rigor without intuition becomes tyranny. Intuition without rigor can become self-deception.** The useful daily question is often simply: **Did I do the things that matter?**

## Chapter 8 — When the Number Becomes Me

A measurement can slowly change grammar. **I received a poor performance rating** becomes **I am a poor performer.** **I weigh this much** becomes **I am fat.** **My book didn't sell well** becomes **I'm not a good writer.** A description becomes an identity.

This becomes especially powerful when numbers have real consequences. Performance ratings can affect jobs, income, opportunities, and security. Organizations need compression to make decisions at scale, but employees experience those decisions at full resolution. People therefore do not merely optimize metrics. They optimize for safety.

A measurement can be true without being the truth about you. **The number can be right without becoming a verdict.** Hold it with open hands. Ask what it legitimately says, what context is missing, what belongs to you, and what the number is not qualified to say. No metric we have invented is qualified to answer the question **Am I enough?**

## Chapter 9 — Nickels and Noses

Churches need numbers too. Attendance matters when deciding how many chairs are needed. Offerings matter when paying salaries and electric bills. Growth can reveal meaningful things about whether a community is reaching people.

But spiritual life exposes the limits of measurement particularly quickly. Attendance can tell us how many people entered a room. It cannot tell us what happened inside them. Offerings can measure money given but not generosity growing. We can count programs without knowing whether people are becoming more loving, courageous, forgiving, wise, or whole.

The things that matter most do not owe us a metric. **Size tells us about size.** Growth does not always mean becoming bigger. Sometimes the most important growth happens quietly, invisibly, and beyond our ability to put it on a dashboard.

## Chapter 10 — The Things We Cannot Measure

Measurement can tell us true things about almost anything. We can study the physiology of love, the acoustics of music, the psychology of grief, the economics of art, and the neurological correlates of experience. The mistake is not measuring these things. It is assuming the measurement contains the whole reality.

There is a view from outside and a view from inside. We can measure what happened and still ask what it was like. We can describe an event and still ask what it meant. Love, beauty, grief, experience, and meaning are not made less real because they resist complete numerical representation. **The limitations of an instrument do not establish the limitations of reality.**

Different questions require different ways of knowing. Numbers can correct stories, and stories can restore context that numbers removed. Wisdom requires evidence, experience, relationship, reason, intuition, and judgment to remain in conversation. **Reality is always richer than its representation.**

## Chapter 11 — Useful Servants

The answer to bad measurement is not no measurement. Organizations still need budgets, schedules, goals, quality measures, financial reports, performance information, and dashboards. Individuals still benefit from health data, financial information, and feedback. The goal is not to fire the numbers. **It is to clarify the reporting structure.**

Start with the point. Know what the number actually knows. Ask what behavior you are making rational. Keep conversation attached to measurement. Let the metric lose when reality requires it. Watch what the measurement system teaches people. Periodically ask whether the metric still deserves its job. **The purpose stays. The proxy is negotiable.**

Above all, use metrics as talking points rather than verdicts. **A good metric should deepen the conversation, not end it.** Use numbers to know where to look. Use relationships to understand what you find. The purpose of measurement is not to eliminate judgment. It is to give judgment better information.

## Conclusion — Enough

Eventually, *Number Go Up* becomes a number too. Copies sold, pages read, reviews, rankings, revenue, and subscribers will tell me something real about how this book performs. I want those numbers to go up. The argument of this book was never that numbers do not matter.

But those numbers cannot tell me whether the book mattered. They cannot tell me whether a manager listened differently, whether someone stopped confusing a performance rating with an identity, whether a team reconsidered a destructive incentive, or whether someone looked at a number and remembered to ask **What's the point?**

Our culture is very good at **more**. More growth. More productivity. More achievement. More followers. More optimization. There is always another number and almost always somewhere for it to go. But human beings need another category: **enough**. Enough is not surrender. It is the recognition that growth serves a purpose and that not everything meaningful must continually prove its value by becoming larger.

# A Few Questions Worth Keeping

If you remember nothing else from this map, a handful of questions can take you surprisingly far:

**What's the point?**

**What does this number actually know?**

**What did we have to leave out in order to make it this clean?**

**How could someone make this number look fantastic without improving the thing we actually care about?**

**What behavior are we making rational?**

**What happened here?**

**What context are we missing?**

**What does the person closest to the reality see that I don't?**

**Can the metric lose?**

**What are we teaching people?**

**Do we still need this number?**

**Did I do the things that matter?**

And when the metric and reality seem to disagree:

**What does the number see that I don't?**

**What do I see that the number doesn't?**

None of these questions eliminates the need for judgment. That's the point.

The simplest map of the entire book may be three words:

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment**

Meaning tells us what matters.

Measurement helps us understand it.

Judgment helps us decide what to do.

Keep them in that order.

And remember what this appendix has necessarily demonstrated by existing at all:

**The map is useful.**

**The map is incomplete.**

**The territory is always larger.**
