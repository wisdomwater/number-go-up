# 3 - When the Measure Becomes the Target

> *A measure can help us see progress. A target can quietly become a substitute for it.*

## Goodhart’s Law

There is a familiar idea in economics and management usually summarized as Goodhart’s Law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The wording is tidy enough to fit on a slide, which means it has probably suffered a little from the same problem it warns us about. Still, the idea is useful because it names something that happens almost everywhere people are asked to improve a number.

A measure usually begins as a proxy for something harder to see directly. We count commits because we care about software progress, bugs closed because we care about quality, schedule performance because we care about execution, and token usage because we care about whether people are actually trying new tools. None of these measures is absurd on its face. They are attempts to compress something messy into a signal we can compare and discuss.

The trouble begins when the proxy acquires consequences of its own. Once people know that a number affects praise, scrutiny, promotion, reputation, or leadership attention, the number becomes something worth improving in itself. The original question—*What outcome are we trying to create?*—can slowly give way to another one: *What makes this number look better?*

Sometimes those two questions still point in the same direction. Sometimes they drift apart so gradually that nobody notices the separation until the dashboard looks excellent and the underlying system feels strangely worse. That is what makes Goodhart’s Law more interesting than simple cheating. Most of the time, no one is falsifying data or scheming in a conference room; people are simply getting better at succeeding according to the rules they have been given.

The map has not disappeared. It has become important enough that people begin arranging the territory to fit it.

## Visible Activity

Software engineering has always had an awkward relationship with productivity. A factory can count widgets coming off a line, but software does not cooperate quite so neatly. One engineer can spend a day writing hundreds of lines of code while another spends the same day deleting fifty and fixing a problem that has bothered the team for six months. Naturally, being engineers, we have responded to this ambiguity by inventing more numbers.

It is not unheard of to look at things such as commits, bugs resolved, features completed, or other visible artifacts as signals of productivity. There is some logic to this because work has to leave evidence somewhere, and an engineer who consistently ships code and fixes problems probably is contributing something useful. The difficulty begins when the evidence becomes easier to evaluate than the work itself.

I once worked with an engineer in system validation who wrote code much faster than most of the rest of us. He delivered ahead of schedule often enough that he regularly received service awards and recognition for getting things done quickly. From the perspective of visible output, he looked terrific: code appeared quickly, milestones moved, and completed work accumulated at a satisfying pace.

The problem was that some of that speed came from cutting corners. His components accumulated defects faster than the rest of ours, often because testing had been rushed or the implementation had not been thought through as carefully as it might have been. Those bugs usually came back to him, and because he was also fast at fixing them, he generated yet another impressive stream of visible activity.

That created a strange measurement problem. A high commit rate suggested productivity, and a high bug-fix rate suggested someone who was responsive and effective at resolving problems. Both numbers could be true at the same time, while still leaving out the relationship that mattered most: some portion of the second metric existed because of the first.

Meanwhile, another engineer might take longer, test more carefully, and quietly prevent several of those bugs from ever existing. There is no ticket for the defect that never escaped. Nobody gets an automated notification celebrating the issue that failed to happen because somebody slowed down three weeks earlier and thought the problem through.

## What Leaves a Trace

Metrics naturally favor work that leaves evidence behind. A commit exists. A closed bug exists. A thousand new lines of code can be counted with almost insulting ease. The engineer who spends three days discovering that the correct solution requires only twenty carefully chosen lines may have done the harder work, but the dashboard has no particular reason to know that.

Software makes this especially strange because more is often not better. Complicated code can be easier to write than simple code; you can keep adding conditions, abstractions, and special cases until the thing finally behaves. Simplifying it takes a different kind of effort because you have to understand the problem well enough to remove what is unnecessary without breaking what remains. An engineer can spend a week making a system smaller, clearer, and easier to maintain and appear, according to a naïve lines-of-code metric, to have spent the week going backward.

The same blindness applies to work that never reaches a repository. A coworker can wander over with a problem that has blocked them for half a day, and ten minutes of conversation may save another two days of investigation. Perhaps you remember seeing the same failure years ago, know which person owns the mysterious subsystem, or simply ask the question that exposes the bad assumption. The team has just gained a meaningful amount of productivity, but there may be no commit, ticket, or status field with your name attached to it.

Good engineering contains a lot of these negative spaces. Bugs avoided, complexity removed, dead ends abandoned early, coworkers unblocked, designs made understandable enough that the next person does not need a week to decipher them. When these things work, what they often produce is less visible activity rather than more.

This is where a metric can remain perfectly accurate while telling a misleading story. The commit count may be right, the bug count may be right, and the lines of code may be correct down to the last semicolon. What is missing is the judgment needed to understand what those events meant and how they related to everything that did not happen.

The temptation, of course, is to solve this by measuring more things. Add a quality score, collaboration score, complexity score, mentoring score, perhaps a helpfulness dashboard while we are at it. At some point, though, we run into the uncomfortable possibility that certain forms of value are hard to measure precisely because they depend on context and judgment. Sometimes the dashboard cannot see the work because the work was making everyone else’s dashboard look a little better.

## When the Proxy Becomes the Point

AI is the hot topic in software right now, and like most hot topics it arrived carrying both genuine possibility and a certain amount of organizational fever. Some engineers adopted it almost immediately because they could feel the value in their own work: faster exploration, better first drafts, easier automation, quicker answers to questions that used to require a long search through documentation. Others were more skeptical, either because their existing workflows worked well or because they had lived through enough technology cycles to know that enthusiasm and usefulness are not identical twins.

Naturally, we started measuring adoption. One of the easiest signals available was LLM token usage. At first, that made sense. If the goal was simply to know whether people were trying the tools, then token consumption provided a rough indication that something was happening.

The trouble begins when the visible signal acquires authority beyond the question it was designed to answer. I had a manager tell me that a script I had written, one that already worked well on its own, needed to be converted into an agent skill. The technical question of whether that was the best design was secondary. What mattered was demonstrating that we were embracing the AI vision.

That is the moment when the proxy starts eating the purpose. The original question might have been, *Can AI help us do this work better?* Once adoption itself becomes the target, the question shifts toward, *Can we make this look more like AI?* Implementation cost, runtime efficiency, maintainability, and whether the old solution was already perfectly adequate can become awkward details standing between us and the story we want the metric to tell.

None of this means the push toward AI is foolish. Some of these tools are genuinely useful, and organizations would be unwise to ignore them simply because the hype is occasionally exhausting. The absurdity appears when the signal of adoption becomes more important than the judgment the adoption was supposed to improve.

At that point, the engineering problem becomes wonderfully simple.

AI number go up.

## The Seduction of the Hard Number

There is something comforting about hard numbers, especially in organizations. They appear objective. A commit count is a commit count, a bug count is a bug count, and a token is a token. Compared with the messiness of human judgment, the number can feel almost meritocratic: everyone is being evaluated against the same thing.

That clarity is part of the appeal. People generally want to know what is expected of them, and once an expectation becomes measurable they can organize themselves around it. In that sense, the old saying is mostly right: people tend to do what you pay them for, what you praise them for, and what you repeatedly ask them to explain.

But a number never removes judgment. Judgment was already present when someone decided what to count, how to count it, what threshold mattered, and what conclusion should follow from the result. The spreadsheet may hide those choices behind clean columns and percentages, but they have not vanished; they have simply moved upstream.

That matters because human systems are adaptive. Give people a target and they will become increasingly skilled at reaching it, sometimes in ways the designer never anticipated. The more confidence we place in the number itself, the easier it becomes to confuse success at the metric with success at the underlying purpose.

The answer is not to abandon measurement. Numbers can correct intuition, expose wishful thinking, and force us to confront results we would otherwise explain away. But they still need context, interpretation, and occasionally someone willing to say, “Yes, the number went up. I’m just not convinced that was the thing we meant to improve.”

Good judgment lives in that pause.

## What the Number Cannot Decide

A few principles are worth keeping.

Measures are useful because they make something visible, and targets are useful because they give effort direction. But once a measure becomes a target, people will learn how to improve the number, whether or not the underlying reality improves with it.

Hard numbers can feel objective, but they never remove judgment. Someone chose what to count, what to ignore, and what the result was supposed to mean. Interpretation remains part of the system whether we acknowledge it or not.

The metric is evidence, not a verdict.

And when the map starts looking cleaner than the territory, it may be time to leave the dashboard and go see what is actually happening.
