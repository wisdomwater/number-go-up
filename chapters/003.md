# 3: The Dashboard Is Not the World

### You Cannot Manage What You Can't See

Imagine the CEO of a company with fifty thousand employees decides dashboards are too abstract.

She has read the first two chapters of this book and taken them very seriously. Metrics are incomplete. Context matters. The people closest to the work understand things the numbers cannot see.

Fine.

No more dashboards.

From now on, she will understand the company by talking directly to the people doing the work.

She sits down with an engineer.

"How are things going?"

The engineer explains that a recent change exposed a memory-allocation problem in a subsystem that was designed several years ago. The problem only appears under a particular workload, and the team has narrowed it down to an interaction between two components that were never expected to operate this way.

Forty-five minutes later, the CEO understands the problem much better.

Only 49,999 employees to go.

There is a reason organizations create dashboards.

At some point, direct experience stops scaling.

A manager with six employees may understand a great deal about each person's work. A director responsible for sixty people cannot know the same level of detail. A vice president responsible for six thousand certainly cannot.

This isn't a failure of leadership.

It's arithmetic.

The larger the organization becomes, the more reality has to be compressed before anyone can make sense of it.

Individual events become categories. Categories become counts. Counts become trends. Trends become charts. Charts become dashboards.

Something is lost at every step.

Something is gained too.

Suppose you're an engineer on a team that is running late.

From where you sit, the explanation may be obvious. A dependency arrived late. A technical problem turned out to be harder than expected. A requirement changed.

You understand your Tuesday.

Your manager, however, may be looking at ten teams and notice that seven of them are running late.

That's a different kind of information.

One delayed team may require understanding the team.

Seven delayed teams may require understanding the system.

Maybe planning assumptions are unrealistic. Maybe several teams depend on the same overloaded organization. Maybe priorities keep changing. Maybe a shared process is creating delays.

The engineer is trying to solve Tuesday.

The manager is trying to understand why everyone seems to be having the same Tuesday.

Both perspectives matter.

The person close to the work sees details that disappear at scale. The person farther away can sometimes see patterns that disappear in the details.

**The ground reveals detail. Altitude reveals pattern.**

The mistake is assuming that one view is inherently more real than the other.

A manager who only trusts the dashboard can become detached from the work.

An employee who only trusts personal experience can miss patterns larger than their own experience.

Organizations need both.

The question is how to move between them.

### Compression Is the Point

Suppose an executive asks:

"How's the project going?"

This is an unreasonable question if taken literally.

The project has been running for six months. Thirty-seven people have worked on it. There have been hundreds of meetings, thousands of emails and messages, architectural decisions, revised requirements, staffing changes, vendor problems, bugs, breakthroughs, misunderstandings, and at least one meeting that probably could have been an email.

How's the project going?

Well.

How much time do you have?

But that's usually not what the executive is asking.

The executive may be responsible for twenty projects. She needs to know which ones are healthy and which ones require attention.

So we compress six months of organizational reality into something she can use.

Green.

Yellow.

Red.

There is something objectively funny about this.

Thirty-seven human beings spend half a year navigating a complicated technical system, and eventually the whole experience becomes a yellow circle on PowerPoint.

But that yellow circle may be exactly what the executive needs.

If the question is:

*Which of my twenty projects needs attention?*

Yellow might be enough.

If the question is:

*Should we cancel this project?*

Yellow probably isn't enough.

If the question is:

*Is the project manager performing poorly?*

I certainly hope we have more information.

The problem isn't that the yellow dot is too simple.

The problem is using a low-resolution representation to make a high-resolution decision.

**Match the resolution of the information to the resolution of the decision.**

This sounds obvious, but organizations violate it constantly because information has a tendency to travel.

Imagine a project is delayed three weeks.

At the engineering level, the explanation might be fairly detailed. A customer changed an important requirement after implementation began. The original architecture no longer fits. The team has redesigned part of the system, understands the new approach, and believes the revised schedule is realistic.

The engineering manager summarizes this for the director.

The customer changed a requirement. The team had to redesign part of the system. Delivery will move three weeks.

The director updates the vice president.

Project delayed three weeks due to requirement change.

Eventually the executive dashboard says:

> Schedule: Yellow

Nobody lied.

Nobody even summarized irresponsibly.

The information was compressed because each level needed less detail.

Then, three months later, someone reviewing organizational performance notices that this team missed its original delivery date.

"Why can't this team deliver on time?"

Now we have a problem.

The yellow status may have been perfectly adequate for deciding where executive attention was needed. It is much less adequate for judging the team's execution.

Information created for one decision has been recruited for another.

The missing context suddenly matters.

This is one reason dashboards can become more powerful than their designers intended. Once information is clean, visible, and available, we are tempted to reuse it.

The number is already there.

The status is already there.

Why not use it?

Because sometimes the question has changed.

A weather map can tell me whether I should bring an umbrella. I probably shouldn't use it to decide where to build a bridge.

The map isn't bad.

I changed the job.

A useful discipline, then, is not merely asking whether a metric is accurate. We also need to ask whether it has enough resolution for the decision we're about to make.

A yellow dot can tell us where to look.

It should not always tell us what to think.

### When the Dashboard Becomes More Real Than the Work

There is another way abstraction can get us into trouble.

Sometimes the people closest to the work sense a problem before the dashboard can see it.

I've experienced this in software.

A project can look green.

Milestones are being completed. Major features work. Testing is mostly passing. The schedule looks reasonable.

And yet the engineers working inside the system begin to feel uncomfortable.

The architecture is becoming fragile.

Changes that should be simple are taking longer.

Fixes in one area are producing unexpected behavior somewhere else.

Nobody can point to a catastrophic failure because there isn't one.

Not yet.

"So what's the problem?"

Well.

It's complicated.

This is not an especially powerful position to occupy when there is a green dashboard behind you.

The dashboard says the project is healthy.

The engineers say something feels wrong.

Which one should we trust?

Again, that's probably the wrong question.

The dashboard is reporting the things it was designed to report. The engineers are noticing things that may not yet have crossed one of its thresholds.

Both can be true.

"The data says everything is fine" often means something slightly narrower:

Everything **we currently measure** is fine.

That may be enough.

Or it may not.

Expertise frequently includes the ability to notice weak signals before they become obvious failures. An experienced engineer may recognize that a codebase is becoming difficult to change before defect counts rise. A project manager may sense that a schedule is becoming unrealistic before a milestone is officially missed.

This kind of knowledge is uncomfortable because it is difficult to compress.

Sometimes it's wrong.

People have hunches. Experts have biases. Teams can become pessimistic. An engineer who dislikes an architectural decision may interpret every problem as evidence that the architecture is doomed.

That is exactly why we need data.

But the reverse is also true.

The absence of a red box does not prove the absence of a problem.

The most useful response to disagreement between the dashboard and the people closest to the work is usually curiosity.

What are they seeing?

What is the dashboard seeing?

What evidence would help us distinguish a weak signal from an unfounded concern?

This problem appears not only in dashboards but in the processes we use to describe work.

Software development processes often look wonderfully orderly from a distance.

Requirements.

Design.

Implementation.

Testing.

Deployment.

This is, in fact, a reasonable description of software development.

It just isn't always how Tuesday goes.

Tuesday may begin in implementation, where we discover that a requirement doesn't make sense. That sends us back to requirements, which exposes a design assumption nobody noticed. We revise the design, implement half of it, test something, discover an old dependency, find the one person who remembers why it was built that way five years ago, reconsider the design, implement again, test again, swear quietly, fix something, and eventually deploy.

Later, the project-management system reports:

> Implementation complete.

Technically, yes.

The simplified process isn't necessarily wrong. We need simplified processes because thirty-seven people cannot coordinate by exchanging personal narratives about their Tuesday.

The danger comes when the simplified description stops being a coordination tool and becomes a demand that reality conform to the description.

**A good process describes work well enough to coordinate it. A bad process requires people to pretend the work happened the way the process says it should have happened.**

That distinction matters because simplified descriptions often travel upward and then return as instructions.

A process is created to help management understand and coordinate work. Over time, the organization begins treating the process as the way work is supposed to happen.

Employees then learn to make the messy reality fit the clean representation.

That can create cynicism.

From the employee's perspective, leadership doesn't understand how the work actually happens.

But once again, proximity doesn't grant omniscience.

The process the engineer finds annoying may exist because another team depends on a predictable handoff. It may satisfy a regulatory requirement. It may prevent a failure that happened before the engineer joined the company.

The employee sees the cost of the process.

The manager may see the reason for it.

Neither view contains the whole system.

Which means the answer cannot simply be: management needs to get closer to the work.

There is such a thing as getting too close.

### The Right Altitude

Suppose a manager reads everything so far and decides to fix the problem.

Clearly the dashboard is too abstract.

Time to come down from the mountain.

The manager starts attending team meetings.

Good.

Then design reviews.

Still reasonable.

Then bug triage.

Okay.

Then the manager begins reviewing individual work items.

"Can you explain why this task is estimated at five days?"

"Have we considered doing it this way?"

"Can you write up the alternatives?"

"Can we have a meeting to discuss the document?"

"Would you mind adding a section to the document explaining what we discussed in the meeting about the document?"

At some point, the team begins longing for the dashboard.

Getting closer to the work is not automatically better.

At one extreme, management is so distant that employees think:

*You don't understand my work.*

At the other, management is so involved that employees think:

*You don't trust me to do my work.*

Both are failures of altitude.

A manager needs enough distance to see the system and enough proximity to understand what the system cannot show.

There is no single correct altitude.

There is only an altitude appropriate to the question.

Suppose twelve teams are missing deadlines.

Stay high for a moment.

Look for patterns.

Are priorities changing too often? Is there a shared dependency? Are planning assumptions unrealistic? Is the organization overloaded?

Now suppose eleven teams are doing fine and one is consistently struggling.

Come down.

What is happening on that team?

Maybe the project is unusually difficult. Maybe the team has a staffing problem. Maybe the manager needs help.

Or maybe the team simply isn't executing well.

Altitude helps determine which questions become visible.

Proximity helps answer them.

The skill is moving between the two.

This also means managers need to remember that their presence changes the environment when they descend.

A senior leader may enter a design review and say:

"I wonder if we should consider rebuilding this component."

To the senior leader, this may be brainstorming.

To the team, it may sound remarkably like:

> Rebuild this component.

Three weeks later, the leader wonders why everyone ran off and did something they never actually asked for.

Authority adds weight to curiosity.

This is the same measurer-measuree dynamic we saw in the previous chapter. The person with more authority may experience a conversation as exploration. The person with less authority may experience it as direction.

So changing altitude requires more than showing up.

It requires awareness of what your presence does when you arrive.

The goal is not to understand every implementation detail or participate in every decision.

It is to know enough about the territory that the map remains connected to it.

**Stay connected without taking over.**

That may mean talking with people doing the work.

It may mean asking a manager for the story behind an unusual number.

It may mean joining a review occasionally, visiting a customer, reading a bug report, or watching how a process actually works.

Then go back up.

That's important.

The purpose of descending isn't to relocate permanently.

A manager isn't supposed to live at thirty thousand feet. Nor is a manager supposed to permanently set up a folding chair beside the engineer's desk.

Good management requires movement.

Go high enough to see the pattern.

Come down far enough to understand what it means.

Then go back up and see whether what you learned changes the pattern.

### Dashboards Should Create Questions

We often treat dashboards as answer machines.

How are sales?

Green.

How's the project?

Yellow.

How's customer satisfaction?

Eighty-four.

How's engineering productivity?

Up 7.3 percent.

There.

Management achieved.

But the most useful dashboards may do something less impressive.

They tell us where to look.

Suppose customer complaints rise 18 percent.

We could immediately conclude that customer satisfaction is getting worse.

Or we could ask:

Why?

Maybe satisfaction really is getting worse.

Maybe a recent product release introduced a problem.

Maybe the company launched a new feedback system and customers can finally report issues that were previously invisible.

The number directs attention.

Reality provides context.

Then the context may change how we interpret the number.

The same should be true when the dashboard looks good.

Organizations are usually quite skilled at investigating bad numbers.

Revenue misses target.

What happened?

Customer complaints rise.

What happened?

A project turns red.

What happened?

Then:

"Revenue exceeded target by 3 percent."

Everybody stop.

Something has gone terribly right.

We should probably understand why.

Maybe a team did excellent work.

Maybe market conditions changed.

Maybe a one-time event created the increase.

Maybe we discovered something worth repeating.

Green deserves curiosity too.

Otherwise we risk making a subtle mistake: red numbers require explanation, while green numbers explain themselves.

They don't.

A dashboard is a compressed representation of a system. Whether the number is good or bad, the same question remains useful:

**Does this number help me know where to ask better questions?**

That is a more modest job for a dashboard than telling us what reality means.

It's also a more useful one.

A yellow project status doesn't need to contain six months of history. It needs to tell the executive:

*You may want to look here.*

Then someone comes down in altitude.

They talk with the project manager. They learn about the changed requirement. They discover that the team handled it well and that the three-week delay may actually be evidence of sound judgment.

Now something important has happened.

The dashboard sent us toward reality.

Reality changed our understanding of the dashboard.

Maybe we return to the executive view and notice that four other yellow projects have the same dependency. What looked like isolated project problems becomes a systemic pattern.

So we go back up.

**The dashboard sends us toward reality. Reality sends us back toward the dashboard with better questions.**

That's the relationship we're after.

Not numbers versus experience.

Not managers versus employees.

Not dashboards versus stories.

Movement.

The ground reveals detail.

Altitude reveals pattern.

Sometimes we need one. Sometimes we need the other. Most difficult organizational decisions require both.

The metric should begin the investigation, not conclude it.

Use the dashboard.

That's what it's there for.

Just keep one foot on the ground.
