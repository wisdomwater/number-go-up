# Chapter 3: The Dashboard Is Not the World

## You Cannot Manage What You Can’t See

Let's begin with a defense of dashboards.

They are incredibly useful.

This may seem like an odd place to start immediately after spending an entire chapter explaining all the strange things that can happen when human beings discover which numbers are being watched.

But organizations have a problem.

They're big.

Even relatively small organizations are too complicated for any one person to understand completely. Once you have hundreds, thousands, or tens of thousands of people doing different kinds of work, the idea that a leader could simply understand what's happening by paying closer attention becomes absurd.

Imagine the CEO of a company with fifty thousand employees deciding that dashboards are dangerous abstractions.

No more metrics.

No more reports.

From now on, she is going to understand the business by talking directly to the people doing the work.

This sounds wonderfully human.

On Monday morning she sits down with an engineer.

“What are you working on?”

The engineer explains.

Forty-five minutes later, the CEO has learned quite a bit about a problem involving memory allocation that she did not know existed.

Excellent.

Only 49,999 employees to go.

By lunch, the limitations of the strategy have become apparent.

The CEO doesn't need to understand every memory allocation problem.

She needs to understand whether the engineering organization can deliver the products the company has promised.

She doesn't need to listen to every customer service call. She needs to know whether customers are generally getting their problems resolved.

She doesn't need to inspect every invoice. She needs to know whether the company is financially healthy.

She doesn't need to sit in every hiring interview. She needs to know whether the organization can attract and retain the people it needs.

To do that, reality has to be compressed.

Thousands of individual events become categories.

Categories become counts.

Counts become trends.

Trends become charts.

Charts become dashboards.

By the time information reaches senior leadership, enormous amounts of detail have disappeared.

This isn't necessarily a failure.

It's the point.

Abstraction allows us to think about things that would otherwise overwhelm us.

We do this constantly.

Consider a map.

A road map does not contain every tree, mailbox, pothole, driveway, dog, pedestrian, storm drain, and suspiciously aggressive goose you might encounter on your journey.

Thank God.

If it did, the map would become almost as difficult to navigate as the world itself.

The map is useful precisely because it leaves things out.

A dashboard works the same way.

A manager overseeing twenty projects probably doesn't need to know every decision made on every project. She needs a representation that allows her to see which projects are progressing, which are stuck, which require additional resources, and which appear to be wandering quietly toward disaster.

So we create abstractions.

Green. Yellow. Red.

On track. At risk. Behind.

Revenue. Cost.

Defects.

Customer satisfaction.

Headcount.

Schedule.

Each one compresses a complicated reality into something a human mind can hold.

And something important happens when we do this.

We gain perspective.

The engineer working inside a particular component may understand that component better than anyone else in the company. She knows its history. She knows why the ugly piece of code everyone complains about is still there. She knows which dependency breaks every third Tuesday for reasons nobody has completely understood.

That knowledge is valuable.

But she may not know that five other teams are encountering similar problems.

A manager looking across those teams might.

The manager loses detail but gains pattern.

One team misses a deadline.

That may mean almost anything.

Six teams miss deadlines for the same dependency.

Now we may have discovered something.

One customer complains.

Maybe the customer had an unusual problem.

Two hundred customers complain about the same thing.

That's a pattern.

One engineer says a process is wasting time.

Perhaps she's right.

Perhaps she simply hates filling out forms.

When engineers across twelve teams are all saying the same thing, the abstraction begins revealing something the individual experience couldn't.

This is one of the great powers of measurement. 

t allows us to see the forest.

The cliché exists for a reason.

When you're standing next to a tree, you can see things about that tree that nobody looking down from a helicopter will ever see. You can inspect the bark. You can see where a branch is damaged. You can notice insects eating the leaves.

From the helicopter, most of that disappears.

But something else becomes visible.

The forest.

You can see where it begins and ends. You can see where growth is dense and where it has thinned. You can see a fire spreading three miles away from the person examining the bark.

Neither perspective contains the whole truth.

The ground reveals detail.

Altitude reveals pattern.

Organizations need both.

This is why I don't think the conflict between management and the people doing the work can be reduced to a simple story where management lives in spreadsheets while enlightened frontline employees understand reality.

Sometimes the spreadsheet sees something the frontline employee can't.

Sometimes the abstraction is exactly what we need.

The trouble begins when we forget what an abstraction is.

A dashboard isn't reality.

It's reality after we've decided which parts to keep.

That doesn't make it false.

It makes it useful.

But it also means that every time we move upward—from work to category, from category to metric, from metric to dashboard—we are making choices about what disappears.

And eventually we have to ask whether the things that disappeared were things we could afford to lose.

## The View From 30,000 Feet

The ground reveals detail.

Altitude reveals pattern.

Those aren't competing forms of knowledge. They're complementary.

Imagine an engineering team has been struggling to deliver a project.

Ask the engineers what's happening and you'll probably get a detailed answer.

A dependency changed unexpectedly. The original requirement underestimated the complexity of a particular feature. A key engineer was pulled onto another project. Testing uncovered an architectural problem nobody anticipated.

All of that matters.

Now imagine you're the manager responsible for ten teams.

You notice something the engineers on this particular team can't easily see.

Seven of your ten teams are behind schedule.

That's different.

One team being late probably requires understanding the team.

Seven teams being late probably requires understanding the system.

Maybe they're all depending on the same overloaded infrastructure group.

Maybe requirements are consistently arriving before they're ready.

Maybe the organization has committed to more work than it has people to perform.

Maybe a process introduced six months ago is adding far more overhead than anyone expected.

Maybe the company has developed the fascinating strategic practice of making everything the highest priority.

The engineer inside one team may not be able to see any of this. She's trying to solve Tuesday.

The manager is trying to understand why everyone seems to be having the same Tuesday.

That's what altitude can give us. It allows individual experiences to become patterns. But the pattern alone doesn't explain itself.

Suppose the dashboard shows that seven teams are behind schedule.

What should management conclude?

Perhaps the teams aren't executing well.

Perhaps the schedules were unrealistic.

Perhaps staffing is inadequate.

Perhaps some external dependency has become a bottleneck.

Perhaps the work is genuinely harder than expected.

The dashboard can reveal that something is happening without necessarily revealing what that something means.

This is where the ground becomes valuable again.

The manager sees the pattern. Then she goes looking for context.

She talks to the teams.

One says requirements keep changing.

Another says the approval process has become painfully slow.

A third says the same infrastructure team is blocking them.

A fourth mentions that two experienced engineers left and were never replaced.

Now the abstraction and the lived experience begin talking to each other.

The dashboard says:

**Something is happening across the system.**

The people say:

**Here's what it looks like from inside.**

Together, those two perspectives can produce something neither could produce alone.

Understanding.

This seems obvious when written down. It is considerably less obvious inside an organization.

There is a natural tendency for people at different levels to distrust one another's perspective.

People doing the work can look upward and think:

**They have no idea how this actually works.**

Sometimes they're right.

Managers can look downward and think:

**They're too close to the problem to see the larger picture.**

Sometimes they're right too.

The mistake is assuming that because the other person can't see what you see, they must not understand what's happening.

They may understand a different part of what's happening.

Consider our seven delayed teams.

The engineer knows that changing one particular interface will probably cost her team three weeks.

Her manager may not know what the interface does.

But the manager knows that six other teams are making similar changes because of a decision made somewhere else in the organization.

The engineer knows the local cost.

The manager sees the systemic cause.

If they argue over whose knowledge is more legitimate, they can waste a great deal of time being correct at each other.

If they combine what they know, they might actually solve the problem.

This is one of the reasons healthy organizations need information to travel in both directions.

We tend to think of organizational information as something that moves upward.

Employees report to managers.

Managers report to directors.

Directors report to executives.

Work becomes status.

Status becomes metrics.

Metrics become dashboards.

Information rises and becomes increasingly compressed.

But something also needs to move downward.

Context.

Why are we measuring this?

What pattern are we seeing?

What problem are we trying to solve?

What constraints exist outside this team's view?

Why does this deadline matter?

Why did leadership make this tradeoff?

Without that downward flow, people on the ground experience decisions without understanding the larger system that produced them.

And something else has to move upward too.

Not just data.

**Meaning.**

Why did the number change?

What is the metric missing?

Which cases don't fit neatly into the category?

What are people seeing that hasn't reached the dashboard yet?

What looks simple from above but becomes complicated when someone actually tries to do it?

The dashboard needs the ground to interpret it. The ground needs the dashboard to place itself in context.

This creates a kind of organizational stereoscopic vision. Close one eye and you still see something. You may even see it clearly.

But you lose depth.

The same thing happens when organizations rely too heavily on either abstraction or local experience.

Manage entirely from the dashboard and you may see patterns without understanding what produces them.

Manage entirely from individual experience and you may understand dozens of situations deeply without realizing they're all manifestations of the same problem.

One gives breadth.

The other gives depth.

Good judgment comes from moving between them.

This means the manager's job isn't to choose between the spreadsheet and the engineer. It's to let them correct each other.

If the dashboard says everything is fine and the people doing the work say everything is on fire, investigate.

If one team says the entire organization is broken but the broader data shows everyone else functioning normally, investigate that too.

Neither perspective gets automatic authority.

Both are evidence.

And when they disagree, the disagreement itself is information. Something doesn't fit.

Maybe the metric is missing something.

Maybe the local experience is unusual.

Maybe we're averaging together situations that shouldn't be averaged.

Maybe the people doing the work don't have context that changes how the problem looks.

Whatever the explanation, the tension is useful.

We don't have to decide whether the person standing beside the tree or the person looking down at the forest is the one who sees reality.

They both do.

They just see different parts of it.

The challenge is keeping them in conversation long enough to see the whole.

## Compression Is the Point

Every abstraction is an act of omission.

This is not a bug. It's what makes abstraction possible.

If I ask you how your day went, you probably won't begin:

“At 6:42 AM, I opened my eyes.”

Then proceed minute by minute until the present moment.

You compress.

“Pretty good. Work was frustrating, but I had a nice lunch with a friend.”

An entire day disappears into a sentence.

Thousands of events are gone.

The shower.

The commute.

The email you deleted without reading.

The meeting that could have been an email.

The email that somehow became a meeting.

All of it vanished.

And that's fine. You weren't asked to provide a forensic reconstruction of Tuesday. You were asked how your day went. The abstraction was appropriate to the question.

Organizations do the same thing.

A project contains thousands of individual decisions, conversations, problems, discoveries, mistakes, corrections, and compromises. Eventually someone asks:

“How's the project going?”

And all of that becomes:

**Yellow.**

There is something almost comical about this.

Six months of human effort.

Thirty-seven people.

Hundreds of meetings.

Thousands of lines of code.

Three architectural revisions.

A vendor problem.

Two staffing changes.

One spectacularly confusing requirement.

And somewhere in a PowerPoint deck:

Yellow.

But that's not necessarily ridiculous.

The executive looking at twenty projects may not need the story.

At least not yet.

She needs to know which projects deserve her attention.

That's what the yellow dot is for.

It isn't supposed to contain the project.

It's supposed to help her decide where to look.

This gives us a useful way to think about abstraction.

The question isn't:

**Did we lose information?**

Of course we did.

The question is:

**Did we lose information we needed?**

And the answer depends entirely on what we're trying to do.

If I'm deciding which of twenty projects needs attention, green-yellow-red might be perfectly adequate.

If I'm deciding whether to cancel the yellow project, it probably isn't.

If I'm deciding whether the project manager is performing well, I certainly hope we have more.

The same abstraction can be perfectly appropriate for one decision and dangerously inadequate for another.

This is easy to forget because once information has been compressed, the compressed version tends to travel.

The engineer explains the situation to her manager.

The manager summarizes it for a director.

The director puts it into a portfolio review.

The portfolio review becomes a dashboard.

The dashboard appears in an executive meeting.

At every stage, someone removes details that don't seem necessary for the next audience.

This has to happen. Otherwise the CEO eventually receives a 14,000-page weekly status report and spends Monday through Thursday reading about memory allocation.

But compression has an interesting property. It's much easier to remove context than to put it back.

Imagine the engineer originally says:

“We're about three weeks behind because the customer changed a major requirement after implementation started. We've adjusted the design, and we're confident in the new plan, but the original delivery date is no longer realistic.”

That becomes:

**Project delayed three weeks.**

Then:

**Schedule: Yellow.**

Nothing in the final statement is necessarily false. The project really is delayed. The schedule really may deserve to be yellow.

But notice what disappeared.

Why the delay happened.

Whether anyone could have prevented it.

Whether the team is now in control of the situation.

Whether the original schedule was still reasonable after the requirement changed.

Those details may not matter if we're simply trying to identify which projects are late.

They matter enormously if someone later asks:

**Why can't this team deliver on time?**

The abstraction that was perfectly useful for one purpose has quietly been recruited for another.

This is where dashboards can become dangerous. Not because they are simplified. They are supposed to be simplified.

The danger comes when we forget **what the simplification was designed to do**.

A weather app might tell me it's 94 degrees outside. That's useful if I'm deciding whether to wear a jacket. It tells me considerably less about whether a wildfire is likely to spread through a particular canyon.

Same weather.

Different question.

Different information required.

Metrics work the same way. Customer satisfaction might be useful for identifying broad changes in customer sentiment. It may be terrible for deciding whether a particular employee handled a particular customer correctly.

Average delivery time may tell us whether an organization is becoming faster overall. It may tell us very little about why one complicated project took twice as long as another.

Employee turnover may reveal that something deserves attention. It cannot tell us, by itself, whether people are leaving because of compensation, management, workload, career opportunities, retirement, organizational change, or an unusually charismatic competitor handing out free tacos.

The number isn't failing. We're asking it a question it was never designed to answer.

This suggests a simple rule:

**Match the resolution of the information to the resolution of the decision.**

Broad decisions can often tolerate broad abstractions. Specific decisions usually require specific context.

If I'm deciding where to investigate, the dashboard may be enough. If I'm deciding why something happened, I probably need to get closer. If I'm making a consequential judgment about a person, a team, or a particular event, I should probably get closer still.

The more specific and consequential the decision becomes, the more dangerous it is to rely entirely on compressed information.

This is especially important because abstraction creates psychological distance.

“Seven projects are behind schedule” feels like a management problem.

“Maria's team has been working nights for six weeks because a dependency changed after they committed to the schedule” feels different.

The first invites analysis.

The second invites understanding.

We need both.

An organization cannot operate entirely through stories. Individual stories are powerful, but they can also mislead. The most vivid experience isn't necessarily the most representative one. The angriest customer doesn't necessarily represent the customer base. The loudest employee doesn't necessarily represent the team.

That's why we aggregate. That's why we measure. That's why we compress.

But aggregation creates its own blindness. The average smooths the outlier. The category removes the story. The status color removes the cause. The dashboard removes the people.

Again, none of this means we should stop abstracting. It means we should remember what abstraction costs.

Every level of compression trades detail for perspective. Sometimes that's exactly the trade we need.

Sometimes it isn't.

The skill is knowing when the compressed version is enough and when we need to go back toward the source.

A yellow dot can tell us where to look. It should not always tell us what to think.

And the more important the decision becomes, the more willing we should be to leave the dashboard behind and find out what that yellow dot is actually made of.

## When the Dashboard Becomes More Real Than the Work

There is something deeply satisfying about a dashboard.

Reality is messy.

Dashboards are not.

Reality contains explanations, exceptions, contradictions, personalities, dependencies, history, uncertainty, and occasionally someone saying, “Well, it's complicated.”

Dashboards contain boxes.

Green.

Yellow.

Red.

There is a confidence to this.

The line moved 12 percent.

The project is yellow.

Customer satisfaction is 8.4.

Productivity increased.

There it is. We know things. Meanwhile, somewhere underneath the dashboard, someone is trying to explain that the situation doesn't quite work that way.

This is where abstraction can begin to acquire more authority than the reality it was created to represent.

Imagine a team has been reporting a project as green. Technically, this is defensible. The milestones are being completed. The schedule remains intact. The required status indicators are all within acceptable ranges.

But the people doing the work are worried.

The architecture has become increasingly fragile. Testing keeps revealing strange interactions between components. Two experienced engineers have quietly mentioned that they're uncomfortable with the current approach.

Nobody can point to a specific failure yet. There isn't a red box. There may not even be a yellow one. There is simply a growing sense among people close to the work that something isn't right.

Then comes the status review.

The dashboard says green.

Someone raises the concern.

And the conversation goes something like this:

“But we're on schedule, right?”

Yes.

“And the major milestones are complete?”

Yes.

“And testing is passing?”

Mostly.

“So what's the problem?”

Well.

It's complicated.

This is not a strong position to occupy in a meeting with a green dashboard behind you.

The dashboard has facts. You have a feeling. Except it may not really be a feeling. It may be accumulated judgment.

The engineers have spent months inside the system. They've seen problems that individually seem minor but collectively suggest something larger. They know where the shortcuts were taken. They know which tests make them nervous. They know which parts of the design nobody wants to touch.

Their knowledge is real. It's simply difficult to compress.

This happens frequently in complex work because expertise often appears first as discomfort.

Something doesn't look right.

Something doesn't sound right.

Something doesn't behave the way experience says it should.

An experienced doctor may notice something unusual before knowing which test will confirm it.

A teacher may know that a student is struggling even though the grades haven't collapsed.

A mechanic may hear an engine and say, “That's not supposed to sound like that.”

An engineer may look at a design and think:

**This is going to hurt us later.**

None of these judgments should be accepted blindly. Experts can be wrong. Intuition can be biased. People can resist change, misunderstand situations, or simply have bad days.

That's why we have data.

But the fact that judgment is imperfect doesn't make the dashboard complete. The danger comes when one kind of evidence is allowed to invalidate the other.

“The data says everything is fine.”

Maybe.

Or perhaps the data says everything **we currently measure** is fine.

Those are different statements.

This distinction becomes particularly important when dashboards flatten variation.

Suppose the average customer satisfaction score is 8.5 out of 10.

Sounds good.

But averages are talented little machines for making very different worlds look identical.

Imagine one company where nearly every customer rates the experience between eight and nine.

Now imagine another where half the customers give it a ten and half give it a seven.

Or one where most customers are delighted but a small group with a particular product is absolutely miserable.

The average might look similar. The underlying reality is not.

The dashboard compresses the distribution into a single number. Again, that's often useful. But nuance has disappeared.

The same thing happens when we aggregate across teams.

Suppose an engineering organization delivers 90 percent of its projects on time.

Excellent.

But perhaps half the teams deliver nearly everything on time while three teams are in constant crisis.

Maybe those teams are poorly managed. Maybe they own the hardest parts of the product. Maybe every other team depends on them and their apparent underperformance is actually the result of carrying the organization's most difficult work.

“90 percent on time” cannot tell us.

The abstraction has flattened differences that may matter enormously.

Categories do this too.

Consider a project labeled:

**Behind schedule.**

That category can contain wildly different realities.

One project is behind because the team underestimated the work. Another is behind because the customer changed the requirements.

Another lost half its staff. Another deliberately delayed delivery because testing revealed a serious safety issue.

Another is behind because the project is a catastrophe and everyone involved knows it.

Same box.

Different stories.

This is the unavoidable violence of categorization.

To create a category, we take things that are different and decide that, for some purpose, they are similar enough to treat as the same.

Usually that's fine. Sometimes it's enormously useful.

But the category does not erase the differences. It merely stops showing them. That distinction matters because the people looking at the dashboard may never know what was removed.

The person who created the status knows the story.

The person who receives the status sees the category.

This creates a strange asymmetry. People at the bottom of an organization often experience complexity. People at the top often experience clarity.

The higher information travels, the cleaner it becomes.

Uncertainty becomes a status.

Arguments become decisions.

Ranges become estimates.

Stories become numbers.

Messy projects become colored dots.

By the time everything reaches the executive dashboard, the organization can appear considerably more certain about itself than anyone actually doing the work feels.

Then those clean abstractions travel back downward as decisions.

This is where things can get frustrating. Someone close to the work says:

**That's not how it actually happens.**

And someone farther away says:

**That's not what the data shows.**

Both may be telling the truth. The data may accurately represent the abstraction. The employee may accurately represent the reality underneath it.

The conflict isn't necessarily between facts and feelings. It may be between two different resolutions of the same system.

But organizations often give the abstraction an advantage. It has a number. It has a chart. It appears in the official report. Someone spent a surprising amount of time aligning the boxes.

The lived experience has a story. And stories can sound suspiciously like excuses when they conflict with a number.

This is how the representation can slowly become more real than the thing it represents.

The project isn't healthy because the team believes it's healthy. It's healthy because it's green.

The customer isn't satisfied because we understand their experience. They're satisfied because the score is 8.5.

The organization isn't productive because useful things are happening. It's productive because productivity increased 7 percent.

Eventually reality finds itself in the peculiar position of having to prove that it exists.

This is a warning sign.

A healthy dashboard should make complexity navigable. It should not make complexity illegitimate.

When someone close to the work tells us that the abstraction is missing something, our first response shouldn't necessarily be to abandon the abstraction. But it shouldn't be to defend it either.

It should be curiosity.

**What are you seeing that the dashboard isn't?**

Maybe the answer changes nothing. Maybe the person is seeing an unusual case. Maybe the broader pattern really is more important.

But sometimes that question reveals the thing we most needed to know.

The exception.

The hidden dependency.

The fragile architecture.

The miserable group of customers buried inside a healthy average.

The problem everyone on the ground can see but nobody has figured out how to put into a box.

Dashboards are supposed to help us navigate complexity. They become dangerous when they convince us the complexity is gone.

## That’s Not How the Work Actually Happens

Spend enough time inside a large organization and you will eventually hear some version of this sentence:

**That's not how the work actually happens.**

It might be whispered during a meeting. It might appear in a private chat between coworkers. It might be said over lunch with considerably more colorful vocabulary.

Usually it arrives after someone has presented a new process.

On the screen, the process looks beautiful.

Requirements enter here.

Design happens here.

Implementation follows.

Then testing.

Then deployment.

There are arrows.

There are boxes.

Everything moves politely from left to right.

Nothing catches fire.

Nobody discovers halfway through implementation that the requirement doesn't make sense.

No dependency suddenly changes.

No customer reverses a decision.

No engineer spends three days investigating a bug that turns out to be caused by something two layers below the thing she was debugging.

No one says, “Wait. Who approved this?”

It is an extraordinarily peaceful place. And somewhere in the room, the people who actually do the work are thinking:

**That's not how the work actually happens.**

This is one of the sources of tension between upper management and the people closer to the work.

The higher you move in an organization, the more likely you are to interact with abstractions.

Strategies.

Roadmaps.

Budgets.

Headcount.

Milestones.

Portfolios.

Metrics.

Operating models.

The closer you are to the work, the more likely you are to interact with exceptions.

The build failed.

The customer changed their mind.

The requirement is ambiguous.

The test environment is broken.

The new tool doesn't support the thing we were told it supports.

The person who understands the system is on vacation.

Reality has once again declined to follow the PowerPoint.

Neither level is unnecessary. Someone has to think about strategy. Someone has to allocate budgets. Someone has to decide which products get funded and which don't.

Someone has to look across the organization and create enough structure that thousands of people aren't independently sprinting toward whatever seems important on Tuesday morning.

The problem isn't that management deals in abstractions. Management **has** to deal in abstractions.

The problem begins when someone who primarily experiences the abstraction forgets that the people underneath it experience something considerably messier.

Then the abstraction can start coming back down as prescription.

Leadership sees that projects move too slowly. So a new development process is introduced. The process makes perfect sense from above.

There are clear stages.

Each stage has an owner.

Each owner has a deliverable.

Each deliverable has a date.

Each date presumably has another date underneath it.

Visibility improves.

Accountability improves.

The boxes are magnificent.

Then someone tries to use it.

The requirement isn't complete enough to begin design. But according to the process, requirements are complete.

The designer discovers something that requires changing the requirement. But that phase is closed.

Implementation reveals that the original architecture won't work.

Testing begins before implementation is finished because the schedule says testing begins Tuesday.

Now the people doing the work face an interesting choice.

They can follow the process. Or they can accomplish the work.

Usually they attempt both.

This produces a third process.

The real one.

The real process is rarely documented.

It consists of workarounds, judgment calls, side conversations, favors, shortcuts, unofficial coordination, and people quietly doing whatever is necessary to keep the official process functioning.

The official process says:

**Requirements → Design → Implementation → Testing → Deployment**

The real process says:

**Requirements → Design → Wait, what does this requirement mean? → Requirements → Prototype → Discover problem → Design → Implementation → Discover another problem → Ask someone who worked on this five years ago → Implementation → Testing → Swear quietly → Implementation → Testing → Deployment**

And that's on a good project.

This doesn't mean process is useless. Quite the opposite. Without some common structure, complicated organizations become chaos.

The abstraction gives everyone a shared language. It creates expectations. It helps coordinate teams. It lets us identify where work is supposed to be.

The mistake is demanding that reality become as clean as the model.

A good process describes work well enough to coordinate it.

A bad process requires people to pretend the work happened the way the process says it should have happened.

Frontline employees become very good at recognizing the difference. And this is where cynicism can begin.

Leadership announces a new initiative.

The people doing the work exchange looks.

Another transformation.

Another framework.

Another process improvement.

Another tool that will “streamline” something.

Another mandatory training explaining how much time the new system will save once everyone has spent several hours learning how to use it.

From above, this reaction can look frustrating.

Why are these people so negative?

Why are they resistant to change?

Why can't they embrace the new process?

Sometimes that's exactly what's happening.

People can become cynical enough to reject genuinely good ideas simply because they came from management.

Organizations accumulate scar tissue. After enough failed initiatives, even a useful change can trigger antibodies. But cynicism doesn't appear from nowhere.

Sometimes it is learned.

It develops after people repeatedly watch simplified descriptions of their work travel upward and return as instructions for how that work should be done.

The employee says:

**This will add three steps to something that already works.**

Leadership says:

**The new process improves efficiency.**

The employee says:

**We have to enter the same information in two systems now.**

Leadership says:

**The dashboard shows adoption is increasing.**

The employee says:

**The tool doesn't handle our use case.**

Leadership says:

**Ninety-two percent of users completed training.**

Everyone is technically discussing the same initiative. They are not discussing the same reality.

After enough experiences like this, people stop explaining.

They comply.

They fill in the form.

They attend the training.

They update the status.

They learn which boxes need to be green.

Then they go back to their desks and figure out how to get the actual work done.

This is a dangerous moment for an organization because compliance can look remarkably similar to agreement from far enough away.

The dashboard says adoption: 94 percent.

Leadership sees success. The employees see the thing they had to click so the reminder emails would stop. Both numbers and cynicism can now reinforce each other.

Leadership sees employees complaining despite improving metrics and concludes that employees are resistant. Employees see leadership celebrating improving metrics despite their complaints and conclude that leadership doesn't listen. Each side acquires evidence for what it already believes about the other.

Management thinks:

**They don't see the bigger picture.**

Employees think:

**They have no idea how this actually works.**

And sometimes both are right. That's the frustrating part. The people doing the work may not understand the constraints leadership is balancing.

The cumbersome process may exist because of a legal requirement they don't know about.

The annoying documentation may be necessary because another team depends on it.

The deadline may come from a customer commitment that cannot easily move.

The new system may solve a serious problem elsewhere in the organization even if it makes this particular team's life harder.

Frontline experience is not the whole world either. This is why the answer cannot simply be:

**Management should listen to employees.**

Of course they should.

But employees also need enough context to understand what management is trying to accomplish.

We're back to information moving in both directions.

The people at the top need to ask:

**What does this look like when someone actually tries to do it?**

The people at the bottom need to be able to ask:

**What problem are we trying to solve?**

Those two questions belong together.

Without the first, leadership can design elegant systems that collapse on contact with reality. Without the second, employees can optimize their local experience while missing the larger needs of the organization.

The goal isn't for management to abandon abstraction and descend permanently into the details. Nor is it for frontline employees to stop questioning decisions they believe don't make sense.

The goal is to keep the abstraction connected to the work.

Because the sentence:

**That's not how the work actually happens**

isn't necessarily an act of resistance.

Sometimes it's feedback. And organizations that become too cynical about cynicism may miss what the cynics are trying to tell them.

## The Micromanager Comes Down the Mountain

So perhaps we've found the solution.

Management needs to get closer to the work.

Dashboards hide complexity. Processes simplify reality. Important context disappears as information moves upward.

Fine.

Come down.

Talk to people.

Understand what they actually do.

Learn the details.

What could possibly go wrong?

Quite a lot, as it turns out.

I've experienced the opposite problem too.

A manager becomes deeply interested in the details of the work. Not just whether a project is progressing, but how it's progressing. Not just what decision was made, but why. Not just whether a problem was solved, but how it was solved and what other solutions were considered.

At first, this can feel refreshing.

Finally.

Someone in management actually cares about how the work happens.

Then the questions keep coming.

Why did you choose this design?

Did you consider this alternative?

Who reviewed it?

What did they say?

Why is this task taking longer than expected?

Could we break it into smaller tasks?

Have you documented the decision?

Can you send me the document?

Can we have a meeting to discuss the document?

Would you mind adding a section to the document explaining what we discussed in the meeting about the document?

At some point, the team begins longing for the dashboard.

This is the other side of organizational distance. Management can be too far from the work. Management can also be far too close. And both can damage morale.

When management is too far away, people feel unseen. They watch decisions being made by people who don't seem to understand the consequences of those decisions. They hear their complicated work described in language they barely recognize. They begin to believe that leadership doesn't understand what they do.

But when management gets too close, something different happens. People feel distrusted. Every decision requires explanation. Every judgment requires justification. Every deviation becomes a conversation. The manager may believe she is gathering context. The employee may experience something else entirely:

**Apparently I'm not trusted to do my job.**

This matters especially in knowledge work. If you've hired someone because they possess expertise, part of what you've hired is their judgment.

The senior engineer isn't valuable merely because she can type code quickly. She has spent years learning which problems deserve investigation, which risks matter, which shortcuts are harmless, which shortcuts will come back six months later carrying a knife, and which strange-looking solution is actually the least terrible option available.

You cannot fully capture that judgment in a process. That's why you hired the person.

Micromanagement slowly replaces that judgment with approval.

Instead of asking:

**What's the right thing to do?**

the employee begins asking:

**What will my manager want me to do?**

That's a profound change.

The first question exercises expertise. The second exercises prediction. And people can become extremely good at prediction.

They learn which kinds of decisions trigger questions. They learn which details the manager likes to see. They learn how much explanation is necessary. They learn which choices are easiest to defend.

Eventually they may stop making the decision they believe is best and start making the decision they believe will survive review with the least friction.

We have created another optimization problem.

Not:

**Do good work.**

But:

**Do work that is easy to explain upward.**

The manager may never have intended this. In fact, the manager may be trying very hard to be responsible.

If something goes wrong, she's accountable. She wants to understand the risks. She wants to prevent surprises. She wants to help.

Those are reasonable desires. But there is a cost to every request for detail. Someone has to provide it.

A thirty-minute status meeting involving eight people doesn't cost thirty minutes.

It costs four hours.

A weekly report doesn't simply appear because someone created a template. Someone gathers the information. Someone formats it. Someone explains the yellow boxes. Someone revises the explanation because the first explanation was apparently insufficiently executive.

Eventually an organization can spend a remarkable amount of time explaining the work to itself.

The work continues somewhere in the intervals.

This is where micromanagement becomes particularly ironic. The manager gets closer because she wants the work to go better. But the act of getting closer begins interfering with the work.

Decisions slow down because approval is required. Employees hesitate because they expect their judgment to be second-guessed. Meetings multiply because everyone needs context.

The manager becomes a bottleneck. Soon the team has a new operational dependency.

Management.

Then morale begins to change. This isn't simply because people dislike being asked questions. Good employees generally expect accountability. They understand that managers need visibility. They know their decisions affect other people. The problem is the gradual loss of ownership.

There is a particular satisfaction in being trusted with a difficult problem.

Someone says:

**Here's what we need. You know this area. Figure it out.**

That communicates something.

I trust your expertise.

I trust your judgment.

I expect you to tell me when you need help.

I will hold you accountable for the result.

Those things can coexist. Autonomy does not mean absence of accountability. It means having meaningful room to exercise judgment inside it.

Micromanagement collapses that room. And once people stop feeling ownership, something predictable happens.

They stop owning things.

Why take a risk if every decision will be reviewed anyway?

Why solve the larger problem if you're only responsible for the assigned task?

Why exercise judgment if judgment mostly creates additional questions?

Why not simply ask the manager what she wants?

Eventually the manager may become frustrated.

**Why doesn't anyone take initiative?**

This is one of those questions worth sitting with for a moment.

Perhaps the team lacks initiative. Or perhaps the system has been quietly training initiative out of them.

If every meaningful decision travels upward, people eventually learn to send meaningful decisions upward. Then management sees a team that cannot operate independently and concludes that closer supervision was necessary all along.

The loop closes.

This is the mirror image of the problem we saw earlier. When management stays too far away, employees think:

**You don't understand my work.**

When management gets too close, employees think:

**You don't trust me to do my work.**

Both are disheartening.

One removes understanding. The other removes autonomy.

Good management somehow has to provide enough of both.

Understand the work without needing to perform it.

Ask enough questions to recognize problems without requiring an explanation for every decision.

Create visibility without creating surveillance.

Hold people accountable without making them ask permission.

Know when to enter the details.

And, just as importantly, know when to leave.

That's the part we sometimes miss.

We've spent much of this chapter talking about the danger of being too far from reality. But proximity isn't automatically wisdom. Sometimes the best thing a manager can do after understanding the problem is step back and let the people she hired solve it.

Coming down from the mountain is useful. You just don't have to move into the village.
