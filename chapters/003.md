# 3 - The Dashboard Is Not the World

### You Cannot Manage What You Can't See

Imagine the CEO of a company with fifty thousand employees decides dashboards are too abstract.

She has read the first two chapters of this book and taken them very seriously. Metrics are incomplete, context matters, and the people closest to the work understand things the numbers cannot see. Perhaps the whole problem with modern management, she concludes, is that leaders have become too dependent on spreadsheets and dashboards when they should be talking to actual human beings.

Fine. No more dashboards.

On Monday morning she sits down with an engineer and asks how things are going. The engineer explains that a recent change has exposed a memory-allocation problem in a subsystem designed several years ago, and that the problem only appears under a particular workload because two components are interacting in a way nobody originally expected. The CEO asks thoughtful questions, the engineer fills in some history, and forty-five minutes later she understands the problem much better.

This is excellent leadership.

Only 49,999 employees to go.

There is a reason organizations create dashboards, and it isn't simply because managers enjoy charts. At some point direct experience stops scaling. A manager with six employees may understand a great deal about what each person is doing, while a director responsible for sixty people cannot possibly maintain the same level of detail, and a vice president responsible for six thousand people would have to abandon sleep entirely to make a serious attempt.

This isn't a failure of leadership. It's arithmetic.

As an organization gets larger, reality has to be compressed before anyone can make sense of it. Individual events become categories, categories become counts, counts become trends, and eventually the trends appear as charts on a screen somewhere. Something is lost at every step, but something is gained too: a person who could never hold thousands of individual experiences in their head can suddenly see patterns across all of them.

Suppose you're an engineer on a team whose project is running late. From where you sit, the explanation may be obvious. A dependency arrived later than expected, a technical problem turned out to be harder than anyone anticipated, and halfway through implementation someone discovered that a requirement meant something different from what everybody thought it meant.

You understand your Tuesday.

Your manager, however, may be looking at ten teams and noticing that seven of them are running late. The details of your particular dependency still matter, but seven delayed teams suggest a different kind of question. Maybe planning assumptions are unrealistic across the organization, several teams depend on the same overloaded group, priorities keep changing, or the company has developed the fascinating strategic practice of making everything the highest priority.

The engineer is trying to solve Tuesday. The manager is trying to understand why everyone seems to be having the same Tuesday.

That's the gift abstraction gives us. The person close to the work can see details that disappear at scale, while the person farther away can sometimes see patterns that disappear inside those details. Neither view is inherently more real than the other; they are different views of the same reality, each revealing something the other has difficulty seeing.

This is one reason I resist the easy story in which management lives inside spreadsheets while the enlightened people doing the work understand how things *really* are. Sometimes the spreadsheet sees something the frontline employee can't. An engineer may understand one component better than anyone else in the company and still have no idea that five other teams are struggling with the same dependency.

The opposite problem is just as real. A manager looking at five teams may see the common dependency while knowing almost nothing about the technical details that make it difficult, and if she assumes that seeing the pattern means she understands each individual case, the abstraction begins doing work it cannot support.

The ground reveals detail. Altitude reveals pattern.

Organizations need both, which means the interesting question isn't which perspective deserves to win. It's how we move between them without forgetting what each one can and cannot see.

### Compression Is the Point

Suppose an executive asks a perfectly ordinary question: “How's the project going?”

Taken literally, this is an absurd request.

The project has been running for six months. Thirty-seven people have worked on it, producing hundreds of meetings, thousands of messages, architectural decisions, revised requirements, staffing changes, vendor problems, bugs, breakthroughs, misunderstandings, and at least one meeting that probably could have been an email. There are people on the project who couldn't completely answer the question even if you gave them the afternoon.

“How's the project going?”

Well.

How much time do you have?

But that usually isn't what the executive is asking. She may be responsible for twenty projects and needs to know which ones are healthy, which are drifting, and which are quietly approaching the sort of event that eventually requires an executive meeting with the word *urgent* in the subject line.

So we compress six months of organizational reality into something she can use.

Green. Yellow. Red.

There is something objectively funny about this. Thirty-seven human beings spend half a year navigating an enormously complicated technical and organizational system, and eventually the entire experience becomes a yellow circle on PowerPoint.

But the yellow circle may be exactly what the executive needs.

If the question is *Which of my twenty projects deserves my attention?*, yellow may be sufficient. If the question becomes *Should we cancel this project?*, I'd want considerably more information, and if we're about to ask whether the project manager is performing poorly, I certainly hope somebody has taken the time to find out what the yellow circle is actually made of.

The problem isn't that the dot is simple. Simplicity is its job. The problem appears when we use a low-resolution representation to make a high-resolution decision.

This gives us a useful principle for thinking about dashboards: **match the resolution of the information to the resolution of the decision.**

Organizations violate this surprisingly often, partly because compressed information has a tendency to travel. Imagine a project falls three weeks behind because a customer changed an important requirement after implementation had already begun. The existing architecture no longer fits, the team has redesigned part of the system, and after doing the work they believe the revised schedule is realistic.

The engineering manager explains this to the director, who doesn't need every technical detail. The director summarizes it for a vice president, who needs even less, and by the time the information reaches an executive portfolio review, the perfectly accurate summary may simply say:

> Schedule: Yellow

Nobody lied. Nobody even summarized irresponsibly. Each person removed information that didn't seem necessary for the next person to do their job, which is exactly what an organization has to do unless we want the CEO spending Monday through Thursday reading about memory allocation.

Three months later, however, someone reviews delivery performance and notices that this team missed its original date.

“Why can't this team deliver on time?”

Now we have a problem, because information created to answer one question has quietly been recruited to answer another. The yellow status was perfectly adequate for identifying a project that deserved executive attention, but it contains very little of the information required to decide whether the team executed poorly.

This is one of the ways dashboards acquire more authority than their designers intended. Once information is clean, visible, and conveniently sitting there, we're tempted to reuse it. Why gather more context when we already have a number?

Because sometimes the question has changed.

A weather app can tell me whether I should bring an umbrella. I probably shouldn't use the same screen to decide where to build a bridge, not because the weather information suddenly became bad, but because I've given it a different job.

Metrics work the same way. Average delivery time might be very useful for noticing that an organization is slowing down overall while telling us relatively little about why one complicated project took twice as long as another. Employee turnover may reveal a pattern worth investigating while being almost useless, by itself, for explaining why a particular employee left.

The number hasn't failed. We've simply asked it a question it wasn't designed to answer.

Broad decisions can often tolerate broad abstractions. Specific decisions usually require specific context, and the more consequential the decision becomes—particularly when we're judging a person or team—the more willing we should be to leave the dashboard and move closer to the work.

That doesn't mean individual stories automatically deserve more authority. Stories have their own distortions. The angriest customer isn't necessarily representative of the customer base, the loudest employee doesn't necessarily speak for the team, and the most memorable failure can consume organizational attention while a much larger pattern goes unnoticed.

That's why we aggregate in the first place.

The art is knowing when the abstraction is sufficient for the decision we're making and when it isn't. A yellow dot can tell us where to look. It should not always tell us what to think.

### When the Dashboard Becomes More Real Than the Work

There is another problem with clean representations of messy realities: sometimes the clean representation begins to feel more authoritative than the reality it was created to represent.

I've experienced this in software. A project can look green by every measure we've agreed to track. Milestones are being completed, major features work, testing is mostly passing, and the schedule still looks reasonable.

And yet the engineers working inside the system begin to feel uncomfortable.

The architecture is becoming fragile. Changes that should be simple are taking longer, fixes in one area keep producing unexpected behavior somewhere else, and experienced engineers have started exchanging the sort of looks that don't appear in the project-management system.

Nobody can point to a catastrophic failure because there isn't one.

At least not yet.

Eventually someone raises the concern in a status meeting, where it encounters an unfortunate rhetorical disadvantage: there is a green dashboard projected on the wall.

“So what's the problem?”

Well.

It's complicated.

This is not an especially powerful position to occupy when the dashboard behind you is saying everything is fine. The dashboard has milestones, schedules, test results, and numbers, while you have something that sounds suspiciously like a feeling.

Except it may not really be a feeling.

Expertise often includes the ability to recognize weak signals before they become obvious failures. An experienced engineer has seen enough systems deteriorate to recognize certain patterns: increasing coupling, changes that produce surprising side effects, areas of code that everyone has become afraid to touch. A project manager may similarly sense that a schedule is becoming unrealistic before a milestone is officially missed because she has watched enough projects drift toward trouble to recognize what the early stages look like.

That kind of knowledge is difficult to compress. It is also fallible.

Experienced engineers have biases, project managers have hunches that turn out to be wrong, and a team that dislikes an architectural decision can interpret every inconvenience as further evidence that the architecture is doomed. This is precisely why we need measurement: intuition should be challenged by evidence rather than granted mystical authority merely because it comes from someone with twenty years of experience.

But the reverse is also true. The absence of a red box doesn't prove the absence of a problem.

When someone says, “The data says everything is fine,” what they often mean is something slightly narrower: **everything we currently measure is fine.** That may be enough. Or it may be the beginning of a useful conversation.

The best response to disagreement between the dashboard and the people close to the work is usually curiosity. What are the engineers seeing? What is the dashboard seeing? What evidence would help us distinguish a meaningful weak signal from an unfounded concern?

This same tension appears in the processes organizations create to describe work. Software-development processes often look wonderfully orderly from a distance: requirements lead to design, design leads to implementation, implementation leads to testing, and testing leads to deployment.

That is, in fact, a reasonable description of software development.

It just isn't always how Tuesday goes.

Tuesday may begin in implementation, where we discover that a requirement doesn't make sense. That sends us back to the requirement, which exposes an assumption in the design nobody noticed, so we revise the design, implement half of it, test something, discover an old dependency, track down the one person who remembers why it was built that way five years ago, reconsider the design, implement again, test again, swear quietly, fix something, and eventually deploy.

Later, the project-management system reports that implementation is complete.

Technically, yes.

We need the simplified process because thirty-seven people cannot coordinate by exchanging personal narratives about their Tuesday. The danger comes when the description that helped us coordinate the work becomes a demand that reality conform to the description.

A good process describes work well enough to coordinate it. A bad process requires people to pretend the work happened the way the process says it should have happened.

And even here, we should resist making management the villain. The process an engineer finds maddening may exist because another team depends on a predictable handoff, because a regulator requires documentation, or because something went catastrophically wrong six years ago and everyone who remembers why the rule exists has since moved on.

The engineer experiences the cost of the process. The manager may understand the reason for it.

Again, neither view contains the whole system.

Which means the solution can't simply be for management to get closer to the work. There is such a thing as getting too close.

### The Right Altitude

Suppose a manager reads everything so far and decides to fix the problem.

Clearly the dashboard is too abstract. It's time to come down from the mountain and reconnect with the work.

So the manager starts attending team meetings.

Good.

Then design reviews.

Still reasonable.

Then bug triage. Then individual work-item reviews. Before long, an engineer estimates a task at five days and receives a message asking whether they've considered doing it another way. This is followed by a request to write up the alternatives, then a meeting to discuss the document, then a request to add a section to the document explaining what everyone discussed in the meeting about the document.

At some point, the team begins longing for the dashboard.

Getting closer to the work is not automatically better. At one extreme, management is so distant that employees wonder whether anyone making decisions understands what they actually do. At the other, management is so involved that employees wonder whether anyone trusts them to do it.

Both are failures of altitude.

A manager needs enough distance to see the system and enough proximity to understand what the system cannot show. There isn't one correct altitude because the appropriate altitude depends on the question.

If twelve teams are missing deadlines, stay high for a while. Look for patterns across the organization: priorities that change too frequently, a shared dependency, unrealistic planning assumptions, or simply more work than the organization has capacity to perform.

If eleven teams are doing fine and one is consistently struggling, come down. Perhaps the project is unusually difficult, perhaps the team has a staffing problem, perhaps the manager needs help, or perhaps the team simply isn't executing very well.

Altitude helps determine which questions become visible. Proximity helps us answer them.

There is another complication when managers descend: they bring their authority with them.

A senior leader can enter a design review and say, quite sincerely, “I wonder if we should consider rebuilding this component.” To the leader, this is brainstorming. They're thinking aloud, inviting discussion, perhaps even expecting the engineers to explain why rebuilding the component would be a terrible idea.

The team may hear something rather different.

*Rebuild this component.*

Three weeks later, the senior leader discovers that everyone has run off and done something they don't remember asking anyone to do.

Authority adds weight to curiosity.

This is the same measurer-measuree dynamic we encountered in the previous chapter. The person with more authority may experience an interaction as exploration, while the person with less authority has to calculate whether disagreeing is safe and whether a suggestion is really a suggestion.

So changing altitude requires more than simply showing up. It requires some awareness of what happens to the environment when you arrive.

The goal isn't for managers to understand every implementation detail or participate in every decision. It's to know enough about the territory that the map remains connected to it, which might mean occasionally talking to the people doing the work, asking for the story behind an unusual number, sitting in on a review, visiting a customer, reading a bug report, or watching how a process actually works.

Then go back up.

That part matters too. The purpose of descending isn't to relocate permanently. A manager isn't supposed to live at thirty thousand feet, but neither should the manager permanently set up a folding chair beside the engineer's desk.

Good management requires movement. Go high enough to see the pattern, come down far enough to understand what it means, and then go back up to see whether what you learned changes the pattern.

Stay connected without taking over.

### Dashboards Should Create Questions

We often treat dashboards as answer machines.

How are sales? Green.

How's the project? Yellow.

How's customer satisfaction? Eighty-four.

How's engineering productivity? Up 7.3 percent.

There. Management achieved.

The most useful dashboards, however, may have a more modest job. Rather than telling us what reality means, they can tell us where reality deserves our attention.

Suppose customer complaints rise 18 percent. One possible explanation is that customer satisfaction has deteriorated, and we should certainly take that possibility seriously. But perhaps a recent product release introduced one specific problem, or perhaps the company just launched a better feedback system and customers can finally report issues that were previously invisible.

The number directs our attention. Reality supplies the context, and the context may change how we interpret the number.

We should be willing to do the same thing when the number looks good. Organizations tend to be quite skilled at investigating bad news: revenue misses target, customer complaints rise, a project turns red, and everyone immediately wants to know what happened.

Then revenue exceeds the target by three percent.

Everybody stop.

Something has gone terribly right.

We should probably understand why.

Perhaps a team did exceptional work, market conditions changed, a one-time event produced the increase, or somebody discovered something genuinely valuable that could be repeated elsewhere. Green numbers don't explain themselves any more than red ones do; we're simply less inclined to interrogate them because we like what they're saying.

This suggests a better question for a dashboard: **Does this number help me know where to ask better questions?**

That's a more modest role than asking the dashboard to tell us what reality means, but I think it's a more useful one. A yellow project status doesn't need to contain six months of history. It needs to tell the executive that this may be a place worth looking.

Then someone comes down in altitude.

They talk with the project manager and learn about the changed requirement. They discover that the team responded well, that the redesign was probably the right choice, and that the three-week delay may actually reflect sound judgment rather than poor execution.

Now the dashboard means something different.

Perhaps they return to the executive view and notice that four other yellow projects depend on the same organization. What initially looked like a collection of unrelated project problems begins to look like a systemic bottleneck.

So we go back up.

This is the relationship I think we're after. The dashboard sends us toward reality, and reality sends us back toward the dashboard with better questions. Sometimes the abstraction reveals a pattern that no individual could see; sometimes the people closest to the work reveal something the abstraction had to discard.

Neither gets the final word automatically.

The point isn't numbers versus experience, managers versus employees, or dashboards versus stories. The point is movement between levels of reality, using each perspective to correct the blind spots of the other.

The ground reveals detail. Altitude reveals pattern. Most difficult organizational decisions require some combination of the two.

A dashboard can tell us that something deserves our attention. It can show us where the pattern changed, where the outlier appeared, or where something seems to be moving in the wrong direction. What it usually cannot do, all by itself, is tell us what that change means.

For that, we have to go look.

The metric should begin the investigation, not conclude it.

Use the dashboard. That's what it's there for.

Just keep one foot on the ground.
