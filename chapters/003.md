# 3 — When the Territory Notices the Map

There is something wonderfully uncomplicated about measuring a machine. If a processor is running too hot, it does not become embarrassed when you display the temperature at a staff meeting. If a piece of software takes too long to complete an operation, it does not choose an easier operation next time so its performance review will go better. Machines may be complicated, but at least they are indifferent to our opinions of them. People have the inconvenient habit of noticing when they are being watched.

This changes measurement in a fundamental way. When we measure a physical object, the measurement generally stands outside the system we are trying to understand. When we measure people, the measurement can enter the system and become one of the forces shaping what happens next. People learn what is being counted, what gets discussed, which numbers produce uncomfortable questions, and which accomplishments receive praise. Then, quite reasonably, they adjust.

Organizations sometimes describe this adjustment as gaming the metric, which makes it sound as though the employees have violated an otherwise pristine measurement system. Sometimes people certainly do manipulate numbers dishonestly, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise. More often, though, the response is subtler and much more human. People simply become attentive to the things the organization has demonstrated that it cares about.

This is one of the strange properties of human systems. A metric can begin as an attempt to observe reality and end up helping create the reality it observes. The map does not merely represent the territory anymore. The territory has noticed the map.

## The Territory Notices

Return to the bug-duration metric from the previous chapter. Leadership wants bugs resolved more quickly, which is a reasonable objective because defects that remain open can delay products, consume engineering time, and leave customers waiting. Someone creates a dashboard showing how long bugs have been open, perhaps with a few useful categories and a nice trend line. At first, it is simply information.

Then someone notices a cluster of old bugs and asks about them. The question travels downward through the organization, perhaps from a director to a manager and then to the engineers responsible for the work. Nobody is angry, and nobody has threatened anyone's career. A leader saw something on a dashboard and did exactly what leaders are supposed to do: asked what was happening.

But the engineer experiences the exchange differently. From the director's perspective, the question may be simple curiosity: *Why are these bugs taking longer?* From the engineer's perspective, the same question arrives through a hierarchy in which decisions about assignments, ratings, promotions, and future opportunities are made. The engineer may reasonably conclude that old bugs create attention and that attention creates explanations. Next time there is a choice between a straightforward bug and a complicated one with an uncertain ending, that knowledge is now part of the decision.

Nobody had to issue a bad instruction. In fact, every person involved may have behaved exactly as we would want them to behave. The leader looked at data, the manager followed up, and the engineer learned from feedback. The problem is that feedback in a human system carries meaning in addition to information.

This is where the distinction between the measurer and the measured begins to matter. The person creating the metric may see a neutral instrument for understanding performance. The person whose work appears inside it may experience the same instrument as an indication of how they are being evaluated. The number hasn't changed, but its meaning depends on which side of the dashboard you are standing.

A thermometer does not care what the doctor thinks of its reading. An engineer does care what a manager thinks of theirs. That difference turns measurement from observation into communication, whether or not anyone intended to communicate anything. Once people understand what the organization watches, they begin learning what the organization values.

## When Measurement Acquires Consequences

Metrics rarely become powerful because someone announces that everyone must now worship the dashboard. Their authority develops gradually. A team begins tracking something because it would be useful to know, the number appears in a report, the report becomes part of a regular meeting, and eventually people notice that certain numbers receive more attention than others. What began as visibility slowly acquires consequences.

The consequences do not have to be formal. Perhaps nobody's bonus is tied to the bug-duration number, but a manager whose team consistently appears red may find themselves answering the same question every month. A manager who wants to avoid that conversation naturally asks the team to focus on the old bugs. Engineers who want to help their manager naturally respond. The organization has created an incentive without ever writing one down.

This happens because hierarchy gives curiosity weight. When I ask a coworker why something happened, they may hear a question. When someone several levels above them asks exactly the same thing, they may hear a request for justification even if no criticism was intended. Leaders sometimes underestimate this because they experience themselves as ordinary people asking reasonable questions, while the person answering is acutely aware that the ordinary person asking the question can influence their career.

That does not mean leaders should stop asking questions. An organization in which senior people are afraid to inquire about anything would be a peculiar improvement. It does mean that measurement cannot be separated completely from the relationships in which it operates. Information travels through structures of authority, trust, history, and expectation, and those structures affect how the information is received.

This is one reason a metric's official purpose tells us less than we might think. We may say the dashboard exists “for visibility” or “to start conversations,” and we may sincerely mean it. The more revealing question is what happens after the conversation starts. If every outlier eventually produces pressure to move the number, people will learn that lesson regardless of what the dashboard's introductory slide says.

Over time, this creates an organizational vocabulary that nobody had to design. Green means safe. Red means attention. Improvement means relief, while an outlier means someone may have to explain themselves. The colors were intended to simplify information, but human beings are very good at discovering the social meaning hidden inside a color.

## The Number Becomes the Target

There is a well-known idea usually summarized as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it tends to become less useful as a measure. The exact mechanics vary, but the basic problem is easy to understand. We choose a number because it correlates with something we care about, then begin managing the number as though it were the thing itself. Eventually people discover ways to improve the representation without producing an equivalent improvement in the underlying reality.

Imagine a customer-service organization that wants customers to receive help quickly. Someone notices that long calls often correspond with difficult experiences, so the organization begins tracking average call duration. A target of eight minutes seems reasonable, and perhaps initially it helps identify inefficient processes. Then employees learn that calls longer than eight minutes create problems for them.

The original goal was to help the customer. The operational goal has quietly become finishing the call. Those goals overlap much of the time, which is why the metric seemed sensible in the first place. The trouble appears precisely in the cases where they stop overlapping.

A representative may rush an explanation, transfer a difficult customer, or solve the immediate problem without addressing the underlying issue that will cause another call tomorrow. None of those behaviors require the employee to be lazy or malicious. If the organization repeatedly communicates that eight minutes is success, employees will naturally learn how to produce eight-minute calls. They are responding to the system that has been built around them.

This suggests a useful question whenever we create a performance measure: *How could someone make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing I care about?* The question is not an accusation. It is a way of discovering the distance between the metric and the purpose it represents. The larger that distance, the more room there is for rational behavior to move the number without moving the underlying reality.

The problem becomes especially difficult when the underlying purpose is complicated. Software quality is not one thing, customer satisfaction is not one thing, and employee productivity is certainly not one thing. We select a measurable feature because we need some way to see what is happening. Then the precision of the number can make the fuzzy reality behind it surprisingly easy to forget.

## Rational People in Irrational Systems

One of the easiest mistakes in discussions about metrics is to divide the organization into enlightened people who understand reality and foolish people who chase numbers. Real organizations are rarely that convenient. Managers usually have legitimate reasons for wanting visibility, employees usually have legitimate reasons for responding to incentives, and executives usually have legitimate reasons for asking why an important number is moving in the wrong direction. Dysfunction can emerge even when nearly everyone involved is behaving rationally.

Consider again our two engineers. One produces code quickly, introduces several bugs, and then fixes those bugs quickly, generating a steady stream of visible accomplishments. Another spends more time understanding the architecture, catches problems before they escape, mentors a less experienced teammate, and takes ownership of a subtle defect that requires weeks to reproduce. Both may be doing valuable work, but one produces much more activity that fits neatly into the organization's tracking systems.

This is not necessarily because the organization values carelessness. It may explicitly value quality, mentoring, collaboration, and thoughtful engineering. The difficulty is that prevented bugs do not arrive in the database with a field saying *this defect would have existed if Susan hadn't noticed the race condition on Tuesday*. A bug that never happens is wonderfully useful and statistically elusive.

Mentoring has a similar problem. An experienced engineer can spend an hour helping someone understand a system well enough to avoid weeks of future confusion. The resulting improvement may appear later as someone else's productivity, or it may never become visible at all. If the mentor had spent that hour closing a bug, the accomplishment would have been much easier to count.

This creates a gap between what organizations say they value and what their measurement systems make visible. The gap does not prove that the stated values are insincere. It may simply reflect the practical difficulty of representing complicated human contributions inside systems designed to summarize them. Still, people living inside the system have to decide where to spend their time, and visibility becomes one of the facts they take into account.

When people respond to these incentives, leaders may become frustrated. Why are engineers avoiding the difficult bugs? Why is everyone focused on short-term deliverables? Why are teams reluctant to take risks when innovation is one of our stated values? Those are reasonable questions, but another question may be even more useful: *What behavior have we made rational?*

That question shifts our attention away from individual character and toward the environment in which choices are being made. If taking a difficult bug creates three weeks of red dashboard entries while closing five easy bugs creates visible evidence of productivity, we should not be astonished when people notice the difference. If experimentation is celebrated in speeches but failed experiments require defensive explanations, employees will eventually develop a sophisticated understanding of which value has more practical authority. Human beings are excellent students of consequences.

This does not remove personal responsibility. People can behave courageously inside imperfect systems, and they can also behave badly inside good ones. But an organization that depends entirely on everyone resisting its incentives has designed a very fragile system. If we want different behavior, it is worth understanding what the current environment is teaching.

## The Limits of Control

The natural response to all of this is to design better metrics. If bug count creates the wrong incentive, perhaps we should measure defect severity. If call duration distorts customer service, perhaps we should add customer satisfaction. If code output misses mentoring and prevention, perhaps we need a more comprehensive model of engineering contribution.

Sometimes that is exactly the right answer. Measurements can be improved, incentives can be redesigned, and badly chosen targets can be replaced with better ones. We should not turn every difficulty into a philosophical mystery when a corrected spreadsheet would solve the problem. Good management includes learning from unintended consequences and adjusting the system.

But notice what begins happening as we add measurements. One number becomes three, three become seven, and eventually we have constructed a dashboard elaborate enough to reassure ourselves that no important dimension has escaped. Then people learn the new dashboard. They discover which measures matter most, which ones compensate for others, and what kinds of explanations are accepted when the overall picture is questioned.

The system has become recursive. I measure your behavior, you notice what I am measuring and adjust, I observe the adjustment and modify the measurement, and you notice what I noticed and adjust again. Each iteration can genuinely improve our understanding, but none gives us a position completely outside the system. The people doing the measuring are part of the environment experienced by the people being measured.

This is different from optimizing a machine. When I profile software, the software does not develop a theory about why I am profiling it. It does not worry that a slow function will disappoint me, hide work that is difficult to quantify, or become resentful because another function received more recognition. Human systems contain people interpreting one another, and those interpretations become causes in their own right.

That doesn't mean human systems are unknowable or that measurement is futile. Organizations learn, improve, and coordinate through data every day. It means only that the dream of finding the perfect set of metrics that will make a human organization behave predictably may ask more of measurement than measurement can provide. Better instruments can improve our view without giving us complete control over what we see.

There is an important humility in recognizing this. The manager does not have to choose between blindly trusting the dashboard and throwing it away. The engineer does not have to choose between accepting every metric as truth and dismissing all management information as bureaucracy. Both can begin with the possibility that the number is telling the truth about something while leaving important parts of the story outside the frame.

That possibility takes us back to the map. Maps are useful because they leave things out, and organizations could not function without them. But once human beings begin responding to the map, the relationship between representation and reality becomes more complicated. The map changes how people move through the territory, and their movement changes what the next version of the map will show.

We still need the map.

The harder question is how much of the territory disappears when we rise high enough to see the whole thing.
