# 3 — When the Territory Notices the Map

There is something wonderfully uncomplicated about measuring a machine. If a processor is running too hot, it does not become embarrassed when you display the temperature at a staff meeting. If a piece of software takes too long to complete an operation, it does not choose an easier operation next time so its performance review will go better. Machines may be complicated, but at least they are indifferent to our opinions of them. People have the inconvenient habit of noticing when they are being watched.

That changes measurement in a fundamental way. When we measure a physical object, the measurement generally stands outside the system we are trying to understand. When we measure people, the measurement can enter the system and become one of the forces shaping what happens next. People learn what is being counted, what gets discussed, which numbers produce uncomfortable questions, and which accomplishments receive praise.

Then, quite reasonably, they adjust.

Organizations sometimes describe this adjustment as *gaming the metric*, which makes it sound as though employees have violated an otherwise pristine measurement system. Sometimes people certainly manipulate numbers dishonestly, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise. More often, though, the response is subtler and much more human. People simply pay attention to what their environment rewards, punishes, celebrates, and questions.

This is one of the strange properties of human systems. A metric can begin as an attempt to observe reality and end up helping create the reality it observes. The number is still measuring something real, but now it is also participating in what happens next. The territory has noticed the map.

## The Territory Notices

Imagine an engineering organization that wants bugs resolved more quickly. This is a perfectly reasonable objective because defects that remain open can delay products, consume engineering time, and leave customers waiting. Someone creates a dashboard showing how long bugs have been open, perhaps grouped by severity or component with a useful trend line showing whether things are improving. Nobody is trying to create a dystopian workplace. At first, it is simply information.

Then someone notices a cluster of old bugs and asks about them. The question travels downward through the organization, perhaps from a director to a manager and then to the engineers responsible for the work. Nobody is angry, nobody threatens anyone's career, and there may not even be a suggestion that something has gone wrong. A leader saw something interesting on a dashboard and did exactly what leaders are supposed to do: asked what was happening.

The engineer experiences the exchange from a different position. From the director's perspective, *Why are these bugs taking longer?* may be simple curiosity. From the engineer's perspective, the same question has traveled through a hierarchy that influences assignments, performance ratings, promotions, and future opportunities. The engineer does not need to be paranoid to notice that old bugs attract attention and attention eventually requires explanation.

Next week, that engineer has a choice. One bug looks straightforward and will probably be closed by Friday, while another involves a subtle interaction between several components and may take weeks just to reproduce reliably. The complicated bug might be more important, and perhaps the engineer chooses it anyway. But the dashboard is now one of the facts entering the decision.

Nobody issued a bad instruction. The director looked at useful information, the manager followed up on a reasonable question, and the engineer learned something from the interaction. Every person involved may have behaved exactly as we would want them to behave. The interesting thing is what the system communicated without anyone intending to communicate it.

This is where the distinction between the measurer and the measured becomes important. The person looking at the dashboard may experience a metric as information: *Something unusual is happening here, and I'd like to understand it.* The person represented on the dashboard may experience exactly the same metric as evaluation: *Something unusual is happening here, and I may have to justify it.* The number hasn't changed. What changed is where the person is standing.

## When Visibility Acquires Consequences

Metrics rarely become powerful because someone announces that everyone must now worship the dashboard. Their authority develops gradually, usually through perfectly ordinary organizational routines. A team begins tracking something because it would be useful to know, the number appears in a report, the report becomes part of a monthly review, and eventually people notice that certain numbers receive more attention than others. What began as visibility slowly acquires consequences.

Those consequences do not have to be formal. Perhaps nobody's bonus is tied to bug duration, but a manager whose team consistently appears red may find themselves answering the same question every month. The manager naturally asks the team to focus on the old bugs, and engineers who want to help their manager naturally respond. The organization has created an incentive without ever writing one down.

This happens because hierarchy gives curiosity weight. When a coworker asks why something happened, I may hear a question; when someone several levels above me asks exactly the same thing, I may hear a request for justification. Leaders can underestimate this because they experience themselves as ordinary people asking reasonable questions. The employee answering is also aware that this particular ordinary person can influence what happens to their career.

That does not mean leaders should stop asking questions. An organization where senior people are afraid to inquire about anything would be a peculiar improvement, and avoiding uncomfortable information is hardly the answer to misusing it. It means that questions travel through relationships involving authority, trust, history, and expectation. Those relationships become part of what the question means.

This is why a metric's official purpose tells us less than we might think. We can say that a dashboard exists “for visibility” or “to start conversations,” and we may sincerely mean it. The more revealing question is what happens after the conversation starts. If every outlier eventually produces pressure to move the number, people will learn that lesson regardless of what the dashboard's introductory slide says.

Eventually an organizational vocabulary develops that nobody formally designed. Green means safe, red means attention, and an outlier means somebody may have to explain themselves. Improvement brings relief. Human beings are remarkably good at discovering the social meaning hidden inside a colored box.

## The Number Becomes the Target

There is a well-known idea usually summarized as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it tends to become less useful as a measure. We choose a number because it corresponds with something we care about, then begin managing the number as though it were the thing we care about. Most of the time the two continue moving together, which is why the metric remains useful. The trouble appears in the cases where they separate.

Imagine a customer-service organization that wants people to receive help quickly. Someone notices that very long calls often correspond with difficult customer experiences, so the organization begins tracking average call duration. A target of eight minutes seems reasonable, and initially it may expose inefficient processes or places where representatives need better tools. Then employees learn that calls longer than eight minutes create questions.

The original goal was to help the customer. The operational goal has quietly become finishing the call. Those goals overlap much of the time, so an employee can satisfy both without noticing any tension. The interesting cases are the ones where helping the customer requires eleven minutes.

Now the representative has a decision to make. They can continue troubleshooting, rush the explanation, transfer the customer, or solve the immediate problem without exploring the underlying issue that may cause another call tomorrow. None of those choices requires the employee to be lazy or malicious. If the organization repeatedly communicates that eight minutes represents success, employees will naturally learn what eight-minute success looks like.

This suggests a useful question whenever we create a performance measure: **How could someone make this number look fantastic without actually improving the thing I care about?** The question isn't an accusation against the people being measured. It is a way of stress-testing the distance between the metric and its purpose. If we can imagine several perfectly rational ways to improve the score while making the underlying work worse, we have learned something important about the measure.

The danger grows as the underlying purpose becomes more complicated. Software quality is not one thing, customer service is not one thing, and employee contribution is certainly not one thing. We select a measurable feature because we need a practical way to see what is happening. Once consequences attach to it, however, that feature can become disproportionately important simply because everyone knows how to see it.

## Rational People in Irrational Systems

Consider two experienced engineers working on the same product. One writes code quickly, introduces several bugs, and then fixes those bugs quickly, producing a steady stream of visible activity. Another spends more time understanding the architecture, catches problems before they escape, mentors a less experienced teammate, and takes ownership of a subtle defect that requires three weeks to reproduce. Both may be working hard and doing valuable things, but their work leaves very different footprints.

The first engineer produces artifacts the organization already knows how to count. Features are completed, bugs are filed, bugs are fixed, and each event appears somewhere in a system with a date attached to it. The second engineer may prevent a defect that would have consumed weeks of work, but the prevented defect never appears in the database. Prevention has terrible marketing.

Mentoring has a similar problem. An experienced engineer can spend an hour helping someone understand a system well enough to avoid weeks of future confusion, and the resulting benefit may eventually appear as somebody else's productivity. Perhaps it never becomes visible at all. If the mentor had spent the hour closing another bug, the accomplishment would have been much easier to count.

This does not mean the organization secretly values bugs more than prevention or individual output more than mentoring. It may explicitly say the opposite, and the people saying it may mean every word. The problem is that some forms of contribution naturally leave more measurable evidence than others. Once visibility carries consequences, that difference matters.

An engineer living inside this system eventually has to decide where to spend time. Should I take the messy bug that may consume three weeks, or close several problems I know I can finish? Should I spend the afternoon helping a teammate understand the architecture, or complete the task attached to my own name? People do not need to sit at their desks calculating these tradeoffs cynically for the environment to influence them.

This is why I am reluctant to describe these situations primarily as failures of character. Managers usually have legitimate reasons for wanting visibility, employees have legitimate reasons for responding to incentives, and executives have legitimate reasons for asking why an important number is moving in the wrong direction. Dysfunction can emerge even when nearly everyone involved is behaving rationally. A system can produce an outcome nobody explicitly wanted.

When that happens, leaders may become frustrated. Why are engineers avoiding the difficult bugs, why are teams focused on short-term deliverables, or why does everyone seem reluctant to take risks when innovation appears prominently among the company's values? Those are reasonable questions. Another one may be even more useful: **What behavior have we made rational?**

That question changes the conversation. Instead of beginning with the assumption that people have failed to understand the organization's values, we become curious about what they have learned from its consequences. If experimentation is celebrated in speeches but failed experiments reliably produce defensive reviews, employees will eventually understand which message carries more practical authority. Human beings are excellent students of consequences.

This does not eliminate personal responsibility. People can behave courageously inside imperfect systems, just as they can behave badly inside well-designed ones. But an organization that depends upon everyone continually resisting the incentives around them has created a fragile arrangement. If we want different behavior, it is worth understanding what the current environment is teaching.

## The Limits of Better Metrics

The natural response is to fix the measurement. If bug duration creates the wrong incentive, perhaps we should account for severity or complexity; if call duration distorts customer service, perhaps we should add customer satisfaction. If visible output misses mentoring and prevention, perhaps we need a more comprehensive model of engineering contribution. Sometimes this is exactly the right answer.

I don't want to turn every measurement problem into a philosophical mystery when a corrected spreadsheet would solve it. Bad metrics can be replaced, incentives can be redesigned, and unintended consequences can teach us how to build better systems. This is the feedback loop doing what it is supposed to do. We observe the problem and adjust.

But there is a temptation hiding inside the adjustment. One number becomes three, three become seven, and eventually we construct a dashboard elaborate enough to reassure ourselves that no important dimension has escaped. Then the people inside the system learn the new dashboard. They discover which measures matter most, which ones compensate for others, and what kinds of explanations are accepted when the overall picture is questioned.

The system has become recursive. I measure your behavior, you notice what I am measuring and adjust, I observe the adjustment and modify the measurement, and you notice what I noticed and adjust again. Each iteration may genuinely improve our understanding. None gives us a position completely outside the human system we are trying to understand.

That is the limit worth noticing here. It does not mean measurement has failed, and it certainly does not mean organizations should stop using data. It means that measuring people is partly a relationship with them. The act of observing becomes one of the conditions under which they act.

This calls for something more modest than either blind faith in metrics or cynical rejection of them. A manager can look at a number and ask what is happening without assuming the number has already explained it. An employee can recognize that a metric reveals something real without accepting that it contains the entire story of their work. Both can treat the measurement as the beginning of inquiry rather than the end of judgment.

That may be the most useful change in posture available to us. The question shifts from *How do we make this number move?* to *What is this number helping us notice?* Sometimes the answer will lead directly to action. Sometimes it will lead to another question.

Either way, the dashboard has done something important.

It has shown us where to look.

The harder problem is deciding what we see when we get there.
