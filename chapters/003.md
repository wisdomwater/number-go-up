# Chapter 3: The Dashboard Is Not the World

## You Cannot Manage What You Can’t See

Let's begin with a defense of dashboards.

They are incredibly useful.

This may seem like an odd place to start immediately after spending an entire chapter explaining all the strange things that can happen when human beings discover which numbers are being watched.

But organizations have a problem.

They're big.

Even relatively small organizations are too complicated for any one person to understand completely. Once you have hundreds, thousands, or tens of thousands of people doing different kinds of work, the idea that a leader could simply understand what's happening by paying closer attention becomes absurd.

Imagine the CEO of a company with fifty thousand employees deciding that dashboards are dangerous abstractions.

No more metrics.

No more reports.

From now on, she is going to understand the business by talking directly to the people doing the work.

This sounds wonderfully human.

On Monday morning she sits down with an engineer.

“What are you working on?”

The engineer explains.

Forty-five minutes later, the CEO has learned quite a bit about a problem involving memory allocation that she did not know existed.

Excellent.

Only 49,999 employees to go.

By lunch, the limitations of the strategy have become apparent.

The CEO doesn't need to understand every memory allocation problem.

She needs to understand whether the engineering organization can deliver the products the company has promised.

She doesn't need to listen to every customer service call. She needs to know whether customers are generally getting their problems resolved.

She doesn't need to inspect every invoice. She needs to know whether the company is financially healthy.

She doesn't need to sit in every hiring interview. She needs to know whether the organization can attract and retain the people it needs.

To do that, reality has to be compressed.

Thousands of individual events become categories.

Categories become counts.

Counts become trends.

Trends become charts.

Charts become dashboards.

By the time information reaches senior leadership, enormous amounts of detail have disappeared.

This isn't necessarily a failure.

It's the point.

Abstraction allows us to think about things that would otherwise overwhelm us.

We do this constantly.

Consider a map.

A road map does not contain every tree, mailbox, pothole, driveway, dog, pedestrian, storm drain, and suspiciously aggressive goose you might encounter on your journey.

Thank God.

If it did, the map would become almost as difficult to navigate as the world itself.

The map is useful precisely because it leaves things out.

A dashboard works the same way.

A manager overseeing twenty projects probably doesn't need to know every decision made on every project. She needs a representation that allows her to see which projects are progressing, which are stuck, which require additional resources, and which appear to be wandering quietly toward disaster.

So we create abstractions.

Green. Yellow. Red.

On track. At risk. Behind.

Revenue. Cost.

Defects.

Customer satisfaction.

Headcount.

Schedule.

Each one compresses a complicated reality into something a human mind can hold.

And something important happens when we do this.

We gain perspective.

The engineer working inside a particular component may understand that component better than anyone else in the company. She knows its history. She knows why the ugly piece of code everyone complains about is still there. She knows which dependency breaks every third Tuesday for reasons nobody has completely understood.

That knowledge is valuable.

But she may not know that five other teams are encountering similar problems.

A manager looking across those teams might.

The manager loses detail but gains pattern.

One team misses a deadline.

That may mean almost anything.

Six teams miss deadlines for the same dependency.

Now we may have discovered something.

One customer complains.

Maybe the customer had an unusual problem.

Two hundred customers complain about the same thing.

That's a pattern.

One engineer says a process is wasting time.

Perhaps she's right.

Perhaps she simply hates filling out forms.

When engineers across twelve teams are all saying the same thing, the abstraction begins revealing something the individual experience couldn't.

This is one of the great powers of measurement. 

t allows us to see the forest.

The cliché exists for a reason.

When you're standing next to a tree, you can see things about that tree that nobody looking down from a helicopter will ever see. You can inspect the bark. You can see where a branch is damaged. You can notice insects eating the leaves.

From the helicopter, most of that disappears.

But something else becomes visible.

The forest.

You can see where it begins and ends. You can see where growth is dense and where it has thinned. You can see a fire spreading three miles away from the person examining the bark.

Neither perspective contains the whole truth.

The ground reveals detail.

Altitude reveals pattern.

Organizations need both.

This is why I don't think the conflict between management and the people doing the work can be reduced to a simple story where management lives in spreadsheets while enlightened frontline employees understand reality.

Sometimes the spreadsheet sees something the frontline employee can't.

Sometimes the abstraction is exactly what we need.

The trouble begins when we forget what an abstraction is.

A dashboard isn't reality.

It's reality after we've decided which parts to keep.

That doesn't make it false.

It makes it useful.

But it also means that every time we move upward—from work to category, from category to metric, from metric to dashboard—we are making choices about what disappears.

And eventually we have to ask whether the things that disappeared were things we could afford to lose.

## The View From 30,000 Feet

The ground reveals detail.

Altitude reveals pattern.

Those aren't competing forms of knowledge. They're complementary.

Imagine an engineering team has been struggling to deliver a project.

Ask the engineers what's happening and you'll probably get a detailed answer.

A dependency changed unexpectedly. The original requirement underestimated the complexity of a particular feature. A key engineer was pulled onto another project. Testing uncovered an architectural problem nobody anticipated.

All of that matters.

Now imagine you're the manager responsible for ten teams.

You notice something the engineers on this particular team can't easily see.

Seven of your ten teams are behind schedule.

That's different.

One team being late probably requires understanding the team.

Seven teams being late probably requires understanding the system.

Maybe they're all depending on the same overloaded infrastructure group.

Maybe requirements are consistently arriving before they're ready.

Maybe the organization has committed to more work than it has people to perform.

Maybe a process introduced six months ago is adding far more overhead than anyone expected.

Maybe the company has developed the fascinating strategic practice of making everything the highest priority.

The engineer inside one team may not be able to see any of this. She's trying to solve Tuesday.

The manager is trying to understand why everyone seems to be having the same Tuesday.

That's what altitude can give us. It allows individual experiences to become patterns. But the pattern alone doesn't explain itself.

Suppose the dashboard shows that seven teams are behind schedule.

What should management conclude?

Perhaps the teams aren't executing well.

Perhaps the schedules were unrealistic.

Perhaps staffing is inadequate.

Perhaps some external dependency has become a bottleneck.

Perhaps the work is genuinely harder than expected.

The dashboard can reveal that something is happening without necessarily revealing what that something means.

This is where the ground becomes valuable again.

The manager sees the pattern. Then she goes looking for context.

She talks to the teams.

One says requirements keep changing.

Another says the approval process has become painfully slow.

A third says the same infrastructure team is blocking them.

A fourth mentions that two experienced engineers left and were never replaced.

Now the abstraction and the lived experience begin talking to each other.

The dashboard says:

**Something is happening across the system.**

The people say:

**Here's what it looks like from inside.**

Together, those two perspectives can produce something neither could produce alone.

Understanding.

This seems obvious when written down. It is considerably less obvious inside an organization.

There is a natural tendency for people at different levels to distrust one another's perspective.

People doing the work can look upward and think:

**They have no idea how this actually works.**

Sometimes they're right.

Managers can look downward and think:

**They're too close to the problem to see the larger picture.**

Sometimes they're right too.

The mistake is assuming that because the other person can't see what you see, they must not understand what's happening.

They may understand a different part of what's happening.

Consider our seven delayed teams.

The engineer knows that changing one particular interface will probably cost her team three weeks.

Her manager may not know what the interface does.

But the manager knows that six other teams are making similar changes because of a decision made somewhere else in the organization.

The engineer knows the local cost.

The manager sees the systemic cause.

If they argue over whose knowledge is more legitimate, they can waste a great deal of time being correct at each other.

If they combine what they know, they might actually solve the problem.

This is one of the reasons healthy organizations need information to travel in both directions.

We tend to think of organizational information as something that moves upward.

Employees report to managers.

Managers report to directors.

Directors report to executives.

Work becomes status.

Status becomes metrics.

Metrics become dashboards.

Information rises and becomes increasingly compressed.

But something also needs to move downward.

Context.

Why are we measuring this?

What pattern are we seeing?

What problem are we trying to solve?

What constraints exist outside this team's view?

Why does this deadline matter?

Why did leadership make this tradeoff?

Without that downward flow, people on the ground experience decisions without understanding the larger system that produced them.

And something else has to move upward too.

Not just data.

**Meaning.**

Why did the number change?

What is the metric missing?

Which cases don't fit neatly into the category?

What are people seeing that hasn't reached the dashboard yet?

What looks simple from above but becomes complicated when someone actually tries to do it?

The dashboard needs the ground to interpret it. The ground needs the dashboard to place itself in context.

This creates a kind of organizational stereoscopic vision. Close one eye and you still see something. You may even see it clearly.

But you lose depth.

The same thing happens when organizations rely too heavily on either abstraction or local experience.

Manage entirely from the dashboard and you may see patterns without understanding what produces them.

Manage entirely from individual experience and you may understand dozens of situations deeply without realizing they're all manifestations of the same problem.

One gives breadth.

The other gives depth.

Good judgment comes from moving between them.

This means the manager's job isn't to choose between the spreadsheet and the engineer. It's to let them correct each other.

If the dashboard says everything is fine and the people doing the work say everything is on fire, investigate.

If one team says the entire organization is broken but the broader data shows everyone else functioning normally, investigate that too.

Neither perspective gets automatic authority.

Both are evidence.

And when they disagree, the disagreement itself is information. Something doesn't fit.

Maybe the metric is missing something.

Maybe the local experience is unusual.

Maybe we're averaging together situations that shouldn't be averaged.

Maybe the people doing the work don't have context that changes how the problem looks.

Whatever the explanation, the tension is useful.

We don't have to decide whether the person standing beside the tree or the person looking down at the forest is the one who sees reality.

They both do.

They just see different parts of it.

The challenge is keeping them in conversation long enough to see the whole.

## Compression Is the Point

Every abstraction is an act of omission.

This is not a bug. It's what makes abstraction possible.

If I ask you how your day went, you probably won't begin:

“At 6:42 AM, I opened my eyes.”

Then proceed minute by minute until the present moment.

You compress.

“Pretty good. Work was frustrating, but I had a nice lunch with a friend.”

An entire day disappears into a sentence.

Thousands of events are gone.

The shower.

The commute.

The email you deleted without reading.

The meeting that could have been an email.

The email that somehow became a meeting.

All of it vanished.

And that's fine. You weren't asked to provide a forensic reconstruction of Tuesday. You were asked how your day went. The abstraction was appropriate to the question.

Organizations do the same thing.

A project contains thousands of individual decisions, conversations, problems, discoveries, mistakes, corrections, and compromises. Eventually someone asks:

“How's the project going?”

And all of that becomes:

**Yellow.**

There is something almost comical about this.

Six months of human effort.

Thirty-seven people.

Hundreds of meetings.

Thousands of lines of code.

Three architectural revisions.

A vendor problem.

Two staffing changes.

One spectacularly confusing requirement.

And somewhere in a PowerPoint deck:

Yellow.

But that's not necessarily ridiculous.

The executive looking at twenty projects may not need the story.

At least not yet.

She needs to know which projects deserve her attention.

That's what the yellow dot is for.

It isn't supposed to contain the project.

It's supposed to help her decide where to look.

This gives us a useful way to think about abstraction.

The question isn't:

**Did we lose information?**

Of course we did.

The question is:

**Did we lose information we needed?**

And the answer depends entirely on what we're trying to do.

If I'm deciding which of twenty projects needs attention, green-yellow-red might be perfectly adequate.

If I'm deciding whether to cancel the yellow project, it probably isn't.

If I'm deciding whether the project manager is performing well, I certainly hope we have more.

The same abstraction can be perfectly appropriate for one decision and dangerously inadequate for another.

This is easy to forget because once information has been compressed, the compressed version tends to travel.

The engineer explains the situation to her manager.

The manager summarizes it for a director.

The director puts it into a portfolio review.

The portfolio review becomes a dashboard.

The dashboard appears in an executive meeting.

At every stage, someone removes details that don't seem necessary for the next audience.

This has to happen. Otherwise the CEO eventually receives a 14,000-page weekly status report and spends Monday through Thursday reading about memory allocation.

But compression has an interesting property. It's much easier to remove context than to put it back.

Imagine the engineer originally says:

“We're about three weeks behind because the customer changed a major requirement after implementation started. We've adjusted the design, and we're confident in the new plan, but the original delivery date is no longer realistic.”

That becomes:

**Project delayed three weeks.**

Then:

**Schedule: Yellow.**

Nothing in the final statement is necessarily false. The project really is delayed. The schedule really may deserve to be yellow.

But notice what disappeared.

Why the delay happened.

Whether anyone could have prevented it.

Whether the team is now in control of the situation.

Whether the original schedule was still reasonable after the requirement changed.

Those details may not matter if we're simply trying to identify which projects are late.

They matter enormously if someone later asks:

**Why can't this team deliver on time?**

The abstraction that was perfectly useful for one purpose has quietly been recruited for another.

This is where dashboards can become dangerous. Not because they are simplified. They are supposed to be simplified.

The danger comes when we forget **what the simplification was designed to do**.

A weather app might tell me it's 94 degrees outside. That's useful if I'm deciding whether to wear a jacket. It tells me considerably less about whether a wildfire is likely to spread through a particular canyon.

Same weather.

Different question.

Different information required.

Metrics work the same way. Customer satisfaction might be useful for identifying broad changes in customer sentiment. It may be terrible for deciding whether a particular employee handled a particular customer correctly.

Average delivery time may tell us whether an organization is becoming faster overall. It may tell us very little about why one complicated project took twice as long as another.

Employee turnover may reveal that something deserves attention. It cannot tell us, by itself, whether people are leaving because of compensation, management, workload, career opportunities, retirement, organizational change, or an unusually charismatic competitor handing out free tacos.

The number isn't failing. We're asking it a question it was never designed to answer.

This suggests a simple rule:

**Match the resolution of the information to the resolution of the decision.**

Broad decisions can often tolerate broad abstractions. Specific decisions usually require specific context.

If I'm deciding where to investigate, the dashboard may be enough. If I'm deciding why something happened, I probably need to get closer. If I'm making a consequential judgment about a person, a team, or a particular event, I should probably get closer still.

The more specific and consequential the decision becomes, the more dangerous it is to rely entirely on compressed information.

This is especially important because abstraction creates psychological distance.

“Seven projects are behind schedule” feels like a management problem.

“Maria's team has been working nights for six weeks because a dependency changed after they committed to the schedule” feels different.

The first invites analysis.

The second invites understanding.

We need both.

An organization cannot operate entirely through stories. Individual stories are powerful, but they can also mislead. The most vivid experience isn't necessarily the most representative one. The angriest customer doesn't necessarily represent the customer base. The loudest employee doesn't necessarily represent the team.

That's why we aggregate. That's why we measure. That's why we compress.

But aggregation creates its own blindness. The average smooths the outlier. The category removes the story. The status color removes the cause. The dashboard removes the people.

Again, none of this means we should stop abstracting. It means we should remember what abstraction costs.

Every level of compression trades detail for perspective. Sometimes that's exactly the trade we need.

Sometimes it isn't.

The skill is knowing when the compressed version is enough and when we need to go back toward the source.

A yellow dot can tell us where to look. It should not always tell us what to think.

And the more important the decision becomes, the more willing we should be to leave the dashboard behind and find out what that yellow dot is actually made of.
