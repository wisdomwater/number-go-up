# Chapter 10 — The Things We Cannot Measure

## Eventually the Numbers Run Out of Language

Let's start with a song.

I play music, so this is familiar territory for me.

A song is wonderfully measurable.

It has a duration.

A tempo.

A key.

Frequencies.

Amplitudes.

Rhythms.

Intervals.

We can analyze the waveform.

We can identify every note.

We can measure exactly when each note begins and ends.

We can examine the harmonics produced by the instruments and the acoustic properties of the room in which they were recorded.

If the song is released commercially, we can measure even more.

Streams.

Downloads.

Sales.

Chart position.

Radio plays.

Likes.

Shares.

Skips.

How many people listened to the first thirty seconds.

How many finished the song.

How many came back and listened again.

If we really wanted to get ambitious, we could put sensors on someone while they listened.

Heart rate.

Breathing.

Skin conductance.

Brain activity.

We could ask them questions afterward and turn their answers into data.

Modern science and technology can tell us an astonishing amount about what happens when a human being listens to music.

And then I can ask a very simple question.

**Was it beautiful?**

Suddenly the numbers become less talkative.

They don't become useless.

That's an important distinction.

Maybe millions of people listened to the song repeatedly. That tells us something.

Maybe listeners consistently report being moved by it.

That tells us something too.

Maybe certain musical structures reliably produce particular emotional responses.

Interesting.

Study them.

Measure them.

Learn everything we can.

But none of those measurements quite answers the question I asked.

Was it beautiful?

Or an even more personal question:

**What did it feel like to hear it?**

Maybe you've had this experience.

A song you've heard a hundred times comes on at exactly the right moment.

Nothing about the recording has changed.

Same notes.

Same tempo.

Same singer.

Same waveform.

But something about you has changed.

Maybe someone you love has died.

Maybe you've fallen in love.

Maybe you're driving home after receiving news you don't know how to process.

Maybe a lyric that had always been background suddenly seems to be speaking directly to you.

And for reasons you cannot fully explain, you're crying in the car.

What happened?

We can measure parts of it.

Tears are measurable.

Heart rate is measurable.

The acoustic signal is measurable.

If we knew enough about the brain, perhaps we could describe an extraordinary amount of neural activity taking place while the song played.

All of that would be real.

None of it would be the crying in the car.

This is the territory we've been approaching throughout this book.

There is reality.

And there are representations of reality.

The representation can be extraordinarily precise.

It can reveal things we could never discover through intuition alone.

It can correct us.

It can surprise us.

It can help us understand what happened.

But the representation and the experience are not identical.

This isn't an argument against science.

Quite the opposite.

One of the great achievements of science is learning how to isolate aspects of reality so we can examine them carefully.

We measure.

Compare.

Test.

Repeat.

We strip away ambiguity wherever we can.

And it works spectacularly well.

The mistake comes afterward.

Because measurement is so powerful, we can begin to assume that whatever is most precisely measurable must also be the most complete description of what is real.

We've encountered this assumption everywhere.

A performance rating is precise.

A career is not.

Weight is precise.

Health is not.

Attendance is precise.

Transformation is not.

Revenue is precise.

Organizational value is not.

The measurable thing feels solid.

The larger reality feels fuzzy.

So we give the solid thing more authority.

But perhaps we've confused two different qualities.

**Precision is not completeness.**

A song can be represented digitally with extraordinary precision.

That representation is good enough to reproduce the sound in my headphones.

But nowhere in the file is the meaning the song has for me.

There isn't a field containing:

**This was playing during the summer when everything changed.**

There is no metadata tag for:

**This reminds me of my father.**

No bit rate for longing.

No frequency for grief.

No amplitude for beauty.

Those things appear when the music encounters a human life.

And that human life brings context.

Memory.

Relationship.

History.

Expectation.

Loss.

Hope.

The song is measurable.

The encounter is something more.

This is why some of the most important questions we ask sound strangely resistant to numbers.

Was it beautiful?

Did it move you?

Do you trust her?

Have you forgiven him?

What does this mean to you?

Are you happy?

Do you love them?

Was the suffering worth it?

Has this changed you?

We can gather evidence around every one of those questions.

Sometimes quite a lot of evidence.

But eventually we reach a point where another decimal place doesn't get us any closer to the thing we're trying to understand.

The numbers have told us what they know.

Now the question has moved somewhere else.

This does not mean we've left reality behind.

It may mean we've encountered a part of reality that has to be approached differently.

There is an old philosophical problem hiding here.

What is the difference between describing an experience from the outside and actually having the experience from the inside?

What is it like to see red?

To taste coffee?

To feel pain?

To hear a song and suddenly find yourself crying?

Philosophers have spent a great deal of time arguing about these questions.

They even have a word for this first-person quality of experience.

**Qualia.**

I have no intention of solving the philosophy of consciousness in a book that began with corporate dashboards.

But qualia gives us language for something we've been bumping into from the beginning.

There is a view from outside.

And there is a view from inside.

Both are real.

But they are not the same view.

Eventually, the numbers run out of language.

The experience keeps going.

## The View From Inside

There is a famous thought experiment in philosophy involving a scientist named Mary.

Mary knows everything there is to know about the science of color vision.

She understands light.

She understands wavelengths.

She understands the eye.

She understands the brain.

She knows exactly what happens when light with a wavelength we call red reaches the retina and the nervous system begins processing it.

There is only one problem.

Mary has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room.

She has never actually seen red.

Then one day she leaves the room.

She sees a red apple.

And philosophers ask:

**Did Mary learn something new?**

Did she discover what red looks like?

This is known as the knowledge argument, and philosophers have been arguing about what it proves for decades.

I am going to do something unusual for an author and resist the temptation to settle it.

You're welcome.

The debate touches some enormous questions about consciousness, the brain, and whether a complete physical description of reality would contain everything there is to know about experience.

Those are fascinating questions.

They are also considerably above the pay grade of a book called *Number Go Up*.

I'm interested in something simpler.

There is an obvious difference between reading a description of an experience and having the experience.

You can study coffee.

You can learn its chemical composition.

You can measure its temperature.

You can understand how roasting changes flavor compounds.

You can map what happens in the brain when someone drinks it.

You can read thousands of tasting notes written by people who take coffee considerably more seriously than I do.

And none of those things is quite the same as taking a sip.

The sip gives you something from the inside.

Philosophers use the word **qualia** for these subjective qualities of experience.

The redness of red.

The bitterness of coffee.

The sharpness of pain.

The feeling of warmth.

The sound of a trumpet.

The experience of hearing a familiar voice.

What is it like?

That question is strange because the answer often seems impossible to fully transfer.

Try describing red to someone who has never seen.

Or the taste of chocolate to someone who has never tasted it.

Or pain to someone who has never felt pain.

Eventually language starts pointing.

**It's like...**

And then we reach for another experience.

It's like this.

Except different.

We are trying to use one person's experience to build a bridge toward another person's.

Sometimes it works surprisingly well.

Sometimes it doesn't.

This distinction matters for our discussion of measurement because measurement generally gives us a view from outside.

We observe.

Record.

Count.

Compare.

Represent.

We turn some aspect of reality into information that can travel.

That's one of measurement's greatest powers.

I don't have to personally stand inside a furnace to know its temperature.

In fact, I would strongly prefer not to.

An instrument can experience the relevant part of the furnace for me and return a number.

Now we can share the information.

Compare furnaces.

Set thresholds.

Build control systems.

Make decisions.

The number travels remarkably well.

First-person experience does not.

If I tell you my pain is a seven, I've compressed something intensely private into a number.

That may be useful.

A doctor can work with seven.

Seven can go in a chart.

Seven can be compared with the four I reported yesterday.

But you still don't feel my seven.

You know something about my pain.

You do not have my pain.

And even the number itself depends on interpretation.

Is my seven the same as your seven?

Was yesterday's four really a four?

What does seven mean to someone who has lived with pain for twenty years compared with someone experiencing severe pain for the first time?

The metric creates a bridge.

It does not eliminate the distance.

We've been seeing versions of this throughout the book.

A manager sees a performance rating.

The employee experiences the year.

The organization sees headcount.

The people counted experience careers.

A health tracker sees hours of sleep.

I experience waking up exhausted.

A church sees attendance.

The person in the chair experiences whatever happened inside them while they were there.

The view from outside isn't false.

Neither is the view from inside.

They answer different questions.

This is especially important because large systems cannot operate entirely from the inside.

An executive cannot personally experience the workday of fifty thousand employees.

A government cannot know every citizen intimately.

A hospital administrator cannot inhabit every patient's pain.

At scale, we need representations.

We need categories.

We need summaries.

We need numbers.

We need maps.

The problem begins when the representation acquires so much authority that the experience it represents becomes secondary.

**The dashboard says you're doing fine.**

But I'm not doing fine.

**The survey says employees are engaged.**

But the people on my team are exhausted.

**Your numbers look healthy.**

But something feels wrong.

Neither side automatically wins.

Maybe my perception is mistaken.

Maybe the dashboard is catching something I cannot see.

Maybe the survey is more representative than the three frustrated coworkers I talked to.

Maybe the medical numbers are reassuring for good reason.

First-person experience is not infallible.

We are spectacularly capable of misunderstanding ourselves.

But neither is the representation complete.

The disagreement is information.

It tells us there is something worth investigating.

**What does the number see that the experience doesn't?**

And:

**What does the experience see that the number doesn't?**

Wisdom may require holding both questions at once.

This is where the problem of qualia becomes much more ordinary than philosophy seminars make it sound.

Every human organization lives with some version of the gap between outside and inside.

The organization sees a resource-allocation problem.

The manager sees a performance-management problem.

The employee experiences a life.

The hospital sees a case.

The patient experiences fear.

The school sees a test score.

The student experiences learning.

The church sees a person in a seat.

The person experiences hope, boredom, grief, belonging, doubt, peace, irritation, or perhaps an urgent desire for the service to end because they forgot to eat breakfast.

The external representation and the internal experience overlap.

But they never perfectly collapse into one another.

Perhaps that's one reason metrics become dangerous when we ask them to carry too much authority.

They are extraordinarily good at giving us a view from somewhere.

Then their precision tempts us to mistake that view for everywhere.

But there is always another perspective.

There is always more territory.

And when the territory is a human being, some of it can only be encountered from the inside.

That doesn't make measurement inadequate.

It makes measurement an instrument.

And every instrument, no matter how powerful, has a range.

## The Instrument Is Not the Reality

Imagine I step onto my bathroom scale.

It says 247 pounds.

Then I ask it whether I'm healthy.

The scale remains silent.

So I step off and back on.

247.

Apparently it didn't understand the question.

I try again.

Still 247.

This is not a design flaw.

The scale is doing exactly what it was built to do.

It measures weight.

It doesn't measure blood pressure.

It doesn't measure cardiovascular fitness.

It doesn't measure blood sugar.

It doesn't measure strength.

It doesn't measure whether I can walk up a flight of stairs without reconsidering my life choices.

And it certainly doesn't measure whether I'm happy.

The scale has a domain.

Every instrument does.

A thermometer measures temperature.

A ruler measures distance.

A clock measures time.

A speedometer measures speed.

We would never criticize a thermometer because it cannot tell us how long a table is.

We would criticize the person trying to measure the table with a thermometer.

The problem isn't the instrument.

It's the question.

This seems obvious when we're talking about physical instruments.

It becomes less obvious when the instrument is measurement itself.

Measurement has been so successful across so many domains that it can begin to feel less like an instrument and more like the language reality is ultimately written in.

If something is real, surely there must be some way to measure it.

Maybe.

There is an important distinction here.

We may be able to measure **something about** almost anything.

Love affects behavior.

Grief affects the body.

Music produces neurological responses.

Trust influences decisions.

Stress changes hormones.

Relationships create observable patterns.

Even experiences we think of as deeply subjective leave footprints in the physical world.

Those footprints can be studied.

They should be.

The mistake is assuming that because we can measure something about a reality, we have therefore measured the reality itself.

Suppose I want to measure a forest.

Easy.

How many trees?

How many acres?

Average rainfall?

Biomass?

Carbon storage?

Species diversity?

Soil composition?

Temperature?

Canopy density?

All measurable.

Now tell me the measurement of **the forest**.

There isn't one.

There are measurements of the forest.

Each reveals a dimension.

Each answers a question.

None is the forest.

The question always comes first.

**What are you trying to know?**

That determines the instrument.

This is precisely what we forget when metrics acquire authority.

A metric answers one question reasonably well, so we start letting it answer neighboring questions.

Then more distant questions.

Eventually the metric is wandering around the organization answering questions nobody asked it to answer.

Bugs closed becomes productivity.

Productivity becomes performance.

Performance becomes value.

Attendance becomes engagement.

Engagement becomes transformation.

Weight becomes health.

Health becomes discipline.

Income becomes success.

Followers become relevance.

The instrument keeps producing numbers.

We keep expanding the domain.

The number hasn't changed.

**Our claim about what the number means has.**

That distinction may be one of the simplest ways to protect ourselves from metric overreach.

Whenever a number starts carrying significant authority, ask:

**What question was this instrument actually designed to answer?**

Not:

**What questions could I possibly use it to answer?**

My scale was designed to answer:

**How much do you weigh?**

It was not designed to answer:

**Have you been good this week?**

I added that feature myself.

A performance rating might be designed to summarize how well someone performed against certain expectations during a particular period.

Already, that's a complicated task.

But it certainly wasn't designed to answer:

**Is this person intelligent?**

**Has their career mattered?**

**Are they worthy of respect?**

**Will they succeed somewhere else?**

**Are they enough?**

Those aren't additional columns we forgot to include.

They're different questions.

This matters because instruments don't usually announce their own limitations.

My scale doesn't display:

**247 pounds. Please note that no conclusions regarding character should be inferred from this measurement.**

Although perhaps it should.

The boundary has to come from us.

We have to remember what was measured.

We have to remember what wasn't.

We have to resist the seduction of precision.

Because precise answers are incredibly tempting when the real questions are ambiguous.

**What makes a good life?**

Difficult.

**What's your net worth?**

I can calculate that.

**Am I healthy?**

Complicated.

**What do I weigh?**

Step right up.

**Am I doing meaningful work?**

Could take years to answer.

**How many tasks did I complete this week?**

Finally, a question with a checkbox.

The danger isn't that the simpler questions are irrelevant.

Money matters.

Weight matters.

Tasks matter.

Performance matters.

The danger is allowing the questions we can answer precisely to displace the questions we actually care about.

This is why the order matters so much:

**Meaning → Measurement → Judgment.**

Meaning determines the question.

Measurement provides information.

Judgment decides what the information means in context.

If we begin with measurement, the available instruments start determining which questions we ask.

We stop asking:

**What matters?**

And start asking:

**What can we count?**

Those are not the same question.

Science understands this better than our dashboards sometimes do.

Good measurement requires defining what is being measured.

It requires knowing the limitations of the instrument.

It requires uncertainty.

Calibration.

Context.

Researchers don't generally point a sensor at the universe and announce that whatever the sensor detects is all that exists.

The discipline comes partly from knowing what the instrument cannot tell you.

We need the same humility in ordinary life.

Measurement is one of the most powerful instruments human beings have ever developed.

It lets us see patterns our senses miss.

It lets us compare across time and distance.

It lets reality challenge our intuitions.

It lets us build bridges, treat disease, understand stars, run organizations, and notice that I am consuming considerably more calories than I remember consuming.

Wonderful instrument.

Keep it.

Use it.

Trust it when it is answering the question it was designed to answer.

But don't confuse the instrument with the reality.

**The limitations of an instrument do not establish the limitations of reality.**

A thermometer cannot measure love.

That tells us nothing about whether love exists.

It tells us we brought the wrong instrument.

And some of the questions that matter most may not be asking for an instrument at all.

They may be asking for a person.

## Tell Me How Much You Love Your Wife

I love my wife.

I have no difficulty writing that sentence.

Now ask me how much.

The question immediately becomes strange.

How much do I love her?

Seven?

Ninety-three?

Four and a half kilograms?

Perhaps we could establish a baseline and report year-over-year growth.

For me, the example is my wife. Substitute your husband, partner, or whoever occupies that place in your own life. The measurement problem is the same.

We know what it means to love someone.

Or at least we know enough that the sentence makes sense.

We recognize love when we encounter it.

We feel its presence.

We certainly feel its absence.

Love may be one of the most consequential realities in a human life.

And yet the moment someone asks us to express it as a quantity, the question begins to fall apart.

That doesn't mean love leaves no evidence.

Quite the opposite.

If I say I love my wife but consistently ignore her, lie to her, humiliate her, betray her trust, and refuse to make room for her needs, she would be justified in questioning my definition of love.

Love does things.

It has consequences.

It produces behavior.

It shapes choices.

You can see it.

Sometimes you can even count things associated with it.

How much time do we spend together?

How often do we talk?

How frequently do we express affection?

How many date nights do we have?

How much money do we spend on each other?

How many household tasks do I perform without needing a parade afterward?

These things might tell us something.

Some could even be useful.

If my wife and I realize we haven't spent meaningful time together in months, putting a regular date night on the calendar might be a very good idea.

Measurement can help relationships too.

The trouble begins when we confuse evidence of love with love itself.

Suppose I discover that couples who report high relationship satisfaction spend an average of seven hours a week in meaningful conversation.

Excellent.

I shall talk to my wife for seven hours.

Problem solved.

At six hours and fifty-eight minutes on Sunday evening, I glance at the clock.

**Two more minutes. Please continue telling me about your day. Our marriage is almost healthy.**

We recognize the absurdity because relationship changes the meaning of the behavior.

Seven hours of attentive conversation might express love.

Seven hours of checking a box might be unbearable.

The measurable action can remain identical while the human reality changes completely.

Intention matters.

Attention matters.

History matters.

Trust matters.

Context matters.

Meaning matters.

This is one reason relationships are such a powerful example of the limits of reduction.

A marriage can be described in facts.

Years together.

Children.

Income.

Arguments.

Vacations.

Messages sent.

Time spent in the same room.

We could collect thousands of data points.

With enough data, we might even predict meaningful outcomes.

Perhaps we could identify patterns associated with satisfaction, conflict, separation, or resilience.

That would be useful knowledge.

But imagine receiving a report containing every measurable fact about your relationship.

At the bottom it says:

**Complete Description of Your Love.**

You would know immediately that the title was wrong.

Not necessarily because any of the data was inaccurate.

Because the claim was too large.

The measurements describe dimensions of the relationship.

They do not contain the relationship.

A relationship is lived.

It exists through time.

It accumulates memory.

There are jokes nobody else understands.

Arguments nobody else remembers correctly.

Ordinary Tuesday nights.

Shared disappointments.

Decisions made together.

Meals.

Touch.

Silence.

Sacrifice.

Boredom.

Forgiveness.

The thousand tiny accommodations through which two lives slowly make room for one another.

Much of this is observable.

Some of it is measurable.

But the meaning exists in the whole.

This is what compression struggles with.

To create a metric, we have to leave things out.

That's not a criticism.

That's how metrics work.

But relationships often derive their meaning precisely from the context we would have to remove to make them cleanly measurable.

Consider a very simple act.

I bring my wife a cup of coffee.

We can count that.

**Coffee delivered: one.**

But what does it mean?

Maybe nothing.

Maybe I was already in the kitchen.

Maybe she asked me.

Maybe I'm apologizing.

Maybe she had a terrible night and I know she's exhausted.

Maybe it's something I've done every morning for twenty years.

Maybe she hates coffee and this is evidence that I have learned absolutely nothing.

The observable behavior doesn't contain its own meaning.

The relationship supplies it.

This is true beyond romantic relationships.

Friendship works this way.

Parenthood works this way.

Trust works this way.

Loyalty works this way.

Someone can sit beside you for an hour and do almost nothing.

Measured as activity, the hour is unimpressive.

Experienced in grief, it may be an act of enormous love.

Someone else can give you brilliant advice for an hour and leave you feeling completely alone.

Same duration.

Very different reality.

Meaning changes the measurement.

Or perhaps more precisely:

**Meaning is often found in the information the metric had to discard.**

Context.

History.

Intention.

Relationship.

The view from inside.

That's why the question **How much do you love someone?** feels so strange.

We understand what *more love* might mean.

I can become less selfish.

More attentive.

More faithful.

More patient.

More willing to sacrifice.

Love can deepen.

Relationships can grow.

But that growth does not necessarily resolve into a quantity.

Sometimes the most accurate answer is frustratingly human.

**I love her deeply.**

Scientifically useless.

Personally meaningful.

And perhaps that tells us something.

Not every true statement becomes more true when we attach a number to it.

Sometimes precision clarifies.

Sometimes precision distorts.

Sometimes the right response to a question is measurement.

And sometimes the right response is to recognize that the question is asking us to speak a different language.

Love has one.

So does beauty.

## Beauty Has Terrible KPIs

Beauty creates a similar problem.

I play music.

I also write books.

These are unfortunate hobbies for someone who likes objective feedback.

There are numbers everywhere.

A song has streams.

A concert has ticket sales.

A video has views.

A book has sales.

Reviews.

Ratings.

Rankings.

Subscribers.

Downloads.

Every creative act can now be surrounded by a small cloud of analytics telling us how it performed.

This can be useful.

If I publish a book and nobody buys it, that tells me something.

Maybe nobody knows it exists.

Maybe the title is confusing.

Maybe the cover isn't working.

Maybe the subject is too narrow.

Maybe the book simply isn't very good.

The number cannot tell me which explanation is correct, but zero sales is still information.

Once again, reality gets to disagree with me.

I cannot publish a book, sell four copies, and confidently announce that the market has confirmed my status as the defining literary voice of my generation.

Although technically four people may agree.

The problem comes when performance becomes our measurement of beauty or value.

A song with a billion streams is popular.

That is an objective statement.

A song with ten streams is not.

Also objective.

But:

**The song with a billion streams is one hundred million times more beautiful.**

Something has gone wrong.

Popularity is measurable.

Beauty is experienced.

The two can be related.

Beautiful things can become popular.

Great books can sell millions of copies.

Extraordinary music can reach enormous audiences.

But popularity also depends on marketing, distribution, timing, familiarity, social networks, algorithms, fashion, luck, and whether someone with twelve million followers happened to use the song behind a video of their dog.

The number tells us what happened in one dimension.

Then we add the meaning.

This becomes particularly dangerous for creative people because the numbers arrive so quickly.

Write something.

Publish it.

Refresh.

Refresh.

Refresh.

Apparently the human response to creativity can now be observed in real time.

For most of human history, an artist could finish something and experience at least a brief period of uncertainty about whether anyone liked it.

We have solved that problem.

Your phone will now inform you immediately.

Thirty-seven likes.

Is that good?

Depends.

How many did the last post get?

Forty-two.

Oh.

Number go down.

Art bad.

This is Goodhart's Law with a guitar.

Once the measurable response becomes the target, creative decisions begin bending toward it.

What gets clicks?

What gets shared?

What holds attention?

What does the algorithm reward?

What kind of title performs?

What kind of thumbnail works?

What topic will sell?

Again, these are not stupid questions.

If you're trying to reach people, you should probably care whether you're reaching them.

I want people to read what I write.

Musicians generally prefer that someone listen.

There is no special virtue in creating work nobody wants and then declaring the audience insufficiently sophisticated to appreciate it.

But something changes when the response metric becomes the reason for creating.

**What do I want to say?**

becomes:

**What will perform?**

That's the same shift I experienced playing music at church.

What will speak to the heart?

What will draw people?

The questions can coexist.

The trouble begins when one becomes incapable of losing.

This book will eventually have numbers attached to it.

Copies sold.

Reviews.

Amazon ranking.

Maybe revenue.

Perhaps some wonderfully specific dashboard will tell me that readers abandon the book at Chapter 6 and I should have put more explosions in the quantified-self section.

Those numbers will matter to me.

Of course they will.

I would rather people read the book than not read it.

I would rather it sell well than poorly.

I am not spiritually advanced enough to look at terrible sales and experience complete detachment.

But what exactly will sales tell me?

They will tell me how many copies sold.

They may tell me something about reach.

They may tell me something about marketing.

They may tell me something about whether the idea resonated with people strongly enough for them to buy it.

They will not tell me everything I most want to know.

Did someone recognize themselves in these pages?

Did a manager look differently at the person behind a bad number?

Did someone step onto a scale and, for once, refuse to turn the result into a judgment about their worth?

Did a team reconsider a metric that was quietly teaching people the wrong behavior?

Did someone ask:

**What's the point?**

at exactly the right moment?

Maybe five hundred people read the book and one person changes how they lead because of it.

How many units of success is that?

I don't know.

This is not an excuse to declare every unsuccessful creative project secretly profound.

Sometimes the audience is telling us something we need to hear.

Sometimes the book isn't working.

Sometimes the song isn't very good.

Sometimes the joke simply isn't funny.

Creative people can hide behind subjectivity just as easily as managers can hide behind dashboards.

**You just don't understand my art** is not always evidence of misunderstood genius.

Reality still gets a vote.

The challenge is allowing the measurable response to inform the work without letting it define the work.

That's difficult because numbers provide something art cannot provide for itself.

Certainty.

I can argue endlessly about whether a paragraph is beautiful.

I cannot argue with the fact that 1,247 copies sold.

So the temptation is to let the precise number settle the ambiguous question.

**Was it good?**

Well, how did it perform?

Now we've substituted a question we can answer for the question we actually asked.

This happens everywhere.

Which movie was better?

Check the box office.

Which musician matters more?

Check the streams.

Which author is more important?

Check the bestseller list.

Which idea is more valuable?

Check the engagement.

The numbers can tell us about popularity, reach, and commercial success.

Those are real things.

But beauty lives somewhere else.

Think about a piece of music that matters deeply to you.

Maybe millions of people love it.

Maybe almost nobody has heard it.

Would another million streams make it more beautiful?

Would losing half its audience make it less so?

The experience remains.

The song meets your memory.

Your history.

Your longing.

Your grief.

Something happens between the work and the person encountering it.

We can measure around that encounter.

We can study it.

We can ask people about it.

But eventually we arrive at the same boundary.

**What was it like?**

And the answer becomes a story.

It reminded me of someone.

It made me feel less alone.

It gave me words for something I couldn't say.

It made the world seem beautiful for three minutes.

Those are terrible KPIs.

They may also be the point.

Beauty does not become less real because it refuses to fit neatly inside the dashboard.

Sometimes the dashboard simply isn't where beauty lives.

And beauty isn't the only human experience that refuses to follow the shape our measurement systems would prefer.

Grief is even less cooperative.

## Grief Refuses the Schedule

Grief is terrible at project management.

It misses deadlines.

It ignores milestones.

It refuses to provide reliable estimates.

Just when you think the project is nearing completion, it reopens an issue you were fairly certain had been closed.

There is no burndown chart.

No predictable recovery curve.

No clean line from broken to healed.

I know something about this personally.

My wife and I lost a child.

I don't need to tell that story here. It belongs to us, and the details aren't necessary for the point I'm trying to make.

But I do want to tell you that it happened.

Because I don't want what follows to sound like an engineer standing safely outside grief, examining it as an interesting measurement problem.

I've lived inside it.

And one of the things grief taught me is that time behaves strangely around loss.

We know this, and yet we still have a powerful instinct to imagine difficult human experiences as progressions.

At first things are terrible.

Then, slowly, things get better.

The pain decreases.

Functioning returns.

Life moves forward.

Number go down.

This isn't entirely wrong.

Time often does matter.

People adapt.

Pain changes.

We learn how to carry things that once seemed impossible to carry.

A person six years after a loss may genuinely be in a different place than they were six days afterward.

But grief has an irritating habit of refusing to stay on the graph.

You can be doing well for months.

Then a song comes on.

Or you smell something familiar.

Or you walk into a room.

Or you reach a date on the calendar.

Or you see something funny and instinctively think, **I need to tell them about this.**

And for a moment, the distance collapses.

The grief is right there.

Did you regress?

Did healing fail?

Has your recovery metric suddenly gone red?

Probably not.

Something meaningful happened.

Love encountered absence.

That's not necessarily a malfunction.

This is where our desire to measure progress can quietly impose the wrong shape on an experience.

Some things really do progress toward completion.

A broken bone heals.

A course ends.

A project ships.

A debt gets paid off.

There is a before, a process, and an identifiable finish.

Human beings understandably borrow that shape for other things.

We want grief to work the same way.

Start here.

Complete the stages.

Do the work.

Reach acceptance.

Done.

Unfortunately, the heart did not review the project plan.

This doesn't mean there are no patterns in grief.

There are.

Psychologists can study them.

Clinicians can identify when grief is becoming debilitating or when someone needs additional support.

Researchers can examine how people adapt after loss.

We can measure sleep, functioning, depression, anxiety, social connection, physical health, and countless other things surrounding the experience.

Those measurements can be enormously useful.

Once again, the argument is not that measurement knows nothing.

It's that the experience is larger than the measurements.

A questionnaire might tell us that someone's distress has decreased significantly over twelve months.

That's meaningful.

But it cannot tell us what it feels like when they set the table and, for half a second, reach for one plate too many.

The first-person experience contains a world.

Memory.

Relationship.

Absence.

Love.

Time.

Identity.

A future that was expected and didn't happen.

All of that can arrive in a single moment.

How many grief units is that?

The question sounds almost offensive.

Not because grief is magical.

Because quantity is the wrong category for much of what we're trying to understand.

This matters beyond grief.

We do the same thing with healing.

Recovery.

Forgiveness.

Trauma.

Relationships.

Career setbacks.

Spiritual growth.

We imagine a line.

Then we watch ourselves.

Am I doing better?

Am I less angry?

Am I less afraid?

Have I moved on?

Why am I still thinking about this?

Why does this still hurt?

We become the measurer and the measuree again.

Only now the performance target is healing.

This can create a particularly cruel form of self-management.

Not only am I hurting.

I am apparently **behind schedule in not hurting anymore.**

Now the pain has acquired a performance review.

The original suffering remains, and we've added judgment about whether we're recovering correctly.

This is the same pattern we've seen with weight.

The number reports something.

Then I supply the verdict.

Except grief often doesn't even give us a number.

So we invent one.

**I should be over this by now.**

According to whom?

What was the baseline?

What was the target?

Who approved the schedule?

Sometimes the answer is simply that we have absorbed a culture that expects progress to look visible, directional, and continuous.

We are comfortable with improvement when it looks like a line going up and to the right.

Or, in the case of suffering, down and to the right.

Less pain.

Less sadness.

Fewer bad days.

More functioning.

More normal.

But perhaps healing sometimes means something different.

Maybe the pain doesn't simply shrink.

Maybe our life grows around it.

Maybe a memory that once produced only anguish eventually carries gratitude too.

Maybe we become capable of feeling joy without believing we've betrayed what we lost.

Maybe grief becomes woven into who we are.

Not gone.

Not defeated.

Integrated.

Try putting that on a dashboard.

This is one of the recurring limitations of compression.

To measure something, we usually need to decide which dimension matters.

Intensity.

Frequency.

Duration.

Behavior.

Functioning.

Each can tell us something useful.

But the meaning of grief may live somewhere else.

Two people can report the same intensity of sadness and be having profoundly different experiences.

One may feel trapped.

Another may feel that sadness is an expression of love.

Same number.

Different meaning.

And meaning changes how the experience belongs inside a life.

This may be why some of our deepest experiences are better told as stories than summarized as scores.

A score tells us where something sits on a dimension.

A story tells us what it means.

Stories preserve things metrics deliberately discard.

Sequence.

Context.

Relationship.

Intention.

Memory.

Change.

Contradiction.

The same person can be healing and hurting.

Grateful and devastated.

Moving forward and carrying the past.

A dashboard would like us to pick a status.

Human beings are annoyingly capable of being all of them before breakfast.

Grief exposes this because it refuses simplification so openly.

It reminds us that not every meaningful change is linear.

Not every recurrence is regression.

Not every pain is a problem to optimize away.

And not every important question can be answered by asking whether the number went up or down.

Sometimes the deeper question is:

**What does this mean?**

And meaning may be the place where measurement encounters its most important boundary.

## Meaning Is Not a Property You Can Weigh

Imagine two people sitting quietly in chairs.

Same posture.

Same room temperature.

Same amount of time.

Same measurable physical activity.

Almost none.

One is sitting in an airport waiting for a delayed flight.

The other is sitting beside the bed of a dying parent.

From a certain distance, the activities look remarkably similar.

Two human beings.

Sitting.

Waiting.

Now describe what is happening.

The first person is bored.

Maybe irritated.

They check the departure board.

Refresh the airline app.

Wonder whether they should get another coffee.

Calculate how much sleep they're going to lose.

The second person may be experiencing one of the most significant hours of their life.

Same sixty minutes.

Completely different reality.

What changed?

Context.

Relationship.

History.

Expectation.

Love.

Fear.

Memory.

Meaning.

None of these necessarily changes the observable action.

But they change almost everything about the experience.

This presents a problem for measurement because measurement usually requires us to remove context.

That's part of how it becomes useful.

If I want to compare two things, I need a common dimension.

Hours worked.

Bugs closed.

Calories consumed.

People attending.

Dollars donated.

Books sold.

Minutes sitting in a chair.

The metric says:

**For the purpose of this question, ignore everything except this.**

That's not a defect.

That's the operation.

Compression requires exclusion.

We decide what information matters for the question we're asking, discard much of the rest, and create something manageable.

That's how a map works.

That's how a dashboard works.

That's how a metric works.

But meaning often moves in the opposite direction.

Meaning comes from putting things back in.

Who is this person?

What happened before?

What are they hoping for?

What are they afraid of?

Who is sitting beside them?

What does this moment represent?

What promises were made?

What has been lost?

What might never happen now?

The more context we restore, the more the meaning becomes visible.

This may be one of the deepest tensions in the entire book.

**Metrics gain clarity by removing context.**

**Meaning often emerges by restoring it.**

Neither operation is wrong.

They simply move in opposite directions.

Suppose an engineer spends three weeks fixing one bug.

The dashboard sees:

**Bugs closed: 1.**

That's clean.

Now put the context back.

The bug was intermittent.

It appeared only under a rare combination of conditions.

It crossed several subsystems.

The engineer had to learn code they didn't write.

The logs were misleading.

The first three apparent causes were wrong.

The eventual fix prevented a class of failures that could have affected thousands of customers.

Same number.

Different meaning.

Or perhaps the opposite happened.

The bug was trivial.

The engineer ignored it for two and a half weeks and fixed it Friday afternoon after someone reminded them.

Again:

**Bugs closed: 1.**

The metric did its job.

It counted the bug.

It was never designed to tell the story.

We get into trouble when we expect the count to carry the meaning.

This is why context so often appears in conversations as an inconvenience.

The dashboard gives us a clean pattern.

Then someone who actually knows the situation says:

**Well, it's a little more complicated than that.**

Of course it is.

Reality has entered the meeting.

Context makes decisions harder.

It introduces ambiguity.

It slows us down.

It prevents easy comparison.

It turns a clean ranking into a conversation.

This can be frustrating when you're trying to manage an organization.

You cannot attach a 900-word personal essay to every cell in a spreadsheet.

At some point, you have to compress.

But we should remember what compression costs.

**Meaning is often found precisely in the information the metric had to discard.**

This is true far beyond work.

Consider money.

A hundred dollars is a hundred dollars.

Wonderful thing about currency.

It gives us a common unit.

But a hundred dollars can mean very different things.

For one person, it is an irrelevant expense.

For another, it is groceries.

For someone else, it is the first money they earned from something they created.

For another, it is money given by a grandparent who has since died.

Same amount.

Different meaning.

Or consider time.

One hour.

Perfectly measurable.

An hour in traffic.

An hour with an old friend.

An hour in a hospital waiting room.

An hour holding a newborn.

An hour doing work you hate.

An hour doing work you love.

Sixty minutes every time.

The clock is correct.

The clock is also spectacularly uninterested in what the hour means.

That's not the clock's job.

It's ours.

This is where the language of the hard problem of consciousness becomes relevant again.

There is what happens.

There is what we can observe about what happens.

And there is what it is like for the person to whom it happens.

Then human beings add another layer.

We interpret it.

We place the experience inside a story.

We ask what it means.

Two people can go through remarkably similar events and understand them differently.

A failure can become evidence that I am incapable.

Or it can become the moment I changed direction.

A loss can become only absence.

Or, over time, it can also become evidence of how deeply someone was loved.

A difficult season can become wasted time.

Or it can become part of the story of who I became.

The event matters.

The interpretation matters too.

Again, this doesn't mean every interpretation is equally true.

Human beings can create terrible meanings.

We can misunderstand what happened.

We can lie to ourselves.

We can turn coincidence into destiny.

We can make ourselves the hero of every story and everyone else the villain.

Meaning needs reality too.

But reality does not arrive already summarized.

We participate in understanding what our experiences mean.

This is why a life cannot be reconstructed from a ledger.

Imagine receiving a spreadsheet containing every measurable fact about a person.

Income by year.

Weight.

Addresses.

Job titles.

Medical results.

Steps.

Hours slept.

Books read.

Miles traveled.

Messages sent.

Performance ratings.

Bank balances.

Maybe, with enough technology, physiological data from every day they lived.

Would you have the person?

You would have an extraordinary record.

You might know things about them that even they had forgotten.

But you would still want to ask:

What did they love?

What frightened them?

What did they regret?

Who mattered to them?

What did they believe?

What did they sacrifice for?

What made them laugh?

What did they do when nobody was watching?

Which moments changed them?

What did they think their life was about?

Now we're getting closer.

Not because numbers have failed.

Because the question changed.

We are no longer asking:

**What happened?**

We are asking:

**What did it mean?**

And meaning is not another column we forgot to add to the spreadsheet.

It exists in the relationship between facts.

Between events.

Between people.

Between past and present.

Between experience and interpretation.

Meaning requires context.

Which means that sometimes, to understand reality more fully, we have to reverse the operation that gave us the metric in the first place.

We have to decompress.

Put the story back.

Put the relationship back.

Put the history back.

Put the person back.

The number may still be there.

It may still matter.

But now it sits inside something larger.

This brings us to a question that modern measurement culture doesn't always handle comfortably.

If numbers are extraordinarily good at answering some questions, but meaning requires something more, how do we know which way of knowing to trust?

Perhaps the answer begins by recognizing that different questions require different kinds of knowing.
