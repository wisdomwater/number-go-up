# The Things That Don’t Fit

## Measurement Creates Distance

Measurement requires a certain kind of distance. To measure something, we usually have to step back from it long enough to identify a feature, isolate it, and decide how to represent it. Weight becomes pounds, distance becomes miles, performance becomes a rating, and time becomes minutes on a clock.

That distance is useful. It helps us compare, notice patterns, test assumptions, and see things that are difficult to recognize from inside the experience itself. Much of science depends on exactly this move: observe carefully, reduce interference, and describe what is happening as clearly as possible.

But distance is still a posture toward reality. A song can be measured in tempo, frequency, volume, and duration. A sunset can be described in wavelengths, angles, atmospheric conditions, and scattering. A conversation can be timed, transcribed, and analyzed for word count.

None of those descriptions are false. They simply arise from a particular vantage point. Something changes when we move from standing outside an experience to participating in it.

The song is no longer only sound waves; it is something we are hearing. The sunset is no longer only light moving through an atmosphere; it is something we are watching while the day disappears. Measurement gives us one kind of clarity by creating separation, but some other kinds of knowing begin when the separation closes.

## Knowing About and Knowing

The opposite of distance is not ignorance. It is presence.

There is a kind of knowledge that comes from standing back and collecting facts, and another that comes from being close enough to participate. I can know a great deal about a person without really knowing them. The distinction becomes obvious in relationships.

In principle, someone could build an astonishingly detailed record of my wife. Her preferences, habits, favorite foods, routines, calendar, photographs, messages, and a thousand other data points could all be collected and organized. Given enough information, the system might even become fairly good at predicting what she will do next.

It still would not contain what it means to share a life with her.

That kind of knowing lives in years of ordinary moments. It is in private jokes, disagreements, reconciliations, familiar silences, repeated stories, grief carried together, and the subtle changes in tone that only someone close enough learns to notice. Much of it would be difficult to explain because the knowledge was formed through participation rather than observation.

Facts still matter. Relationships are full of them, and forgetting your spouse's birthday in the name of mystical presence is unlikely to impress anyone. But information about a person and intimacy with a person are not interchangeable.

Presence changes the knower as well as the thing being known. To know someone relationally is to be affected by them, to risk misunderstanding, to revise assumptions, to forgive and be forgiven. The observer is no longer safely outside the system.

At a certain point, another row in the database does not bring us closer.

We have to show up.

## Explanation and Encounter

Consider a sunset. I could spend the afternoon with textbooks learning about visible light, atmospheric scattering, refraction, and the geometry that places the sun at a particular angle beneath the horizon. I could look up the exact sunset time from astronomical data and know, to the minute, when the event should occur.

Then I could close the book and walk outside.

The sky begins to change. Light stretches across the clouds, colors deepen, shadows lengthen, and for a few minutes I simply stand there watching the day disappear. Have I learned anything that was not already in the textbook?

I think I have, though it is a different kind of learning. I may not leave with another fact that can be entered into a database, but something has happened between me and the world. The sunset has become something I encountered rather than merely something I understood.

That kind of knowing often expresses itself strangely. It inspires poetry rather than a technical report. It makes someone reach for the person standing beside them, grow quiet for a moment, or feel an unexpected gratitude for having been there to see it.

None of this competes with the physics. Knowing why the colors appear does not make them less beautiful, and finding them beautiful does not require pretending that atmospheric optics is unknowable. Explanation and encounter can occupy the same moment without needing to defeat one another.

Perhaps the mistake is assuming that understanding something means we have exhausted it. The textbook can tell me an enormous amount about the sunset.

Then I step outside, and the sunset tells me something else.

## When the Dashboard Comes Along for the Hike

A hike can become a remarkably efficient data source. The watch tracks distance, pace, elevation gain, heart rate, calories, splits, and perhaps whether I am spending enough time in the proper training zone. Before long, the mountain has become a spreadsheet with trees.

There is something satisfying about that. I can glance at my wrist and know exactly how far I have gone, whether I am ahead of pace, and how much climbing remains. The information can be genuinely useful, especially if I am training or trying to understand my fitness.

But attention is finite. If I keep checking the watch, adjusting pace, watching heart rate, and evaluating whether the hike is producing the right training effect, I can spend an hour moving through a beautiful place without seeing much of it. My body is on the trail while my attention is somewhere inside a two-inch screen.

The irony is that the numbers may describe the experience accurately. The elevation gain is real. The pace is real. The heart rate is real. Yet the more engrossed I become in the representation, the easier it is to neglect what is being represented.

At some point I have to look up.

There is wind moving through the trees, a change in temperature as the trail enters shade, the smell of dry earth, and perhaps a sudden opening where the landscape stretches much farther than expected. None of those things require me to stop tracking the hike. They require me to remember that the tracking is not the hike.

The watch can tell me how I moved through the mountain.

It cannot look up for me.

## Attention Is Participation

What we pay attention to is, in a meaningful sense, what we participate in. The watch and the mountain are both present, but they are not equally present to me at the same time. Attention determines which part of the situation becomes vivid enough to shape the experience.

When I focus on pace, heart rate, distance, and elevation, I am participating primarily in the representation of the hike. The numbers are not false. They are abstractions drawn from something real, but they remain one step removed from the wind, the trail, the ache in my legs, and the view beyond the trees.

The same thing happens elsewhere. A photograph can preserve a moment while tempting me to experience the moment through the screen. A performance dashboard can tell me something important about a team while drawing attention away from the people whose work produced the chart.

Even a meal can become a collection of inputs if I stare at the calorie tracker long enough. Protein, carbohydrates, fiber, portion size, daily target. All useful information, but there is still food on the plate and perhaps another person sitting across from me.

The tools are not the problem. The question is where my attention has gone. What I attend to becomes textured, memorable, and alive, while what I ignore can recede even when it remains physically right in front of me.

The danger of measurement, then, is not only that the representation may be incomplete. We can become so absorbed in the representation that we stop participating in the reality it was meant to help us understand.

## What Cannot Be Abstracted Away

My wife and I lost a daughter. My oldest lost his little sister, and my youngest lost his twin. Anyone who has experienced the death of someone deeply loved knows how quickly language reaches its limits around that kind of loss.

At first, grief can feel sharp enough to occupy nearly everything. Over time, the edge changes. The pain becomes more like a dull ache carried somewhere in the background while routines return, work resumes, dinner still needs to be made, and life does the strange thing it always does by continuing.

Then something opens the door again. You are at a wedding and watch a father dance with his daughter, or a song catches you unprepared, or some ordinary detail suddenly belongs to another time. For a few moments the years between then and now seem much thinner than they did a minute before.

There is no useful dashboard for that. No chart can tell me that sadness is down twenty percent year over year or that this quarter's grief indicators are trending favorably. The very idea feels absurd because grief is not simply an output waiting to be optimized.

I can understand things about grief. I can read about how loss affects memory, the body, relationships, and attention, and that knowledge can be helpful. But when the ache rises again, knowing about grief does not place me outside it.

The only thing I have found that helps in those moments is presence. I have to let the sadness be sad, allow the memory to arrive, and stop trying to make the feeling move faster than it is ready to move. The release comes less from solving the grief than from no longer resisting its presence.

Some realities do not become easier merely because we can describe them well. They have to be encountered directly. Grief, when it returns, asks to be felt.

## The Person Behind the Description

Grief also taught me something about the limits of description. My daughter lived for ten days, so there is not a long archive of memories to draw from. We have the facts of her birth, the statistics recorded by the hospital, the monitoring and treatment, and photographs from the NICU.

All of that information is real, and I am grateful to have it. It tells part of the story. But none of it can capture what it felt like to hold my child.

That gap matters. A person can be described with remarkable precision and still remain more than the description. Height, weight, oxygen levels, heart rate, dates, diagnoses, photographs—each says something true, yet none contains the presence of the person those facts belonged to.

Perhaps that is one of the simplest ways to name the mystery at the center of human life. We are embodied and measurable, shaped by biology and history, yet encountering another person never feels like encountering a sufficiently detailed dataset. There is always someone there.

This is also why grief cannot be solved by information. We do not grieve missing data. We grieve a presence that cannot be reconstructed by assembling every fact we still possess.

The measurements remain.

What they measured was someone we loved.

## Returning to the Child

Maybe this is something children understand before they have language for it. They meet the world directly. A puddle is for jumping in, a hill is for climbing because it is there, and a piano key is pressed because someone wants to hear what happens.

The experience does not have to justify itself by leading somewhere else. The child is not yet asking whether puddle-jumping scales or whether the hill contributed adequately to quarterly cardiovascular targets. There is simply the strange and immediate discovery that the world is here and can be entered.

Adulthood teaches us other ways of seeing, and we need them. We learn cause and effect, planning, measurement, optimization, risk, and responsibility. We learn to ask what something will cost, what it will produce, and whether there is a better way to do it.

Those questions help us build lives, solve problems, care for people, and avoid doing unnecessarily foolish things. But they can become so habitual that we forget there was ever another mode of attention available to us.

Wonder asks us to recover that earlier posture without surrendering what adulthood taught us. It is not ignorance. It is the ability to understand something and still allow ourselves to encounter it before turning it into another problem to solve.

To look at the sunset without needing anything else from it. To hear the song without evaluating the performance. To hold someone we love without converting the moment into a lesson, an optimization, or a story about what comes next.

For most of this book, I have been defending measurement and then trying to place boundaries around it. I still believe in those tools. I have spent much of my life using them, and they have helped me understand things I could not have understood otherwise.

But there are moments when the instruments can be set down. No dashboard. No optimization. No sequence of inputs and outputs demanding another result. Just the strange privilege of being here while this is happening.

Perhaps maturity is not leaving the child behind.

Perhaps it is learning how to return with our eyes open.
