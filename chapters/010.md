# 10 — The Things That Don't Fit

Almost anything can be measured if we are determined enough. Music has frequency, tempo, amplitude, harmonic structure, and patterns that can be analyzed mathematically. A human body has heart rate, blood pressure, hormones, electrical activity, oxygen saturation, and thousands of other quantities medicine can observe. Even experiences like love, grief, beauty, and happiness leave traces that researchers can study.

This makes it tempting to divide the world into two categories: things that can be measured and mysterious things that cannot. I don't think that distinction holds up very well. Give human beings enough curiosity and sufficiently clever instruments, and we will find something measurable about almost any experience. The more interesting question is what kind of knowledge the measurement gives us.

I can measure the sound coming from a piano while someone plays a piece of music. I can examine what happens in the listener's brain, ask them to describe their emotional response, and perhaps discover patterns shared across many listeners. All of this could deepen our understanding of music and of ourselves. None of it requires me to pretend that I have therefore experienced the song.

That distinction matters because measurement usually asks us to stand at a certain distance from the thing we are studying. We observe, describe, compare, categorize, and look for patterns. These are extraordinarily powerful ways of knowing the world. They are not the only ways we know it.

Sometimes we know something because we participate in it.

## You Can Measure Almost Anything

Consider a sunset. There is nothing scientifically mysterious about the fact that light from the setting sun passes through more atmosphere, causing shorter wavelengths to scatter differently and producing the reds and oranges we see near the horizon. We can measure wavelengths, atmospheric conditions, particulate matter, cloud cover, and the precise position of the sun. A scientist could tell me far more about the physical event than I am capable of explaining.

Then I look up.

The explanation does not disappear. I do not need to choose between atmospheric physics and beauty, as though appreciating one requires me to become suspicious of the other. Knowing why the sky appears red does not make it less red. Explanation and encounter answer different questions.

This is where arguments about measurement can become unnecessarily dramatic. Saying that a sunset is beautiful is not proposing an alternative theory of atmospheric optics. Beauty does not compete with science for jurisdiction over light scattering. It describes something about the encounter between this world and a person capable of being moved by it.

The same thing happens with music. A particular chord progression may have a structure we can analyze, and our response to it may involve neurological processes that researchers can observe. Understanding those processes could be fascinating. Yet the analysis and the experience remain distinguishable because I can possess one without possessing the other.

Someone could know considerably more than I do about the acoustics of a piano while being unmoved by a particular piece. I could know almost nothing about the acoustics and find myself unexpectedly crying while listening to it. Neither experience disproves the other person's knowledge. They are encounters with the same reality from different positions.

This is not an argument for ignorance. I would rather live in a world where scientists continue asking why things happen, physicians understand bodies, engineers understand systems, and musicians understand their instruments. Wonder does not require protecting reality from explanation. If anything, deeper knowledge can give us more to wonder about.

The boundary appears when we assume that explanation has exhausted the thing explained. A description can become extraordinarily detailed without becoming the experience it describes. We can know more and more about the sunset while still needing to look at it. The measurements are true, and the sky remains larger than the spreadsheet.

## Knowing About and Knowing

There are things I know about my wife that could be entered into a database. I know her birthday, preferences, history, routines, the foods she enjoys, things that annoy her, and stories she has told enough times that I can occasionally provide supporting dialogue. With sufficient dedication, I could construct an impressive information system. It would still not be my marriage.

Knowing a person is different from possessing information about a person. Information matters because relationships depend partly on attention, memory, and understanding. Forgetting everything someone tells us is not usually considered evidence of profound relational wisdom. But intimacy includes something that cannot be replaced by accumulating facts.

I know my wife because I have shared a life with her. We have eaten ordinary dinners, made decisions, disagreed, laughed, worried, waited, changed, and watched years accumulate around us. Much of what I know is difficult to separate from the history through which I learned it. The knowledge is participatory.

We understand this distinction in simpler experiences. I can study coffee chemistry, learn about extraction, roast levels, water temperature, and the compounds responsible for flavor. None of that knowledge places the taste of coffee in my mouth. Eventually, if I want to know what this cup tastes like, I have to drink it.

A forest can be approached in the same two ways. I can learn its species, rainfall, geology, ecological history, and the processes that sustain it. Then I can walk beneath the trees, hear my footsteps on the trail, smell damp earth, and discover what it is like to be there on this particular morning. The scientific knowledge may deepen the walk without replacing it.

This is not a contest between objective and subjective knowledge. We need the distance that lets us examine things carefully, and we also need participation because we are not creatures standing outside the world. We are bodies inside ecosystems, people inside relationships, musicians inside music, and observers whose own lives are among the things we are trying to understand. Sometimes knowing requires distance, and sometimes knowing requires entering.

Mastery has trained us especially well in the first posture. Stand back, isolate the variables, reduce interference, observe what happens, and construct a model. That posture has given us extraordinary power. The danger is assuming that whatever cannot be known from that posture is somehow less real.

## Beauty Doesn't Need to Win an Argument

Beauty is troublesome because it is so easy to recognize and so difficult to make behave. I can tell you that a piece of music is beautiful, a mountain is beautiful, or the light coming through a window is beautiful, and you may understand immediately what I mean. Then you may look at the same thing and feel almost nothing. Beauty refuses to operate like a reliable output from standardized inputs.

This does not make beauty arbitrary or meaningless. People can learn to notice things they once overlooked, develop taste, study artistic traditions, and become more capable of perceiving what others have found beautiful. Culture and experience shape attention. Still, there remains something about beauty that arrives as encounter rather than conclusion.

The optimizer can have trouble leaving the encounter alone. A hike becomes exercise, exercise becomes steps, steps contribute to cardiovascular health, and cardiovascular health improves longevity. Reading becomes a yearly book count, travel becomes a list of countries visited, and playing piano becomes evidence that I am maintaining cognitive flexibility as I age. We are remarkably skilled at giving pleasure a business case.

None of those secondary benefits are imaginary. Hiking is exercise, reading can educate us, travel can broaden experience, and playing music may indeed be good for the brain. But if every experience must defend itself by producing some future benefit, we have quietly declared the present insufficient. Nothing is allowed merely to be worth doing.

A child rarely needs this justification. A child picks up a rock because the rock is interesting, asks a question because the question occurred to them, and runs down a hill because apparently gravity deserved further investigation. The world has not yet been fully organized according to usefulness. Encounter comes before optimization.

We cannot return to that innocence, nor should we want to. Adults know that hills have consequences for knees, rocks sometimes need to remain where they are, and somebody eventually has to make dinner. We have learned responsibility, causality, restraint, and all the other achievements of mastery. Wisdom does not require forgetting them.

But perhaps we can recover something the child knew before we became competent. We can look at something without immediately asking what it is for. We can listen to music without improving ourselves, walk without maximizing the walk, and sit with someone we love without turning the conversation into relationship maintenance. We can let beauty be enough reason to pay attention.

This is wonder after mastery. It is not ignorance of how the world works but a willingness to encounter the world even after we understand something about its workings. The adult knows the sunset has an explanation and looks anyway. Knowledge remains, but possession loosens its grip.

## Grief Refuses the Dashboard

There are places where this distinction becomes much less playful. My wife and I lost a child. I don't need to tell that story here because it belongs to us, and the details are not necessary for the point I am trying to make. But I do want to tell you that it happened.

I say this because I don't want what follows to sound like an engineer standing safely outside grief, examining it as an interesting measurement problem. I have lived inside it. I know something about grief because I have read about it and listened to others describe it, but I also know it because it entered my life. Those are not the same kind of knowledge.

Grief can certainly be measured. A physician can ask someone to rate pain, a therapist can use an assessment, and researchers can study sleep, stress hormones, depression, behavior, social functioning, and changes in the brain. These measurements can be enormously useful, especially when they help identify someone who needs care. Measurement can build bridges toward help.

But the measurement is not the grief. A pain score of eight may help a clinician decide what to do next, but it does not contain the loss that gave rise to the number. Two people can circle the same response on a questionnaire while inhabiting completely different histories. The instrument helps us see something without giving us the experience from inside.

This is where the engineering impulse encounters one of its hardest boundaries. When someone we love is suffering, we naturally want to do something. We look for the fix, the right words, the useful action, the intervention that will make the situation move in the desired direction. Love itself can make us desperate for an outcome.

Sometimes there are things to do. Meals need to be brought, appointments made, responsibilities covered, phone calls answered, and practical burdens carried. Competence can be an expression of love. There are moments when the most compassionate thing in the world is noticing what needs doing and quietly doing it.

Eventually, though, we may arrive at a place where there is nothing left to fix. The person is still grieving, the loss is still real, and no action available to us can restore what was lost. The optimizer experiences this as helplessness. Perhaps the better word is presence.

Can I stay when I cannot solve it? Can I sit beside someone without requiring their pain to improve so that I can feel useful? Can I resist turning their grief into a problem whose successful resolution would reassure me that I did the right thing? These are different forms of competence than the ones most of us were rewarded for developing at work.

The strange thing is that presence can matter enormously while accomplishing almost nothing that belongs on a dashboard. Someone stayed. Someone listened. Someone did not look away when there was nothing to say. A life contains moments when that is enough.

## The Mysterious Center

If we begin peeling away the measurable layers of a human life, there is always more to measure. We can measure the body, behavior, economic activity, relationships, language, attention, emotional responses, and patterns across years. New technologies will undoubtedly allow us to measure things we currently cannot imagine. I see no reason to place an arbitrary fence around scientific curiosity and declare that mystery begins precisely where today's instruments stop.

The mystery I am describing lies somewhere else. It appears in the fact that a person can be known in extraordinary detail without becoming reducible to the details. I could possess every measurable fact available about someone and still not have encountered them as a friend, spouse, child, colleague, or stranger sitting across the table. Information accumulates without turning into relationship.

The same pattern appears throughout the experiences we have been exploring. We can measure music without the measurements becoming music, study beauty without the analysis becoming beautiful, and examine grief without the data grieving. Each layer of knowledge may be real and valuable. Yet the thing itself remains something we participate in.

This is why I have become less interested in calling these things immeasurable. The word creates an unnecessary argument about whether science might someday measure some feature we currently cannot. I would rather say they are **not exhausted by measurement**. The distinction leaves room for every measurement we may ever discover without asking those measurements to become the reality they describe.

There is humility in this, but not the humility of ignorance. It is the humility of recognizing that knowing something does not necessarily mean possessing it. A person can understand a great deal about music and still be surprised by a song, understand relationships and still be surprised by a spouse, understand grief and still be overwhelmed by loss. Knowledge does not eliminate encounter.

This may be the mysterious center of the quantified life. Around the edges, almost everything can be counted, compared, analyzed, and improved. We can know our heart rate, net worth, performance rating, body weight, productivity, sleep score, steps, habits, and an ever-growing collection of facts about ourselves. Yet the person receiving all this information never quite appears as another number on the dashboard.

I am the one looking at the numbers.

That sounds almost embarrassingly obvious, but it changes the relationship. The dashboard can describe aspects of my life, but I remain the person deciding what they mean, what matters, what deserves action, what is enough, and what kind of life I am trying to live. Measurement can inform those judgments without making them for me. Meaning enters through a door the instrument cannot open by itself.

Perhaps mystery is not the absence of knowledge. Perhaps it is what remains when knowledge becomes deep enough to recognize that reality can be encountered without ever being completely possessed. The world is intelligible, and we should keep trying to understand it. But intelligibility does not require that everything eventually become small enough to fit inside our models.

We spent the first part of life learning how much could be accomplished by measuring the world accurately. We learned to stand back, analyze, predict, intervene, and make things happen. Those abilities gave us agency and made us competent. They brought us a very long way.

The next movement does not ask us to surrender any of that.

It asks whether we can carry everything we have learned and still allow the world to be wonderful.
