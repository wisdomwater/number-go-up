# Chapter 10

## The Things We Cannot Measure

### Eventually the Numbers Run Out of Language

Let's start with a song.

I play music, so this is familiar territory for me. A song is wonderfully measurable. It has a duration, tempo, key, frequencies, amplitudes, rhythms, and intervals. We can analyze its waveform with extraordinary precision, identify every note, and measure exactly when each sound begins and ends.

Once the song is released, we can measure even more. Streams. Sales. Skips. Repeat listens. How many people made it through the first thirty seconds. If we really wanted to get ambitious, we could put sensors on someone while they listened and measure heart rate, breathing, skin conductance, and brain activity.

Modern science and technology can tell us an astonishing amount about what happens when a human being listens to music.

Then I can ask a very simple question.

Was it beautiful?

Suddenly the numbers become less talkative.

They don't become useless. That's important. If millions of people listen to a song repeatedly, that tells us something. If listeners consistently report being moved by it, that tells us something too. If certain musical structures reliably produce particular responses, study them. Measure them. Learn everything we can.

But none of those measurements quite answers the question I asked.

Maybe you've had the experience of hearing a song you've heard a hundred times, except this time it catches you differently. Nothing about the recording has changed. Same notes, same singer, same waveform.

You have changed.

Maybe someone you love has died. Maybe you've fallen in love. Maybe you're driving home after receiving news you don't know how to process. Something that had always been background suddenly moves to the foreground, and for reasons you cannot completely explain, you're crying in the car.

What happened?

We can measure parts of it. Tears are measurable. Heart rate is measurable. The acoustic signal is measurable. With the right instruments, we can describe an extraordinary amount of what is happening in the brain and body while the song plays.

All of that is real.

But none of those measurements is the crying in the car.

This is the territory we've been approaching throughout this book. There is reality, and there are representations of reality. The representation can be extraordinarily precise. It can reveal things we would never discover through intuition alone. It can correct us, surprise us, and help us understand what happened.

But the representation and the experience are not identical.

This isn't an argument against science. Quite the opposite. One of the great achievements of science is learning how to isolate aspects of reality so we can examine them carefully. Measurement allows us to test our assumptions against something outside ourselves, and that ability has transformed human life.

The mistake comes afterward.

Because measurement is so powerful, we can begin to assume that whatever can be described most precisely must also be the most complete description of what is real.

A digital music file can reproduce a song in my headphones with astonishing fidelity. But nowhere in the file is the meaning that song has for me. There is no metadata field that says, *This was playing during the summer when everything changed.*

No tag for *this reminds me of my father*.

The song becomes meaningful when it encounters a human life. Memory enters. Relationship enters. History, expectation, loss, and hope enter.

The measurable thing hasn't stopped being measurable. Something else has joined it.

**Precision is not completeness.**

That's an easy distinction to forget because precision feels authoritative. A precise answer feels more solid than an ambiguous one. If one description gives me 73.4 and another gives me a story, the decimal point looks considerably more serious.

But precision and completeness answer different questions.

Eventually we reach questions for which another decimal place doesn't seem to move us much closer.

Was it beautiful?

Did it move you?

Do you trust her?

What did this mean to you?

Has this changed you?

We can gather evidence around those questions. Sometimes quite a lot of evidence. But eventually the numbers have told us what they know.

The question hasn't disappeared.

It has moved somewhere else.

### The View From Inside

There is a famous philosophical thought experiment about a scientist named Mary.

Mary knows everything there is to know about the science of color vision. She understands light, wavelengths, the eye, the nervous system, and what happens in the brain when light with a wavelength we call red reaches the retina.

There is only one problem.

Mary has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room.

She has never actually seen red.

Then one day she leaves the room and sees a red apple.

The question philosophers ask is whether Mary learned something new. Did she discover something she didn't know before—what red actually looks like?

This is known as the knowledge argument, and philosophers have been arguing about what it proves for decades. I am going to do something unusual for an author and resist the temptation to settle it.

You're welcome.

The larger debate involves enormous questions about consciousness and whether a complete physical description would contain everything there is to know about experience. Those questions are fascinating. They're also considerably above the pay grade of a book called *Number Go Up*.

I'm interested in something simpler.

There is an obvious difference between reading a description of an experience and having the experience.

You can study coffee. You can learn its chemical composition, understand how roasting changes flavor compounds, measure its temperature, and map what happens in the brain when someone drinks it. You can read tasting notes written by people who take coffee considerably more seriously than I do.

Then you take a sip.

The sip gives you access from the inside.

This first-person quality of experience is sometimes described using the philosophical term *qualia*: the redness of red, the bitterness of coffee, the sharpness of pain, the sound of a trumpet, the experience of hearing a familiar voice.

What is it like?

That question is strangely difficult to answer without eventually pointing toward another experience.

It's like...

Well, it's like this.

Except different.

We use one person's experience to build a bridge toward another person's. Sometimes language does this remarkably well. A good novelist can make us feel that we've entered a life very different from our own.

But there remains a difference between the description and the experience.

Measurement generally gives us a view from outside. We observe, record, count, compare, and represent. We turn some aspect of reality into information that can travel.

That's one of measurement's great powers.

I don't have to stand inside a furnace to know its temperature. In fact, I would strongly prefer not to. An instrument encounters the relevant property and returns a number. Now the information can travel. We can compare furnaces, set thresholds, build control systems, and make decisions.

Numbers travel remarkably well.

First-person experience does not.

Suppose I tell a doctor that my pain is a seven.

Seven is useful. It can go in a chart. It can be compared with the four I reported yesterday. It gives the doctor information that would otherwise be difficult to communicate.

But the doctor does not feel my seven.

The number carries information about my pain without transporting the pain itself.

And even the number depends on interpretation. Is my seven the same as your seven? What does seven mean to someone who has lived with pain for twenty years compared with someone encountering severe pain for the first time?

None of that makes the scale useless. It makes it a bridge.

**The metric creates a bridge. It does not eliminate the distance.**

We've seen this gap throughout the book.

A manager sees a performance rating. The employee experiences the year.

A church sees someone in a chair. The person in the chair experiences whatever is happening inside them.

The outside view isn't false. Neither is the inside view.

They have different access to the territory.

This becomes especially important at scale because large systems cannot operate entirely from the inside. An executive cannot personally experience the workday of fifty thousand employees. A hospital administrator cannot inhabit every patient's pain. At scale we need categories, summaries, representations, numbers.

We need maps.

The problem begins when the map acquires enough authority that the experience it represents becomes secondary.

The dashboard says you're doing fine.

But I'm not doing fine.

Who wins?

We shouldn't automatically answer, *the person*. Human beings are remarkably capable of misunderstanding ourselves. Maybe the dashboard is catching something I can't see. Maybe a survey is more representative than the three frustrated coworkers I happened to talk with. Maybe the medical numbers are reassuring for good reason.

But we shouldn't automatically answer, *the dashboard*, either.

The disagreement is information.

What does the number see that the experience doesn't?

What does the experience see that the number doesn't?

Wisdom may require holding both questions at once.

**There is a view from outside. And there is a view from inside. Both are real. But they are not the same view.**

Measurement gives us extraordinary access to reality from somewhere.

Its precision tempts us to mistake that somewhere for everywhere.

### The Instrument Is Not the Reality

Imagine I step onto my bathroom scale.

It says 247 pounds.

Then I ask whether I'm healthy.

247.

Apparently it didn't understand the question.

So I step off and back on.

Still 247.

This is not a design flaw. The scale is doing exactly what it was built to do.

It measures weight.

A thermometer measures temperature. A ruler measures distance. We wouldn't criticize a thermometer because it can't tell us the length of a table. We'd criticize the person trying to measure the table with a thermometer.

Every instrument has a range.

This seems obvious with physical instruments. It becomes less obvious when the instrument is measurement itself.

Measurement has succeeded across so many domains that it can begin to feel less like an instrument and more like the language reality is ultimately written in. If something is real, surely there must be some way to measure it.

Maybe.

But there's an important distinction hiding inside that statement.

We may be able to measure something **about** almost anything.

Love affects behavior. Grief affects the body. Music produces neurological responses. Trust influences decisions. Stress changes hormones. Relationships create observable patterns. Experiences we think of as deeply subjective leave footprints in the physical world.

Those footprints can be studied.

They should be.

The mistake is assuming that because we can measure something about a reality, we have therefore measured the reality itself.

Suppose I want to measure a forest.

Fine.

How many acres?

How much rainfall?

How much biomass?

What is the species diversity? Carbon storage? Soil composition? Canopy density?

All measurable.

Now tell me **the measurement of the forest**.

There isn't one.

There are measurements *of* the forest. Each reveals a dimension. Each answers a question.

None is the forest.

That's not a mystical claim about forests. It's simply how measurement works.

The question comes first.

What are you trying to know?

That determines the instrument.

This may be one of the simplest ways to protect ourselves from metric overreach. When a number starts carrying significant authority, ask what question the instrument was actually designed to answer.

My scale answers:

*How much do you weigh?*

It does not answer:

*Have you been good this week?*

I added that feature myself.

A performance rating might summarize how well someone performed against a particular set of expectations during a particular period. That's already difficult enough. It wasn't designed to determine whether the person is intelligent, whether their career has mattered, or whether they are worthy of respect.

Those are not additional columns somebody forgot to include.

They're different questions.

Precise answers become especially seductive when the questions we actually care about are ambiguous.

What makes a good life?

Difficult.

What's your net worth?

I can calculate that.

Am I healthy?

Complicated.

What do I weigh?

Step right up.

Am I doing meaningful work?

Could take years to answer.

How many tasks did I finish this week?

Finally, a checkbox.

The simpler questions aren't irrelevant. The danger is letting them inherit the authority of the harder ones.

The instrument knows what it knows.

The boundary has to come from us.

### Love, Beauty, and Grief

How do I know that I love my wife?

That's an odd question.

I could point to evidence. We've built a life together. There is history, commitment, affection, sacrifice, ordinary acts of care, difficult seasons we've walked through, and thousands of interactions no outside observer would find particularly interesting.

The evidence matters.

If I said I loved her while consistently treating her with contempt, the word *love* shouldn't be protected from reality simply because it's an interior feeling. Love has consequences. It enters the physical world.

But my knowledge of the relationship doesn't come only from observing those consequences.

I participate in it.

I know the relationship from inside.

An outside observer could collect enormous amounts of data about our marriage. They might discover patterns we don't see. In some cases, an outside perspective could reveal something important precisely because it isn't entangled in our experience.

But that person would not therefore know the marriage in the same way we know it.

Relationship is itself a form of access.

The same issue appears with beauty.

We can measure characteristics people tend to find beautiful. We can study symmetry, musical structure, preference, engagement, emotional response, sales, museum attendance, streams, likes, and repeat listens.

Useful information.

But popularity and beauty remain different questions.

**Popularity is measurable. Beauty is experienced.**

A song with a billion streams may be beautiful.

A song heard by thirty-seven people may be beautiful.

The stream count tells us something real about the song's reach. It doesn't settle the other question.

And then there is grief.

I know something about this personally.

My wife and I lost a child.

I don't need to tell that story here. It belongs to us, and the details aren't necessary for the point I'm trying to make.

But I do want to tell you that it happened.

Because I don't want what follows to sound like an engineer standing safely outside grief, examining it as an interesting measurement problem.

I've lived inside it.

Grief can be studied.

We can measure sleep. Appetite. Stress hormones. Depression scores. Ability to function. We can study bereavement across thousands of people and learn patterns that may help us care for someone who is suffering.

I'm grateful that we can.

But grief is also the experience of a world that has changed.

Someone who was here is no longer here.

Ordinary objects acquire meaning. Dates on the calendar change. A room changes. A song changes. Time itself can seem to behave differently.

The outside world continues at its normal pace, which occasionally feels like an administrative oversight.

Surely everything should stop for a while.

It doesn't.

People go to work. Groceries need to be bought. Emails arrive with a breathtaking lack of awareness that the structure of your universe has changed.

And grief does not proceed according to a clean schedule.

**Grief is terrible at project management.**

There is no reliable quarterly trend in which sadness declines 12 percent, acceptance rises 8 percent, and we achieve emotional closure by the end of the fiscal year.

There may be patterns. There may be stages or recurring experiences that help us understand what people often go through. But the map doesn't get to order the territory around.

This is where the distinction between the outside and inside view becomes more than philosophical.

A clinician may know things about grief that I don't.

I know what this grief is like.

Those forms of knowledge do not have to compete.

The clinician's knowledge can help me. My experience can tell the clinician something the general model doesn't contain. Neither has universal jurisdiction.

Perhaps this is one reason the most difficult moments of life often require more than information.

Sometimes we need someone who knows what to do.

Sometimes we need someone who knows how to sit beside us.

Those are different skills.

We should be grateful for both.

### Where Meaning Lives

Return to the song.

Same recording.

Same waveform.

Same notes.

Then something happens in your life, and the song changes.

Except, of course, it didn't.

You did.

Or perhaps more accurately, the relationship between you and the song changed.

That's where meaning becomes interesting.

Meaning is not simply another measurable property sitting inside the object waiting for a sufficiently advanced instrument to locate it. A song has acoustic properties. A book has words. A wedding ring has mass and chemical composition.

But what those things mean depends on relationships.

This song and this memory.

This ring and this marriage.

This room and what happened here.

This date and the person who is missing.

Context connects things that measurement often has to separate.

That's not a criticism of measurement. Isolation is one of its strengths. If we want to understand a phenomenon, we often try to control variables, distinguish one influence from another, and remove irrelevant information.

That creates clarity.

Meaning often moves in the other direction.

It reconnects.

The ring matters because of the marriage.

The photograph matters because of the person.

The song matters because of the memory.

The gesture matters because of the relationship and everything that came before it.

**Metrics gain clarity by removing context. Meaning often emerges by restoring it.**

This is why the same behavior can mean radically different things in different circumstances.

Silence can mean peace.

Or contempt.

Or fear.

Or reverence.

Or confusion.

The duration of the silence doesn't settle the question.

You need the story.

A score tells us where something sits on a dimension. A story can tell us what happened around it.

Neither replaces the other.

If I'm making a medical decision, I do not want my doctor to discard the lab results and ask what my cholesterol means to me personally.

Some questions really do need numbers.

But if we're trying to understand why a person made a particular decision, why a relationship matters, why a piece of music moved someone, or why a loss changed the shape of a life, another decimal place may contribute very little.

We need context.

Relationship.

Experience.

Story.

Judgment.

These are not consolation prizes we use when the serious tools fail.

They are ways human beings encounter aspects of reality that don't travel particularly well as numbers.

This is also why meaning is so vulnerable to compression.

The details that look irrelevant to a measurement may be exactly the details that make an event significant.

**Meaning is often found in the information the metric had to discard.**

That doesn't mean we should never compress.

We couldn't function if every representation had to contain every detail.

It means we should remember what compression costs.

And when the question we're asking depends heavily on what was removed, perhaps it's time to change instruments.

### Reality Is Always Richer Than Its Representation

Different questions require different ways of knowing.

If I want to know my blood glucose, intuition is a terrible instrument.

Measure it.

If I want to know whether a large organization is financially healthy, a handful of employee stories will not tell me enough.

Look at the numbers.

If I want to understand how employees across fifty thousand people are experiencing their work, I need data. I cannot build an accurate picture by asking the three people I happen to eat lunch with.

But if I want to understand why one particular employee's year went badly, eventually I may need to sit down and talk with them.

If I want to understand grief across a population, study it.

If I want to understand what this grief means to this person, listen.

If I want to understand the acoustic structure of a piece of music, analyze it.

If I want to know what it is like to hear the song, at some point I have to listen.

There is no universal instrument.

That's uncomfortable because universal instruments would make life much easier. We could put everything important into the same system, compare it, rank it, and optimize accordingly.

Reality has declined to cooperate.

Instead, we have different forms of access.

Measurement.

Observation.

Experience.

Relationship.

Story.

Judgment.

They correct one another.

The outside view can expose self-deception. The inside view can expose what the abstraction erased. Data can reveal patterns that no individual person can see. Stories can restore context that disappeared when thousands of lives became an average.

None gets universal jurisdiction.

This is the larger version of something we encountered while talking about health:

**Rigor without intuition becomes tyranny. Intuition without rigor can become self-deception.**

Rigor asks reality to challenge our stories.

Experience reminds us that the representation is still a representation.

We need both.

Perhaps that's why the map-and-territory metaphor has survived the entire book. A good map is an extraordinary thing. It can show us relationships we couldn't see from the ground. It can help us navigate enormous distances. It can reveal patterns hidden by our limited perspective.

The fact that the map is incomplete is not an insult to the map.

It's what makes the map useful.

If the map contained every tree, stone, smell, sound, conversation, memory, history, and human experience occurring within the territory, it would no longer be much of a map.

Compression is the point.

The trouble begins when we forget that compression occurred.

So return one last time to the song.

We can measure the waveform.

We can measure the brain.

We can measure the tears.

We can ask the listener to rate the experience.

All of it tells us something.

Then the person says:

"That was my daughter's favorite song."

And suddenly we understand something we didn't understand before.

Not everything.

Something.

The waveform didn't become false when we heard the story. The brain scan didn't become irrelevant. The tears didn't stop being measurable.

Context entered.

The territory became a little more visible.

There will always be another perspective. Another relationship. Another piece of history. Another part of the experience that didn't fit inside the representation.

That doesn't mean we stop measuring.

It means we stop asking measurement to be the whole of knowing.

The map can be accurate.

The map can be useful.

The map can even be beautiful.

But the map remains a map.

**Reality is always richer than its representation.**
