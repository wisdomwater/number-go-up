# 10 — The Things That Don't Fit

Almost anything can be measured if we are sufficiently determined. Music can be described through frequency, amplitude, tempo, and duration. A sunset can be analyzed through wavelengths of light, atmospheric particles, humidity, and the position of the sun relative to the horizon. Even experiences such as grief, love, loneliness, and happiness can be approached through surveys, physiological measurements, patterns of behavior, and changes in the brain.

That matters because it prevents us from making the argument too easy. There is no clean boundary with measurable things on one side and mysterious things on the other. Human beings are remarkably inventive measurers, and instruments continue to give us access to dimensions of reality our unaided senses could never perceive. The history of measurement is partly the history of discovering that things we once experienced vaguely can be understood with extraordinary precision.

The more interesting question is not whether something *can* be measured. It is **what kind of knowledge the measurement gives us**. A measurement requires us to select a feature of reality, establish some way of observing it, and represent what we find in a form that can be compared. That act creates knowledge, but it also creates a particular relationship between the observer and the thing being observed.

We step back.

We look.

We describe.

That distance is one of measurement's great strengths. It allows us to notice things that immediate experience can hide, compare one moment with another, test our impressions, and discover patterns that intuition alone might miss. Much of the mastery we have celebrated throughout this book depends upon our ability to stand outside something long enough to understand how it behaves.

But there are other things we know by stepping closer.

### Knowing About and Knowing

I can know an extraordinary number of facts about another person without knowing that person particularly well. I might know where she was born, what she studied, where she works, what music she likes, how much she earns, and what she orders when we go to dinner. With enough information I could construct an impressive profile, perhaps even one capable of predicting some of her choices.

None of that is the same as being married to her.

Marriage contains facts, of course. After enough years together, spouses accumulate an enormous database about one another, including information neither party remembers deliberately collecting. I know expressions, silences, stories, habits, vulnerabilities, recurring jokes, and the subtle difference between several varieties of “I'm fine.”

But even that list misses something. I know my wife not only because I possess information about her but because we have shared a life. The knowing exists partly in the history between us: conversations, arguments, ordinary evenings, losses, celebrations, mistakes, forgiveness, and thousands of moments that would look insignificant to anyone standing outside them.

This is not a less serious form of knowledge because it cannot be placed neatly in a database. In some situations it is the knowledge that matters most. A stranger could possess more demographic information about my wife than I do and still be the last person I would ask what she needs when she is hurting.

We use the same word—*know*—for both kinds of understanding, which can hide how different they are. I can know **about** something by observing, analyzing, and describing it. I can also know something through participation, relationship, and experience.

Neither form makes the other unnecessary.

But they are not interchangeable.

### Beauty Doesn't Need to Win an Argument

A sunset provides an easier example because it is less personal. Suppose I am standing outside as the sun drops toward the horizon and the sky turns orange, red, and purple. There are physical reasons for what I am seeing, and those reasons are not diminished because I find the result beautiful.

Someone who understands atmospheric science could tell me far more about the event than I know. They could explain how sunlight interacts with the atmosphere, why shorter wavelengths are scattered differently from longer ones, and how particles or clouds alter what reaches my eyes. If I misunderstood the physics, their explanation could correct me.

Then I look up.

Nothing about the explanation prevents me from experiencing the sky as beautiful. I do not need to choose between understanding the scattering of light and standing quietly for a moment because the world has become unexpectedly lovely. The scientific explanation tells me something about how the event occurs; the experience of beauty tells me something about what it is like for me to encounter it.

Trouble begins when we force one kind of knowing to compete in the other's territory. Beauty does not need to become an alternative theory of atmospheric optics in order to be real, and physics does not become spiritually impoverished because its equations do not contain the word *beautiful*. They are answering different questions about the same sunset.

This matters because people sometimes defend mystery by attacking explanation. We imagine that wonder survives only in the places science has not yet illuminated, as though every discovery removes another square mile from the territory of awe. That leaves wonder in a precarious position, forever retreating before better instruments.

I don't think mystery needs that protection.

The sunset can become more understandable without becoming less astonishing.

### The Optimizer Goes for a Walk

The tension becomes more subtle when measurement is not trying to explain an experience but improve it. Suppose I go hiking because I enjoy being outdoors. There is nothing wrong with noticing how far I walked, how much elevation I gained, or how hard my heart was working; that information might help me train, choose a route, or understand my health.

Then the optimizer in me discovers the hike.

Now there are opportunities everywhere. I can improve my pace, increase the distance, compare heart-rate zones, track elevation, maintain a streak, and perhaps choose future hikes partly according to how efficiently they advance whatever goal I have established. A walk through the mountains has become surprisingly well managed.

Again, nothing terrible has happened. The data may motivate me to get outside more often, and becoming fitter could allow me to experience places I could not otherwise reach. Mastery can expand experience rather than diminish it.

The question is what happens to my attention while I am there.

If I spend the entire hike evaluating my pace, checking my watch, thinking about the remaining distance, and comparing today's performance with last week's, I may know more about the hike while experiencing less of it. The trail has become an input into a system whose output is improvement.

This is the same tendency we saw in Chapter 5, but here I am interested in something slightly different. The problem is not that the number has become a manager. It is that **observation can occupy the attention that participation requires**.

Sometimes the appropriate response to the mountain is not another data point.

It is to be on the mountain.

### Grief Refuses the Dashboard

There are experiences where this distinction becomes much more serious.

My wife and I lost a child. I don't want to turn that experience into an illustration, and there are parts of it that belong to us rather than to this book. But loss taught me something about the difference between understanding an experience and living inside it.

Grief has measurable dimensions. A doctor or therapist may ask about sleep, appetite, concentration, anxiety, depression, or whether someone is able to function in daily life. Those questions can be enormously important because grief happens in bodies and minds, and sometimes a person needs help that careful observation can identify.

A pain scale can also be useful after surgery even though the number cannot contain pain. Saying “seven” gives another person information they can act upon. The fact that the number is incomplete does not make it trivial; sometimes an incomplete representation is exactly what allows one human being to help another.

But grief itself does not feel like a seven.

It changes the texture of ordinary life. Familiar places become connected to memories, time behaves strangely, and things that once seemed important can temporarily lose their weight while something as ordinary as a song or an empty room becomes almost impossible to bear. The experience is not hidden behind the measurable symptoms, waiting to be reduced correctly if only we had better instruments.

It is the life being lived through them.

This becomes especially clear when there is nothing left to fix. Practical help matters enormously when practical help is possible: bring food, make the call, handle the paperwork, sit with the children, take something off someone's plate. Competence can be an expression of love.

Eventually, though, you may find yourself sitting beside someone in pain with no remaining action that will make the thing unhappen.

For a person accustomed to solving problems, this can feel almost unbearable. The mind keeps searching for something useful to do because usefulness has been one of our most reliable ways of caring. When no solution exists, we can begin to feel that we have nothing to offer.

But sometimes staying is the offering.

Someone sits with us, listens, brings no explanation, and does not look away.

Nothing has been fixed.

Something has still been given.

### The Mysterious Center

This is where I begin to understand mystery differently. Mystery is not simply the territory beyond the current reach of our instruments, the dark corner where explanation has not yet arrived. If that were all mystery meant, better measurement would steadily eliminate it.

The mystery I mean can remain even when the facts are known.

A person can be known in extraordinary detail without being exhausted by those details. We can know someone's genetics, history, habits, preferences, personality traits, medical records, financial circumstances, and thousands of recorded behaviors. Each fact may be true, and together they may tell us a great deal.

Yet when that person walks into the room, something occurs that the profile does not replace.

There is someone there.

This may be the deepest limitation of the dashboard, and it has nothing to do with whether the dashboard is accurate. Every number requires someone for whom the number means something. My weight can be measured precisely, my performance can be evaluated, my finances can be counted, and my behavior can be tracked across years.

But I am the one encountering those measurements.

The person looking at the dashboard never appears on the dashboard in quite the same way.

We can add more measurements, of course. Measure my mood, personality, attention, brain activity, preferences, social relationships, and whatever else becomes technically available. Those measurements may teach us remarkable things about human beings, and I hope we keep learning.

Still, the act of knowing always involves more than information. Someone is looking, interpreting, caring, deciding, suffering, loving, or wondering. Meaning enters because a life is being lived from the inside.

Perhaps that is the mysterious center we have been circling throughout this book.

Not a magical substance hidden beyond measurement.

Not an argument against science.

Not proof that numbers have failed.

Simply the recognition that reality can be **known without being exhausted by our knowledge of it**.

### Returning to the World

The child we met near the beginning of this book did not need to think about any of this. A puddle was something to jump in, a hill was something to climb, and a piano could be touched simply to discover what sound came out. Encounter came first because the child had not yet learned enough mastery to stand very far outside the world.

Growing up required us to learn that distance. We learned to examine things, understand causes, anticipate consequences, and act deliberately. We discovered that some puddles are deeper than they look, some hills require preparation, and making music with other people goes considerably better if everyone agrees on what song they are playing.

I do not want to surrender any of that knowledge.

The question is whether maturity might allow us to return to encounter without pretending we never learned mastery.

I can understand something about the physics of a sunset and still look up. I can track my health and still experience my body as more than a health project. I can practice the piano carefully and then become absorbed in the music rather than my own technique.

And when someone I love is hurting, I can use every practical skill available to help them.

Then, when there is nothing left to solve, I can remain.

Perhaps wonder begins there—not in ignorance, but in the realization that understanding something does not require possessing all of it. The world can be studied without becoming merely an object of study, and a life can be examined without becoming merely a project.

We have spent years learning how to stand back far enough to see clearly.

Maybe wisdom also knows when to step closer.
