## Eventually the Numbers Run Out of Language

Let's start with a song.

I play music, so this is familiar territory for me.

A song is wonderfully measurable.

It has a duration.

A tempo.

A key.

Frequencies.

Amplitudes.

Rhythms.

Intervals.

We can analyze the waveform.

We can identify every note.

We can measure exactly when each note begins and ends.

We can examine the harmonics produced by the instruments and the acoustic properties of the room in which they were recorded.

If the song is released commercially, we can measure even more.

Streams.

Downloads.

Sales.

Chart position.

Radio plays.

Likes.

Shares.

Skips.

How many people listened to the first thirty seconds.

How many finished the song.

How many came back and listened again.

If we really wanted to get ambitious, we could put sensors on someone while they listened.

Heart rate.

Breathing.

Skin conductance.

Brain activity.

We could ask them questions afterward and turn their answers into data.

Modern science and technology can tell us an astonishing amount about what happens when a human being listens to music.

And then I can ask a very simple question.

**Was it beautiful?**

Suddenly the numbers become less talkative.

They don't become useless.

That's an important distinction.

Maybe millions of people listened to the song repeatedly. That tells us something.

Maybe listeners consistently report being moved by it.

That tells us something too.

Maybe certain musical structures reliably produce particular emotional responses.

Interesting.

Study them.

Measure them.

Learn everything we can.

But none of those measurements quite answers the question I asked.

Was it beautiful?

Or an even more personal question:

**What did it feel like to hear it?**

Maybe you've had this experience.

A song you've heard a hundred times comes on at exactly the right moment.

Nothing about the recording has changed.

Same notes.

Same tempo.

Same singer.

Same waveform.

But something about you has changed.

Maybe someone you love has died.

Maybe you've fallen in love.

Maybe you're driving home after receiving news you don't know how to process.

Maybe a lyric that had always been background suddenly seems to be speaking directly to you.

And for reasons you cannot fully explain, you're crying in the car.

What happened?

We can measure parts of it.

Tears are measurable.

Heart rate is measurable.

The acoustic signal is measurable.

If we knew enough about the brain, perhaps we could describe an extraordinary amount of neural activity taking place while the song played.

All of that would be real.

None of it would be the crying in the car.

This is the territory we've been approaching throughout this book.

There is reality.

And there are representations of reality.

The representation can be extraordinarily precise.

It can reveal things we could never discover through intuition alone.

It can correct us.

It can surprise us.

It can help us understand what happened.

But the representation and the experience are not identical.

This isn't an argument against science.

Quite the opposite.

One of the great achievements of science is learning how to isolate aspects of reality so we can examine them carefully.

We measure.

Compare.

Test.

Repeat.

We strip away ambiguity wherever we can.

And it works spectacularly well.

The mistake comes afterward.

Because measurement is so powerful, we can begin to assume that whatever is most precisely measurable must also be the most complete description of what is real.

We've encountered this assumption everywhere.

A performance rating is precise.

A career is not.

Weight is precise.

Health is not.

Attendance is precise.

Transformation is not.

Revenue is precise.

Organizational value is not.

The measurable thing feels solid.

The larger reality feels fuzzy.

So we give the solid thing more authority.

But perhaps we've confused two different qualities.

**Precision is not completeness.**

A song can be represented digitally with extraordinary precision.

That representation is good enough to reproduce the sound in my headphones.

But nowhere in the file is the meaning the song has for me.

There isn't a field containing:

**This was playing during the summer when everything changed.**

There is no metadata tag for:

**This reminds me of my father.**

No bit rate for longing.

No frequency for grief.

No amplitude for beauty.

Those things appear when the music encounters a human life.

And that human life brings context.

Memory.

Relationship.

History.

Expectation.

Loss.

Hope.

The song is measurable.

The encounter is something more.

This is why some of the most important questions we ask sound strangely resistant to numbers.

Was it beautiful?

Did it move you?

Do you trust her?

Have you forgiven him?

What does this mean to you?

Are you happy?

Do you love them?

Was the suffering worth it?

Has this changed you?

We can gather evidence around every one of those questions.

Sometimes quite a lot of evidence.

But eventually we reach a point where another decimal place doesn't get us any closer to the thing we're trying to understand.

The numbers have told us what they know.

Now the question has moved somewhere else.

This does not mean we've left reality behind.

It may mean we've encountered a part of reality that has to be approached differently.

There is an old philosophical problem hiding here.

What is the difference between describing an experience from the outside and actually having the experience from the inside?

What is it like to see red?

To taste coffee?

To feel pain?

To hear a song and suddenly find yourself crying?

Philosophers have spent a great deal of time arguing about these questions.

They even have a word for this first-person quality of experience.

**Qualia.**

I have no intention of solving the philosophy of consciousness in a book that began with corporate dashboards.

But qualia gives us language for something we've been bumping into from the beginning.

There is a view from outside.

And there is a view from inside.

Both are real.

But they are not the same view.

Eventually, the numbers run out of language.

The experience keeps going.
